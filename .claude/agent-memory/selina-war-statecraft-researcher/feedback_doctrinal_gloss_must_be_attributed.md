When two doctrinal categories sit close enough that a one-sentence gloss is tempting (e.g., Rome Statute Art 33(2) enumerates genocide + crimes against humanity; torture is gravely adjacent but NOT enumerated), the gloss must be **quoted-and-attributed** rather than asserted as the source's own text. Same pattern applies to close numerical findings (Shirley flagged the parallel in her Boeing work).

**Why:** Stephen caught two such misstatements across two waves — the Lvova-Belova "operationally directed" gloss and now the Abu Ghraib Art 33(2)/torture gloss. Both would have read as if the treaty text itself enumerated torture under Art 33(2); the treaty text does not. Asserting a defensible scholarly inference in the source's own voice is a rule-05 (overclaim) and rule-06 (quote-integrity) hinge — it launders the inference's evidentiary status. The book argues against responsibility laundering; the prose must not launder evidence about doctrine.

**How to apply:** When drafting any IHL / Rome Statute / command-responsibility passage:
1. Quote the treaty text verbatim for what it *does* enumerate.
2. State separately: "Although X is not enumerated in [Art], the gravity of X as [grave breach / war crime / etc.] under [primary source A] and [primary source B] supports the doctrinal view that..."
3. Cite the independent grounds (Geneva CA3, CAT, Rome Statute Art 8, IMT Charter Art 8, etc.) so the inference does not pretend to ride on the unrelated article's text.
