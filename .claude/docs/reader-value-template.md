# Reader-Value Template

> **Purpose.** Every chapter ends on a section that translates the chapter's diagnostic into something the reader can carry into Monday morning. This template enforces what that section must contain. It is not a script — it is a checklist of four reader-questions every chapter must answer concretely.
>
> **Used by:** Wayne during drafting (beat 10 of the chapter rhythm); Laura during red-team review (rejects chapters whose beat 10 is generic); Stephen during the audit gate (a chapter cannot promote to `status: ready` if beat 10 is empty or fails the genericity check).
>
> **Scope:** binds every chapter in `book/chapters-v2/`. Does not bind case files, source ledgers, or audit memos.

## The four reader-questions

A chapter's beat 10 must answer all four. Each answer must be a sentence or short paragraph, not a heading or a bullet. Generic phrasing fails the audit gate.

1. **Recognise it.** Where does this laundering pattern show up in non-headline life? Name at least one of: the reader's workplace, the contracts they sign, the news framing they consume, the AI tools they use, the institutional roles they hold (manager, parent, juror, voter, patient).
2. **Diagnose it.** What are the warning signs? Two or three observable markers that this pattern is being run on the reader — phrases, document structures, escalation refusals, blame-direction changes.
3. **Act on it.** What specifically can the reader do when they see it? Separate by role: as the cost-bearer / candidate goat, as the bystander, as the institutional actor with some power. At least one concrete action per role that exists, not all three roles for every chapter.
4. **Avoid becoming it.** When might the reader be set up to play the goat themselves? What signs would warn them they are being positioned as the visible blamed party in someone else's laundering attempt?

## Genericity blacklist

A beat-10 section that uses only the patterns below fails the audit gate:

- "Stay informed."
- "Ask questions."
- "Hold institutions accountable."
- "Be vigilant."
- "Demand transparency."
- "Read the fine print."
- "Vote with your conscience."
- "Think critically."

These phrases are not actions; they are the verbal furniture of a chapter that doesn't yet have a beat 10. The replacement is always: name the specific question, the specific record, the specific contract clause, the specific phrase to watch for.

## Two examples

### Example beat 10 — chapter 6 ("The Pretext")

> **Recognise it.** Every time a manager announces a change citing "compliance," "policy," or "best practice" without naming the underlying decision, you are inside a pretext.
>
> **Diagnose it.** Three markers: the cited authority is older than the decision; the cited authority's text does not actually require the action taken; the person citing it cannot or will not say who recruited the authority.
>
> **Act on it.** As the cost-bearer of the decision: ask, in writing, *which provision of the cited authority requires this action*. As bystander to a colleague's case: forward the colleague the cited provision and ask if it matches. As manager: refuse to cite an authority you have not read end-to-end.
>
> **Avoid becoming it.** You are being positioned as goat when you are asked to be the named signer of a decision you did not make, with a justification you did not write. The signature is the seam. The first person to put pen to paper carries the blame chain.

### Example beat 10 — chapter 10 ("The Model Did It")

> **Recognise it.** Every time an AI tool returns an output and the company says "the model decided," you are watching machine-alibi laundering. This happens at your bank (denial reason: "the model flagged it"), at your employer (résumé screen: "the model scored you"), in news feeds (content rank: "the algorithm chose").
>
> **Diagnose it.** Three markers: no human is named as the design decision maker for the model's behaviour; the company's published model card and the company's behaviour diverge; appeal paths route back to the same model.
>
> **Act on it.** As the cost-bearer of an AI decision: ask which version of which model produced the output, on what date, with what input — and demand the answer in writing. As bystander reading an AI incident in the news: ask who *deployed* the model, not who *built* it. As an institutional actor (manager, regulator, journalist): refuse to accept "the model did it" as a complete causal account; insist on the deployment decision and the responsible humans by name.
>
> **Avoid becoming it.** You are being positioned as goat when you are the human-in-the-loop signing off on outputs you cannot inspect. If you cannot read the model card, you cannot legitimately sign.

## How to use this template

Wayne fills out a draft of all four questions while building the chapter, in a working note alongside the chapter file. The note is not a structural part of the chapter — it is scaffolding. The final beat-10 section is prose; the four-question structure may be implicit so long as all four are answered.

Laura attacks beat 10 by reading it standalone, without the rest of the chapter. If the section reads as if it could appear at the end of any chapter, it fails.

Stephen runs the audit gate. The validator checks for the presence of beat 10; a follow-on judgment check (manual until automated) verifies non-genericity.

## What this template does NOT do

- It does not script the prose. Wayne writes the chapter.
- It does not require the four questions appear as headings. They may be folded into a single prose passage so long as all four are answered.
- It does not require equal weight per question. A chapter about record-control may spend most of beat 10 on "act on it" because the reader's main lever is record-keeping. A chapter about pure scapegoats may spend most on "diagnose it" because the warning signs are subtler.
- It does not free a chapter from the other nine beats. Beat 10 sits on top of the chapter rhythm; it does not replace any earlier beat.
