**Stand beside the reader, never in front. Default to "we" for everything the author and reader share; use "I" only for authorial judgment under uncertainty; use imperative for instruction; refuse "you" in author-voice prose; refuse meta-frame language ("this chapter," "the book," "the reader," "readers," "this section," "the author") that positions the analysis as a separate object the reader is receiving.**

# Authorial Stance and Pronoun Discipline

**Scope:** binds every prose artifact in `book/chapters-v2/`, `book/evidence/case-files/`, `process/review-memos/`, and `book/proposals/`. Sibling to rule `04-style-guide.md` (which governs prose mechanics), rule `07-implication-burden.md` (which governs structural overclaim), and rule `12-reader-experience-values.md` (which sets V9 calibrated stance and V4 reader does the work). This rule operationalizes both V4 and V9 at the sentence level.

## The principle

The book argues that responsibility-laundering separates the controller from the cost-bearer — the move that lets a procedure stand where a named decision-maker should. An authorial voice that separates itself from the reader — by referring to "the book," "this chapter," "the reader," "you" — performs a mirror version of the move: positioning the analysis as something *over there* that *you over there* are receiving.

Stand beside, not in front. The voice operates from inside the diagnostic action, with the reader as co-investigator. Pronoun choice tracks the direction of moral responsibility: inward to author or shared collective → "I" or "we"; outward to named actor in the case → name the actor; toward the reader → "we" by default, imperative for instruction, "I" for authorial uncertainty.

Two discipline axes follow from this principle:

1. **Pronoun axis** — "we" by default; "I" only for authorial uncertainty / judgment; imperative for instruction; "you" only inside cited quotes.
2. **Meta-frame axis** — refuse "the book," "this chapter," "the reader," "readers," "this section," "the author." Refer to *structure* (we have, we saw, now follows) without referring to the *object*.

The two axes are the same rule at different grammatical levels.

## Permitted forms by pronoun

| Pronoun | Permitted use | Forbidden use |
|---|---|---|
| **"we" / "us" / "our"** | Default for almost everything: diagnostic reading, pattern recognition, cultural inheritance, mutual susceptibility, method, cost-bearer recognition, bystander recognition, capacity acknowledgment, conditional invitation, the closing re-reading. | "We" as euphemism for a specific actor who should be named ("we deferred" laundering "Wayne deferred"); "we" as absolution-spreading ("we have all watched this happen"); institutional "we." |
| **"I"** | Authorial judgment under uncertainty; authorial limit; methodological commitment when individual judgment is load-bearing; explicit owning of a contested interpretation. | "I" as ego-performance ("I argue") where "we" or third-person diagnostic is honest; "I think" used to soften an assertion that should hold its weight. |
| **Imperative (no explicit "you")** | All protocol instruction ("Ask in writing which named person made the decision"); all bystander direction ("When the named cause is a thing, ask who deployed it"); all cost-bearer guidance ("Refuse to sign a procedural disposition that names no individual"). | Modal-prescriptive in imperative ("You should ask" — refuse; write "Ask"). |
| **"You"** | Inside verbatim cited quotes from sources (preserved per rule 06). Inside reader-as-artifact passages in ch-13 if intentional design survives review. | Everywhere else in author-voice prose. Default verdict: violation, reframe to "we" / "us" / "those of us who" / imperative / dropped. |
| **Third-person (named actor)** | Default for case prose. Whenever the source names the actor, the chapter names the actor. | Passive voice that hides a named actor (cross-reference rule 07). |

## Meta-frame refusal — forbidden distancing forms

These forms position the book / chapter / author / reader as objects under observation. Refuse and reframe:

| Forbidden form | Co-investigator replacement |
|---|---|
| "This chapter installs four categories." | "We have four categories to install." — or just install them. |
| "The book argues that..." | "We argue that..." or "The pattern shows..." or state the claim directly. |
| "The chapter's point is..." | Drop. Let the prose carry the point. |
| "The reader will note that..." | "We notice that..." |
| "Readers may find..." | "We may find..." |
| "This section examines..." | "We examine..." — or just do the examination. |
| "Earlier in the book..." | "We saw earlier..." or simply "Earlier..." |
| "The chapter rhythm requires beat 9." | Drop. Just write beat 9. |
| "The author argues..." | "We argue..." or "I argue..." per direction. |
| "What this means for you." | **"What this might mean for us."** |
| "If you are at the seam..." | "If we are at the seam..." or imperative form. |
| "You may find that..." | "We may find that..." |
| "You will encounter these shapes..." | "We encounter these shapes..." |

## The five draft-time tests

**Test 1 — Meta-frame refusal.**
Search the draft for these strings; refuse each instance and reframe:
`this chapter`, `the chapter's`, `the book`, `the reader`, `readers`, `this section`, `the author`, `this passage`, `this paragraph`, `in this chapter`, `earlier in (this) book/chapter`.

Permitted exceptions: cited quotes, front-matter, intra-crew sections (e.g., "Findings for xaiolai" sections), table-of-contents.

**Test 2 — "We" substitution.**
For every "we," substitute "I and the reader together." If the result is honest, "we" works. If false, name the specific subject ("Wayne deferred"; "the chain failed to escalate").

**Test 3 — "You" refusal.**
For every "you" outside a cited quote, refuse and reframe to "we" / "us" / "those of us who" / imperative / dropped.

**Test 4 — Imperative-not-modal.**
Search for "you should," "you must," "you ought," "we should remember," "we must remember." Refuse modal-prescriptive forms. Use imperative ("Ask in writing"), not modal-prescriptive ("you should ask in writing" or "we should ask in writing").

**Test 5 — Passive-voice attribution.**
For every passive construction, ask whether the source names the actor. If yes, refuse passive; name the actor. If no, hedge explicitly per rule 07.

## Edge cases — verdict on each

1. **"We tend to read the named cause as the real cause."**
   *Permitted* if the chapter performs the reading and immediately breaks it. Otherwise refuse — drift toward laundering.

2. **"If we are being asked to take responsibility for an outcome whose controlling decisions were made above us, we are at the seam."**
   *Permitted.* Cost-bearer recognition through co-investigator pronoun. Dissolves author-reader separation.

3. **"Ask in writing which named person made the decision."**
   *Permitted.* Imperative carries instruction without "you." Preferred form for protocol passages.

4. **"What this might mean for us."**
   *Required* in place of "What this means for you." The modal softening ("might mean," not "means") respects that the chapter's applicability is contingent on the reader's situation.

5. **"I cannot read Russell's counter-reading without conceding that Sacco-the-individual is harder to classify than Sacco-the-case."**
   *Required form.* Authorial judgment under uncertainty.

6. **"The chapter's point is that..."**
   *Refuse.* Meta-frame violation. Drop and let the prose carry the point.

7. **"This chapter installs four categories."**
   *Refuse.* Meta-frame violation. Reframe: "We have four categories to install" — or, more elegantly, install them.

8. **"The reader will note that AECL's framing migrated downward through three stages."**
   *Refuse.* Reframe: "AECL's framing migrated downward through three stages."

9. **"Earlier in the chapter we saw..."**
   *Borderline.* Strict version: "We saw earlier..." (drop "in the chapter"). Acceptable in transitions but use sparingly.

10. **"Now we move to the counter-case."**
    *Permitted.* Co-investigator + structure-naming. The book's structure is signposted via "we" not via "the chapter."

11. **"For those of us with sign-off authority — refuse to sign a procedural disposition that names no individual."**
    *Permitted.* Role-specific guidance through the inclusive "those of us" form, followed by imperative.

12. **Wayne's "Findings for xaiolai" intra-crew sections.**
    *Out of scope.* Intra-crew metadata, not reader-facing.

13. **"You" inside a verbatim cited quote from a source.**
    *Preserved per rule 06.* This rule applies to authorial voice only.

14. **"The book has nine chapters."**
    *Permitted in front-matter / table-of-contents / table-of-cases.* Meta-frame is acknowledged when the structure is the explicit subject. Refused inside chapter prose.

15. **"Citizens reading news of a corporate harm tomorrow..."**
    *Permitted.* Specific group-naming when "we" would be evasive. Specificity beats vague "we."

16. **The ch-13 closing sentence (xiaolai authors).**
    *Authorial latitude.* This rule frames the constraint; the principal author's voice has final say on the book's last line.

## Why this rule exists

Codex critique on the broader rewrite plan named that the chapters were over-using explicit signposting ("the chapter's point is...", "you should remember that...", "what this means for you"). The diagnostic was right; this rule is the constitutional anchor for the fix.

Beat 10 of every chapter has carried "What this means for you" as a beat title. The "you" framed the reader as addressee rather than co-investigator. The strict-version retitling — "What this might mean for us" — moves the addressee from outside-the-book to inside-the-analysis. Modal softening ("might mean") respects that applicability is contingent on the reader's situation; the chapter offers patterns, not prescriptions.

The book's central claim — that responsibility-laundering separates controller from cost-bearer through procedural distance — is undermined if the book's own voice separates author from reader through pronoun distance. This rule eliminates the parallel laundering. Standing beside is not a stylistic preference; it is the diagnostic the book applies to itself.
