## Title

**No One Did It**

## Subtitle

Responsibility Laundering, from the Scapegoat to the Algorithm

## The theme

The book's theme is **responsibility laundering** — power keeps the control and
the benefit while moving the blame, the liability, the moral cost, and the public
suspicion onto a weaker bearer: a junior employee, a proxy, a machine, a market,
a committee, a legal entity, a record.

**Recommended (states the theme):**

> How power keeps the control and gives away the blame.

**Alternatives — short / evocative (front cover):**

## Chapter summaries

> One paragraph per chapter (proposal-synopsis / chapter-cover length), drawn from the chapters by Blair and kept status-accurate for the live cases. For a tighter back-cover, a \~60-word cut of any of these is available on request. **Before any of these go on a public jacket, proposal, or site, run a Nancy (legal) scan** — cover copy is a higher defamation-exposure surface than the manuscript because it travels without the chapters' surrounding citations (the live-content blurbs — ch-8/9/10/12 — especially).

**1. The Altar Moves** — On the night of 27 February 1933 the Reichstag burned, and by dawn four thousand people who had never been near the building were under arrest. The young man caught with the matches, Marinus van der Lubbe, gave the regime the one visible face it needed to convert a fire into the emergency decree and Enabling Act that built the Third Reich — a substitution so complete that the response was waiting before the act occurred. Reaching back through the scapegoat of Leviticus, the *pharmakos* of Athens, and the lustrations of Rome, this opening chapter shows that civilization never stopped sacrificing substitutes; it only stopped naming the substitution out loud. The lesson it installs is the book's first instrument — watch for speed, scale mismatch, and absence — and its first hard sentence: when your face is the public face of a decision you cannot trace upward in writing, you are at the altar.

**2. The Four Goats** — A radiation machine that "could not" have burned its patients; a 737 that flew its crews into the sea; two anarchists executed in 1920s Massachusetts; eleven soldiers court-martialed for Abu Ghraib while the chain of approvals above them aged out of reach; thousands of Ukrainian children moved under decrees written in the language of care. From these five documented cases the chapter builds the book's working taxonomy — pure scapegoat, partial scapegoat, system/object alibi, and cost-bearing goat — naming each shape at the case that teaches it. Paired one-for-one with four interceptions (Dreyfus, Serpico, the Post Office Horizon reversal, the Stolen Generations apology), it installs the recognition that powers every chapter after it: we are inside one of the four shapes whenever the person publicly blamed does not match the person who controlled the decision.

**3. Who Could Have Stopped It?** — Volkswagen took sixteen months to move from "a technical issue" to "a few rogue engineers" to "the defeat-device software did it" — three alibis, each abandoned only as the last one collapsed. Setting Dieselgate beside the Ford Pinto forty years earlier, the chapter forges the book's central instrument: eight questions, asked in order, that pull *blame* apart from *responsibility* by tracking control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, and cost. Run slowly across both cases, the questions expose where each public story stopped short of the chain it claimed to close — and show that the diagnostic survives the leap from software to sheet metal, and from one regulatory era to another.

**4. The Proxy and the Sponsor** — Unmarked green uniforms seized Crimea while their government insisted anyone could buy such uniforms in a shop; a Buk missile tracked by its own brigade's photographs downed Flight MH17 over Donetsk; a Blackwater convoy opened fire in Nisour Square behind a contractor classification engineered to be neither soldier nor citizen. Three procedural shells, the chapter argues, cover one mechanism: a visible actor whose identity has been engineered out, and a sponsor who supplied the means and held the territory while staying clean. It adds three questions to the diagnostic — who supplied the means, who controls the ground, who would lose access if the proxy were prosecuted — and shows that when the answers converge on one entity, the shell has a builder.

**5. The Guilty Goat** — Warren Anderson's arrest at Bhopal airport in 1984 is the most photographed moment of corporate accountability of its decade; a May 1982 safety survey, naming sixty-one hazards, did not surface for fifteen years. This is the book's hardest case, because the named actor was genuinely in the chain — and that, the chapter shows, is exactly what makes the partial scapegoat work: a real culpability at one layer, deliberately treated as if it had settled the account for the design, cost-cutting, and regulatory layers above. Pairing Bhopal with the two 737 MAX crashes, where 346 deaths separated the laundering from its interception, it delivers a short, portable rule — when a guilty actor is named, do not stop; run the eight questions on the floor above.

**6. The Pretext** — "We do not have a policy of separating families at the border. Period," the Homeland Security secretary wrote — the day before a recording of separated children reached the public. Across three cases — family separation, the 2020 census citizenship question, and the 2019 hold on Ukraine military aid — the chapter exposes a shared grammar in which a real statute is made the agent of an action a named official chose, so that *the law required it* removes the decider from the sentence. Drawing on a pretext doctrine the federal courts have run since 1971, it installs six questions for any cited authority — chiefly, what the text actually requires, who recruited it, and what the documented purpose was — and shows that in each case the record names a different reason than the citation does.

**7. The Record Is the Battlefield** — Seema Misra was sent to prison in 2010, eight weeks pregnant, on the say-so of accounting software, after Post Office investigators told her no other sub-postmaster had reported the same problem. They had: hundreds had. Here the book turns the diagnostic on itself and finds its structural limit — when a single institution is at once the deployer, the holder of the audit trail, the complainant, the prosecutor, and the controller of disclosure, the eight questions can be asked but cannot be answered, because the evidence that would answer them is held by the accused. Through the Horizon scandal, with callbacks to Therac-25, Dreyfus, and MH17 at the artifact and state scales, the chapter shows that record-hardening is not one decision but a stack of individually unremarkable defaults — and that each layer must be attacked on its own.

**8. When Power Calls Itself the Goat** — In early 2025 a new administration signed three acts — the January 6 pardons, the Alien Enemies Act removals that sent Kilmar Abrego Garcia and roughly 137 others to El Salvador's CECOT despite a standing immigration order, and the DOGE records litigation — and defended each, in its own communications, as the correction of a prior injustice. The chapter names a single move running through all three (and through Watergate, the Iran-Contra pardons, and the al-Aulaqi memorandum across both parties): the accountability mechanism — prosecution, records statute, judicial review — is recast as the abuse, and dismantling it is recast as the reform. Reading the contemporaneous court orders, Federal Register entries, and dockets against the framings, it installs an administration-agnostic rule — calling the mechanism the laundering *is* the laundering — and shows that the eight-question diagnostic transfers intact: the frame inverts, the chain does not.

**9. The Model Did It** — Across spring 2025 three AI companies told the public, in three different layers of the same technology stack, that a non-human thing had decided: Meta's benchmark "variant" that scored thirtieth as a shipped model, OpenAI's "sycophantic" GPT-4o, and an industry framing of training as "data" rather than acquisition. The chapter reads the grammar — where the verb's subject is the corpus, the model, or the policy, a named human decision has been displaced — and walks the diagnostic three separate times, at input, deployment, and evaluation. Anchored in the *Bartz v. Anthropic* ruling (Judge Alsup's fair-use line drawn at the acquisition record, and the $1.5 billion settlement pending final approval as of the manuscript freeze), it shows the same record-control conflation diagnosed at the Post Office, now at AI scale — and that no AI executive has yet been personally adjudicated liable. The takeaway: demand three records in writing, and read a denial as the finding.

**10. War Is the Perfect Laundry** — It opens on Colin Powell's simulated-anthrax vial at the UN in February 2003 and the phrase that absorbed the collapse that followed — "the intelligence was wrong" — a framing that named the producing institution while the inquiry record located the gap between the hedged assessments and the public certainty in the rooms that controlled the case. Walking the eight questions through the Iraq WMD case (SSCI Phase II, the Duelfer Report, Chilcot's finding of "a certainty that was not justified") and stacking four further documented layers — Abu Ghraib's classified chain of command, MH17's proxy deniability, Blackwater's contractor shell, and the euphemized transfer of Ukrainian children — it shows war running five laundering layers at once. Its rule refuses the domain's claim to exception: the complexity is the laundering surface the diagnostic was built to name, and the diagnostic delivers not remedy but the record on which remedy can later rest.

**11. Make Responsibility Follow Control** — Three lawful documents — the Boeing deferred-prosecution agreement that closed above the Technical Pilots, the Bybee-Yoo torture memo, and the executive order that fenced the Robb-Silberman inquiry away from how policymakers used the intelligence — each contains no unlawful sentence and leaves the same hole: no named person bears the consequence if the control question fails. Running the eight questions forward as a design tool rather than backward as forensics, the chapter shows the hole is already filled in working law — the Park responsible-corporate-officer doctrine, Sarbanes-Oxley's personal certifications, the UK Senior Managers Regime, the Inquiries Act's compulsion power — and engages the strongest counter (that such regimes breed defensive compliance) on its merits. Named-attribution design does not eliminate laundering; it forces it to move and names the cell where it must. The portable rule: if no named person bears the consequence, the design is laundering by construction — add the name before it ships.

**12. Keep the Record** — Daniel Ellsberg's after-hours photocopying of the Pentagon Papers, and the backup tapes that career staff preserved while Oliver North's office shredded and deleted the Iran-Contra paper trail, anchor the layer that makes every prior remedy operate: the record. Against the three standing defenses for suppression — national security, deliberative-process privilege, and routine destruction — the chapter abstracts three portable rules: write it down with a named signer on the day; timestamp and keep custody across at least one party outside the accuser's line; make destruction itself a logged record. A ten-case table sorts surviving records from suppressed ones and shows the difference is the rules, while conceding the strongest counter — that aggressive record-keeping drives decisions into unrecorded channels — and the scope limit where only an outside counter-record reaches. The deficit is enforcement architecture, not statute; insist on a second custodian, because sole custody is the seam.

**13. A Reader's Field Guide** — The book opened on two goats and a watching community that could name the substitution; the closing chapter hands the reader the instrument to resume the watching. Six carryable pages consolidate the whole diagnostic — the eight questions, the four-category taxonomy, the three-layer AI stack, the five-layer war stack, a role-by-domain crosswalk for citizen, juror, journalist, employee, manager, contractor, professional, and voter, and a by-chapter index tethering each tool to the case that earned it. It meets its own strongest objection head-on — that a checklist stripped of its cases becomes a slogan — by keeping every artifact pointed back to its worked example, and it is honest about cost: most interceptions are partial, arrive late, and exact a price on the person who notices first. The inversion is the close: the reader is the escape, and "no one did it" was never a fact but a seam where a name had been lifted out of the sentence.
