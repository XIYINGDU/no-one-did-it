# Ukrainian Children — Cost Bearers Behind the Language of "Evacuation"

Case: Russian transfer and deportation of Ukrainian children
Domain: war / statecraft / international criminal law / forced transfer
Dates/place: Feb 24, 2022 – present (active); Ukrainian sovereign
territory under Russian occupation, deportations into the Russian
Federation and Russian-occupied territories (Crimea, Rostov, Krasnodar,
Moscow region, others).
Case type: cost-bearing goat

Classification note: The children are the cost bearers:
forcibly transferred, naturalized under Russian decrees, placed with
Russian families or in Russian institutions, and stripped of contact
with surviving relatives. Responsibility laundering operates through
the public language of "evacuation," "humanitarian relocation," and
"guardianship," which reframes the transfer as care rather than as
the forcible transfer of children prohibited by Article II(e) of the
1948 Genocide Convention and as the deportation of protected persons
prohibited by Articles 49 and 147 of the Fourth Geneva Convention.

```text
Crisis:
  From the early weeks of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine
  (Feb 24, 2022), Ukrainian children — including children from
  state institutions in occupied territory, children separated from
  their parents at filtration points, and children whose parents
  had been killed, detained, or deported — were transported into
  the Russian Federation. The Russian government and Russian-aligned
  authorities described these movements as "evacuation" or
  "rescue from a war zone." Russian Presidential Decree No. 330 (May
  30, 2022), as expanded by Decree No. 585 (Jul 11, 2022) and
  follow-on instruments, simplified the path to Russian citizenship
  for Ukrainian children, including orphans and unaccompanied
  minors, and authorized placement of Ukrainian children with
  Russian foster families. The OHCHR and Yale HRL reports describe
  these placements as functioning, in practice, as long-term
  guardianship and in some documented cases as adoption; the ICC
  arrest warrants characterize the underlying transfer as the war
  crime of unlawful deportation of children.

Official story (Russian government):
  Russian officials, including Presidential Commissioner for
  Children's Rights Maria Alekseyevna Lvova-Belova, have publicly
  described the transfers as humanitarian "evacuation" from active
  hostilities, characterized the citizenship grants as protective,
  and described placements with Russian families as care for
  children whose parents are absent. Lvova-Belova stated on Russian
  state television in 2022 that she had taken into her family a
  Ukrainian boy from Mariupol identified in Russian state media as
  "Filipp"; RIA Novosti and TASS reported the placement as an act
  of personal care. The ICC arrest warrant of March 17, 2023 names
  this and analogous placements within its allegation that
  Lvova-Belova bears individual criminal responsibility for the
  unlawful transfer of children; Russia does not recognize the
  ICC's jurisdiction and Lvova-Belova has denied wrongdoing. Her
  televised statement functions simultaneously as the strongest
  alibi (presenting the transfer as personal care) and as the
  strongest admission against AP I Art. 78's consent,
  identification-preservation, and return conditions; the chapter
  must narrate both functions.

Blame container:
  There is no scapegoat in the conventional sense — there is a
  laundering-by-language. The category of blame is **diffused into
  euphemism**: "evacuation," "humanitarian corridor," "guardianship,"
  "citizenship for orphans." The blame container is the linguistic
  and bureaucratic frame that presents forced transfer as care. The
  children themselves are not accused of anything; they bear the
  cost while the moral category of "perpetrator" is absorbed by
  abstractions (war, hardship, the conflict) and by procedural
  shells (decrees, institutional placement, citizenship registries).

Actual responsibility chain:
  1. President of the Russian Federation Vladimir Vladimirovich
     Putin: issued the decrees enabling the citizenship pathway
     (No. 330 of May 30, 2022; No. 585 of Jul 11, 2022; subsequent
     instruments). The ICC Pre-Trial Chamber II found reasonable
     grounds to believe Putin bears individual criminal
     responsibility for the alleged unlawful deportation and
     transfer (ICC arrest warrant, Mar 17, 2023).
  2. Russian Presidential Commissioner for Children's Rights Maria
     Alekseyevna Lvova-Belova: identified in OHCHR HRMMU reports
     and Yale HRL/Conflict Observatory investigations as a senior
     official overseeing the placement program, and named on the
     ICC arrest warrant of March 17, 2023 as alleged to bear
     individual criminal responsibility for the unlawful deportation
     and transfer of children. Lvova-Belova has spoken publicly
     about the program on Russian state media. Russia does not
     recognize the ICC's jurisdiction; Lvova-Belova denies the
     allegations.
  3. Ministry for Emergency Situations (EMERCOM), Ministry of
     Defence personnel, Federal Security Service (FSB) filtration
     officers: documented as operationally involved in identifying,
     screening, and moving children at filtration points and
     evacuation routes (OHCHR reports; Yale HRL/Conflict Observatory
     reports).
  4. Federal Agency for Children's Affairs and regional Russian
     authorities (Krasnodar Krai, Rostov Oblast, Moscow Region,
     Republic of Tatarstan, and others): received, placed, and
     issued citizenship documentation.
  5. Russian state media (RIA Novosti, TASS, RT) and the Office of
     the Presidential Commissioner: produced and amplified the
     "humanitarian evacuation" framing, with named officials on the
     record.
  6. Russian-installed occupation authorities in occupied Ukrainian
     territories: identified institutionalized and unaccompanied
     children and facilitated transfers (OHCHR; HRMMU reports).

Control:
  Operational control: Russian federal and regional authorities,
  with chain elements through EMERCOM, the Children's Rights
  Commissioner's office, regional social-services bodies, and
  occupation administrations. Decree-level control: Office of the
  President. The ICC Pre-Trial Chamber found reasonable grounds
  that Putin bears responsibility "for having committed the acts
  directly, jointly with others and/or through others (article
  25(3)(a) of the Rome Statute), and for his failure to exercise
  control properly over civilian and military subordinates who
  committed the acts, or allowed for their commission, and who
  were under his effective authority and control" (article 28(b))
  (ICC press release of Mar 17, 2023, summarizing the Pre-Trial
  Chamber finding; the warrants themselves remain partially
  redacted to protect investigations and witnesses).

Benefit:
  Russian state benefits described in the public record include:
  demographic gain in regions experiencing population decline;
  domestic political narrative ("rescue" of Russian-speaking
  children); pressure on the Ukrainian state's ability to
  account for its own population; integration of occupied
  territories into the Russian federal apparatus through the
  registration and naturalization of minors. The Children's
  Rights Commissioner's public role provides political benefit
  to its holder.

Knowledge:
  The Russian government's knowledge of the practice is
  documentary: the decrees themselves (Nos. 330 and 585 of 2022,
  and subsequent) name the practice and create its legal scaffold.
  The Children's Rights Commissioner has spoken publicly about
  the program in interviews with Russian state media. The ICC
  Pre-Trial Chamber's finding rests on reasonable grounds that
  the named individuals bear individual criminal responsibility
  — a stage that requires knowledge or imputed knowledge under
  Rome Statute Articles 25 and 28.

Preventability:
  Each transfer was preventable: do not move the child across
  the border; do not change the child's name or nationality; do
  not place the child with a foster family in the territory of
  the occupying power; restore the child to identified surviving
  relatives or to the protecting power (Ukraine) when contact is
  possible. International humanitarian law makes the receiving
  state's positive obligations explicit (GC IV Arts. 49–50, 132;
  Additional Protocol I Arts. 74, 78; 1989 CRC Arts. 7, 8, 11,
  35; 1948 Genocide Convention Art. II(e)).

Record controller:
  The record is split. Russian federal and regional databases
  hold citizenship grants, foster placements, and institutional
  placements — not publicly accessible. Ukrainian government:
  the Office of the Ombudsman for Human Rights and the National
  Information Bureau maintain the "Children of War"
  (childrenofwar.gov.ua) registry — publicly searchable. The
  Yale School of Public Health's Humanitarian Research Lab
  (Yale HRL), under the State Department–funded Conflict
  Observatory program, has produced repeated open-source
  investigations geolocating Russian facilities receiving
  Ukrainian children. The OHCHR Human Rights Monitoring Mission
  in Ukraine (HRMMU) maintains UN-side documentation. The ICC
  holds investigative files (not public). Reuters, AP, and the
  Financial Times have produced reconstructions naming
  individual children and locations.

Cost bearer:
  Ukrainian children. The Ukrainian government's "Children of
  War" portal listed, as of public reporting in 2023–2024,
  approximately 19,500+ children recorded on the Ukrainian
  government's Children of War registry as deported or forcibly
  transferred, per the registry's own classification (count as
  of 2026-05-25; figure live and contested) — a figure based on
  Ukrainian case-by-case records and dependent on access to
  families and occupied-territory testimony. The Yale HRL reports document
  a network of at least 43 Russian facilities (Mar 2023 report)
  and subsequent expansions identifying further sites. Returns
  facilitated by mediators (Qatar, the Vatican, Save Ukraine
  and other NGOs) had brought back several hundred children
  by public counts in 2023–2024; the vast majority of the
  registered cases remained unreturned. (Numbers: B-grade —
  investigative and government counts, not adjudicated;
  precise figures contested and changing.)

  Indirect cost bearers: surviving relatives unable to locate
  the children; the Ukrainian state's demographic and legal
  capacity to recover and reintegrate its citizens; the broader
  international legal order's bright-line rule against forced
  transfer of children of a protected group.

How alibi hardened:
  - The publication and consistent state-media use of the words
    "evacuation," "rescue," "humanitarian relocation,"
    "guardianship."
  - The decree apparatus (Nos. 330 of 2022, 585 of 2022, and
    follow-ons) made naturalization a routine bureaucratic act
    rather than a coercive one.
  - High-profile placements (Lvova-Belova's publicly-stated taking
    of the boy identified by Russian state media as "Filipp" into
    her household) framed the program as personal care.
  - The Russian state's non-recognition of the ICC's jurisdiction
    permits domestic media to dismiss the warrants as political.
  - Closed access to Russian institutional records makes the
    children individually invisible to international monitors.

How alibi weakens (record points pressing on the language):
  - ICC Pre-Trial Chamber II warrants, Mar 17, 2023, against
    Putin and Lvova-Belova for the war crime of unlawful
    deportation of population (children) and unlawful transfer
    of population (children) from occupied areas of Ukraine to
    the Russian Federation, contrary to articles 8(2)(a)(vii)
    and 8(2)(b)(viii) of the Rome Statute.
  - UN General Assembly resolutions (ES-11/4 and related).
  - OHCHR HRMMU periodic reports on the human rights situation
    in Ukraine documenting forced-transfer cases.
  - Yale HRL/Conflict Observatory series (initial report Feb 14,
    2023, and subsequent reports) identifying receiving
    facilities, transfer routes, re-education practices, and
    documented cases.
  - UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on
    Ukraine reports (Resolution 49/1 mandate) addressing forced
    transfer of children.
  - PACE Resolutions (Council of Europe) on the matter.
  - AP, Reuters, Financial Times, and the New York Times
    investigative reconstructions identifying individual
    children, named officials, and named facilities — anchored
    in named-source on-the-record statements where available.
  - Returns coordinated by Save Ukraine, Bring Kids Back UA,
    and mediating states (Qatar, the Holy See) — each return
    is documentary evidence that the child was held and
    needed to be retrieved.

Best counterargument:
  Strongest defense of the Russian official position: in
  conditions of active hostilities, some movement of children
  out of immediate harm is permitted under IHL (GC IV Art. 24;
  AP I Art. 78); citizenship grants to orphans can in principle
  be regularization of status; placement with families can in
  principle be foster care. The book's response: IHL conditions
  the permissibility tightly — Art. 78 of AP I requires
  consent of parents or legal guardians where they can be
  found, requires temporary character, requires the child's
  identification documentation be preserved, requires return
  when conditions allow, and requires the receiving party to
  ensure education in the child's own language, culture, and
  religion. The decree apparatus's grant of Russian
  citizenship, change of legal status, and placement with
  Russian families is the opposite of "temporary and
  identification-preserving"; the Pre-Trial Chamber's
  reasonable-grounds finding rests on this distinction. Article
  78(1) of Additional Protocol I expressly forbids foreign
  evacuation of children from occupied territory on safety
  grounds — the carve-out the Russian framing requires is not in
  the text. The further Art 78(2)–(3) conditions on consent,
  identification preservation, and return operate independently.

  Second counterargument the chapter must address: the scale
  figures (19,500+ Ukrainian registry; 6,000+ at known Russian
  facilities per Yale HRL) are not adjudicated. Response: scale
  is B-graded; the legal characterization of the practice does
  not depend on resolving the exact number — it depends on the
  documented existence of the practice at scale, which is A-graded
  through the ICC warrants and OHCHR documentation.

Evidence grade: A

  **B** for the scale and operational specifics:

      but operational, not adjudicated).
    - Major-outlet investigative reconstructions (AP, Reuters, FT, NYT).
  Procedural-stage cap: both named living individuals (Putin,
  Lvova-Belova) are at the **arrest-warrant stage** at the ICC; there
  is no trial verdict. The chapter must use: "the ICC has charged X
  with Y; the case is at warrant stage as of 2023; no trial verdict";
  the words "guilty," "war criminal," or "convicted" are forbidden
  without the qualifier "alleged" or specific procedural posture per
  `defamation-wording/SKILL.md`.

  The ICC chose Rome Statute Article 8 (war-crime articles on
  unlawful deportation/transfer) over Article 6(e) (genocide) for
  individual criminal liability at the warrant stage. Article II(e)
  of the 1948 Genocide Convention is in dispute at the inter-state
  level in *Allegations of Genocide under the Convention on the
  Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Ukraine v.
  Russian Federation)* (ICJ Case No. 182, filed February 26, 2022),
  but the case has taken a posture that complicates any simple
  "Russia on trial at the ICJ" framing: the ICJ's Judgment on
  Preliminary Objections of February 2, 2024 found jurisdiction over
  only part of Ukraine's claim, and the Court's Order of December 5,
  2025 admitted eight Russian counter-claims for adjudication on the
  merits. As of 2026-05-25 the merits phase remains pending;
  proceedings extend into 2027.
```
