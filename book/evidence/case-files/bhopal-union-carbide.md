# Bhopal / Union Carbide — Case File

Case name: Union Carbide India Limited methyl isocyanate (MIC) release,
Bhopal
Domain: industrial disaster / corporate liability / transnational
regulation
Dates and place: Night of December 2–3, 1984, Union Carbide India
Limited (UCIL) pesticide plant, Bhopal, Madhya Pradesh, India. Indian
Supreme Court settlement: February 14–15, 1989. Indian Supreme Court
review judgment: October 3, 1991 (*Union Carbide Corporation v. Union
of India*, AIR 1992 SC 248). US 2nd Circuit decision: *In re Union
Carbide Corp. Gas Plant Disaster at Bhopal*, 809 F.2d 195 (2d Cir. 1987)
— forum non conveniens dismissal upheld; later parent-liability suits
dismissed by SDNY/2d Cir. through 2014.
Case type: system/object alibi
Secondary case type (hybrid): partial scapegoat — Warren Anderson named, parent corporation shielded. See "Best counterargument" and "Open questions" below for the explicit hybrid decision record.

```text
Crisis:
On the night of December 2–3, 1984, approximately 40 tonnes of methyl
isocyanate (MIC) escaped from Tank E610 at UCIL's Bhopal pesticide
plant. The cloud drifted over the densely populated J.P. Nagar and
adjacent settlements. Indian government affidavits to the Supreme
Court of India (1991) acknowledged 3,000+ immediate deaths; subsequent
ICMR-led longitudinal studies and state government figures place total
disaster-attributable deaths at well over 15,000 across the years
1984–1994, with 500,000+ persons exposed. The disaster is the worst
industrial accident in recorded history by acute mortality.

Official story:
Two stories ran in parallel and reinforced each other:
1. **Plant-level failure / sabotage.** Union Carbide Corporation (UCC,
   the US parent) commissioned an internal investigation (the Kalelkar
   report, March 1985) and a later report by Arthur D. Little Inc.
   (1988), both of which attributed the release to deliberate sabotage
   by a "disgruntled employee" who allegedly introduced water into Tank
   E610. UCC has maintained this position in US filings through the 2d
   Circuit appeals.
2. **The plant failed, not the corporation.** UCC framed UCIL as an
   Indian-managed subsidiary operating under Indian regulation, with
   UCC holding only a passive 50.9% equity interest. Under this framing
   the "plant" (its operators, its maintenance, the Indian regulators)
   is the named cause; the US parent is a distant shareholder.

Blame container:
The **plant itself** — its safety systems (refrigeration unit
disconnected, vent gas scrubber and flare tower inadequately maintained
or offline), and the local operators — became the named cause in
international coverage and in UCC's legal framing. Warren Anderson, UCC
chairman, was personally charged with culpable homicide by Indian
authorities in 1984 and again in 1991/1992; he was arrested on arrival
in Bhopal on December 7, 1984, released on bail, and left the country.
India's extradition request (filed 2003, formally pursued 2004–2011)
was never honored by the United States. Anderson died in 2014, never
extradited. The personification of blame in one absent executive,
combined with the reification of the "plant" as cause, is the alibi.
[Evidence grade: A]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Union Carbide Corporation (Danbury, Connecticut) — design authority
  for the MIC unit at Bhopal. UCC's Engineering Department in South
  Charleston, West Virginia produced the original process design;
  UCIL's "Operational Safety Survey" of May 1982 by a UCC team
  documented 61 hazards (including 30 major) and was transmitted to UCC
  management. UCC controlled, via its majority equity and its technical
  service agreements, the safety standards and the budget envelope
  within which UCIL operated. Cost-reduction directives from UCC
  Danbury in 1983–1984 are documented in plaintiffs' filings and in
  Kim Fortun's archival reconstruction.

  UCIL local management exercised day-to-day operational control,
  including the decision to keep the refrigeration unit off (energy
  savings) and the operating practices in the MIC storage area on the
  night of the release.

  Benefit:
  Union Carbide Corporation — through dividend extraction from UCIL,
  the cost-reduction programme that reduced safety staffing and
  maintenance at Bhopal (1983–1984), and the asset structure that left
  UCIL as the legally responsible Indian entity. Dow Chemical Company,
  which acquired Union Carbide in 2001, benefited by acquiring the
  brand without the operational tail (and has consistently asserted
  that the 1989 settlement extinguished further claims).

  Knowledge:
  - May 1982 UCC Operational Safety Survey: 61 hazards (30 major)
    identified at Bhopal, including specific warnings about MIC
    handling.
  - September 1984 internal UCC memorandum: the MIC unit's sister
    plant at Institute, West Virginia had had a "runaway reaction"
    near-miss; the technical analysis was not transmitted in actionable
    form to UCIL Bhopal.
  - UCIL's own internal incident log records repeated MIC leaks in 1981
    (one operator fatality) and 1982.

  Preventability:
  - Refrigeration system on Tank E610 was disabled in mid-1984 (energy
    cost savings) — this is the single most consequential preventable
    failure (refrigeration would have suppressed the MIC reaction even
    if water ingress occurred).
  - Vent gas scrubber was undersized for the actual release rate by
    design (a UCC design parameter).
  - Flare tower was offline for maintenance on the night of release.
  - Population siting: J.P. Nagar had grown around the plant; UCC's
    siting standards for the analogous Institute, WV plant would not
    have permitted that population density.
  - Storage volume in Tank E610 exceeded UCC's own operational
    guidance.

  Record controller:
  Multiple layers, fragmented by jurisdiction:
  - UCC archives, Danbury / Dow Chemical custody (post-2001).
  - UCIL records seized by the Government of India (Madhya Pradesh
    state and the Central Bureau of Investigation).
  - Indian Supreme Court record (case files of *Union Carbide
    Corporation v. Union of India* and *Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of
    India*).
  - US Federal courts: SDNY docket for the original consolidated
    suits; 2d Circuit appellate record.
  - Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) longitudinal study
    archives.
  - Bhopal Medical Appeal and Sambhavna Trust (NGO-curated medical
    and survivor records).
  Crucially, UCC and the Government of India BOTH had an interest in
  closing the case at the 1989 settlement; the survivor groups did not
  control the settlement record.

  Cost bearer:
  Bhopal residents — 3,000+ immediate deaths (Indian government figure
  in 1991 Supreme Court submissions); 15,000–25,000 deaths attributable
  to the disaster across two decades (state government / ICMR figures,
  range varies by source); 500,000+ persons exposed and surveyed in
  ICMR cohorts. Survivors absorbed lifelong respiratory, ocular,
  reproductive, and neurological harm. The 1989 settlement was
  $470 million — under $700 per surviving claimant by some estimates
  and a fraction of UCC's tort exposure under US standards. Second
  generation (children of exposed parents) shows elevated congenital
  disorder rates per Bhopal Medical Appeal / Sambhavna Trust studies;
  ongoing groundwater contamination at the abandoned plant site
  (documented by Greenpeace 1999, CSE/Centre for Science and
  Environment 2009) imposes continuing cost on residents not exposed
  in 1984 at all.

How the alibi hardened:
1. **Parent-subsidiary legal architecture.** The 1989 Indian Supreme
   Court settlement ($470m) was contested by survivor groups but
   ultimately upheld in *Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India*,
   AIR 1992 SC 248 (Oct 3, 1991). The settlement closed the civil case
   against UCC in India; criminal case continued separately.
2. **Forum non conveniens.** *In re Union Carbide Corp. Gas Plant
   Disaster at Bhopal*, 809 F.2d 195 (2d Cir. 1987) affirmed dismissal
   of US-court tort claims on forum non conveniens grounds — the
   Bhopal disaster would be litigated in India, against UCIL primarily,
   not in the US against UCC.
3. **Sabotage narrative.** The Arthur D. Little (1988) report,
   commissioned by UCC, named a "disgruntled employee" — never
   publicly identified — as the cause. This shifted the frame from
   "design and maintenance failure" to "criminal act by a single
   actor", a classic pure-scapegoat sub-move embedded inside the
   system/object alibi.
4. **Anderson scapegoat.** UCC chairman Warren Anderson's December 7,
   1984 arrest-and-release in Bhopal made him the personified blame
   container. The decade-long Indian extradition attempt against
   Anderson (and only Anderson) ran in parallel to the corporate-
   liability cases and absorbed the moral attention that might have
   pursued the parent corporation's design and cost-cutting decisions.
   The Bhopal Chief Judicial Magistrate convicted seven Indian
   nationals (former UCIL officials) of culpable homicide not
   amounting to murder on June 7, 2010 — sentences of two years each.
   No conviction of UCC or its officers.
5. **Acquisition shield.** Dow Chemical's 2001 acquisition of Union
   Carbide allowed Dow to assert (consistently) that the 1989
   settlement extinguished its liability; UCC has continued to exist
   as a wholly-owned Dow subsidiary, complicating chain-of-custody
   arguments.

How the alibi weakened:
1. **Indian Supreme Court 1991 review.** AIR 1992 SC 248 upheld the
   1989 settlement amount but reinstated criminal proceedings against
   UCC and Anderson, formally rejecting the position that the civil
   settlement extinguished criminal liability.
2. **2010 CJM Bhopal convictions** of seven UCIL officials — while
   only two-year sentences, the convictions establish in court that
   the disaster was not a one-employee sabotage event.
3. **Internal UCC documents in litigation.** Plaintiffs' filings in
   *Sahu v. Union Carbide Corporation* (SDNY, 1999–2014) introduced
   UCC engineering documents showing UCC's continuing design and
   safety authority over the MIC unit; the 2014 dismissal (Sahu II)
   was on statute-of-limitations grounds, not on the substantive
   parent-liability question.
4. **Peer-reviewed scholarship.** Fortun, Kim. *Advocacy after Bhopal:
   Environmentalism, Disaster, New Global Orders*. University of
   Chicago Press, 2001. Lapierre, Dominique, and Javier Moro.
   *Five Past Midnight in Bhopal*. Warner, 2002. Both reconstruct the
   UCC chain in detail.
5. **ICMR studies and Sambhavna Trust longitudinal record** establish
   the long-tail mortality and morbidity in a form that the 1989
   settlement figure clearly cannot answer.
6. **Site contamination record.** Greenpeace's 1999 sampling at the
   abandoned plant site (heavy metals, naphthol, mercury) and CSE's
   2009 study extended the responsibility-chain time-horizon past the
   night of December 1984: the abandoned plant continues to cause
   harm and no party has accepted remediation cost.

Best counterargument:
"This is partial scapegoat, not system/object alibi. UCC did not escape
blame — its chairman was personally charged with culpable homicide and
its name became globally synonymous with industrial disaster; the
company paid $470 million; seven UCIL officials were criminally
convicted. The 'alibi' interpretation overstates the corporate
shielding. Furthermore, the sabotage theory was raised by UCC but
never accepted as the basis of any legal settlement, and the Indian
Supreme Court repeatedly held UCC to account."

Shirley's response: under the book's taxonomy, the test is not "did
anyone get punished?" but "did responsibility follow control, benefit,
knowledge, and preventability?" The chain shows UCC controlled the
design, benefited from the cost-cuts, knew about the May 1982 hazards
identified by its own audit team, and could have prevented the disaster
by maintaining refrigeration, scrubber, and flare tower at design
specification. UCC paid $470m — that is approximately UCC's 1985
revenue from Indian operations alone, and a small fraction of US-court
tort exposure under analogous American precedent (e.g., the Exxon
Valdez damages structure). The "plant failed" frame is the operative
alibi at the level of public narrative and legal liability. The Warren
Anderson chase is the partial-scapegoat layer that runs on top of the
system/object alibi. Therefore: **primary system/object alibi, secondary
partial scapegoat** — explicitly a hybrid case, named as such.
```
