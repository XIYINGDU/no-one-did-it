# Alien Enemies Act Invocation and Kilmar Abrego Garcia Wrongful Removal — Case File

Case: March 14, 2025 invocation of the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 (50 U.S.C. §§ 21–24) to authorize summary removal of Venezuelan nationals alleged to be members of Tren de Aragua; the parallel wrongful removal of Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia, a Maryland resident with a 2019 grant of withholding of removal, to El Salvador's CECOT (Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo) prison; Supreme Court per curiam order in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949 (Apr. 10, 2025), directing facilitation of return; SCOTUS per curiam in Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931 (Apr. 7, 2025), and subsequent District Court litigation.
Domain: Alien Enemies Act (1798) — wartime invocation of the President's authority to remove "alien enemies" without ordinary immigration process; due process under the Fifth Amendment; immigration withholding of removal under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3); habeas corpus; Article III remedial power; Supreme Court "shadow docket" practice.
Dates/place: Presidential Proclamation 10903, "Invocation of the Alien Enemies Act Regarding the Invasion of the United States by Tren de Aragua," signed March 14, 2025 (90 Fed. Reg. 13033 (Mar. 20, 2025)). Removal flights to El Salvador's CECOT facility, March 15, 2025. Lead litigation: J.G.G. v. Trump, No. 1:25-cv-00766 (D.D.C., Chief Judge James E. Boasberg, filed March 15, 2025); Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931 (S. Ct. Apr. 7, 2025) (per curiam vacating TRO on venue grounds, requiring habeas in the place of detention); subsequent W.D. Tex. and S.D. Tex. proceedings. Abrego Garcia litigation: Abrego Garcia v. Noem, No. 8:25-cv-00951 (D. Md., Judge Paula Xinis); Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949 (S. Ct. Apr. 10, 2025) (per curiam); Fourth Circuit order April 17, 2025.
Case type: system/object alibi
Secondary case type: cost-bearing goat

Hybrid classification note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`, explicit hybrid declaration). (1) Primary — system/object alibi: the 1798 Alien Enemies Act itself is reified as the operative agent. The procedural shell (a wartime statute repurposed to summary peacetime removal) becomes the named cause: removals are framed as the statute's automatic operation against "alien enemies," not as discretionary executive acts. The legal-form shell absorbs accountability so that no individual removal decision needs to defend itself on the merits of the affected person's status. (2) Secondary — cost-bearing goat: Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia, his family, and (per public record) at least 137 other Venezuelan nationals removed to CECOT on the March 15 flights absorb the cost — incarceration in a high-security foreign prison, separation from U.S.-based families, in Abrego Garcia's case removal directly contrary to an existing 2019 withholding order. The government has formally acknowledged the removal was an "administrative error" as to Abrego Garcia specifically. The chapter-9 *move* this case teaches is **reverse scapegoating** in its emergency-powers variant: federal judges enforcing due process are recast in administration messaging as enablers of "terrorists" and "invaders"; the people removed (most without adjudicated gang membership) are recast as enemy combatants. "Reverse scapegoating" is the descriptor of how the alibi is constructed, not a fifth taxonomy label.

```text
Crisis:
On March 14, 2025, the President signed Proclamation 10903 declaring
that Tren de Aragua, a transnational criminal organization
originating in Venezuela, had perpetrated an "invasion" of the
United States, and invoking the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 (50 U.S.C.
§§ 21–24) to authorize summary apprehension, restraint, and removal
of Venezuelan nationals 14 years of age or older identified as
members of Tren de Aragua. The Alien Enemies Act had been invoked
only three times previously in U.S. history: the War of 1812
(against British nationals), World War I (against German nationals),
and World War II (against German, Italian, and Japanese nationals).
Its peacetime application is unprecedented.

On March 15, 2025, the U.S. government conducted removal flights of
approximately 137 Venezuelan nationals (and a smaller number of
Salvadoran nationals; counts vary by manifest and reporting; B-grade
for exact count) to El Salvador, where they were transferred to CECOT
(Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo), a maximum-security prison
constructed under the El Salvador government of President Nayib
Bukele as part of his "estado de excepción" detention program.

The morning of March 15, 2025, the ACLU and Democracy Forward filed a
class action in the District Court for the District of Columbia on
behalf of five Venezuelan men in immigration detention. Chief Judge
James E. Boasberg held an emergency hearing the same day, issued a
temporary restraining order, certified a class, and ordered that any
removal flights then en route be turned back. The flights continued
to El Salvador. The government's subsequent position was that the
TRO did not bind aircraft already over international waters; Chief
Judge Boasberg's April 16, 2025 finding was that there was "probable
cause" to find the government in criminal contempt of the TRO.

Separately, on March 15, 2025, Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia — a
Salvadoran national, Maryland resident, married to a U.S. citizen,
father of three U.S.-citizen children, with a 2019 grant of
withholding of removal from an immigration judge based on a
particularised threat from gangs in his home town of Los Nogales,
San Salvador — was removed to El Salvador on one of the March 15
flights and transferred to CECOT. The government later acknowledged
in court filings that the removal was the result of an
"administrative error": the 2019 withholding order specifically
forbade his removal to El Salvador.

Abrego Garcia's wife and brother sued in the District of Maryland
(No. 8:25-cv-00951, Judge Paula Xinis). Judge Xinis ordered the
government to "facilitate and effectuate" his return. The government
appealed; the Fourth Circuit declined to stay. The Supreme Court,
in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949, on April 10, 2025, per curiam
unanimously granted the application in part — it lifted the District
Court's deadline as moot (the administrative stay having let the
deadline pass) and required clarification of the term "effectuate,"
but otherwise affirmed: "The order properly requires the Government
to 'facilitate' Abrego Garcia's release from custody in El Salvador
and to ensure that his case is handled as it would have been had he
not been improperly sent to El Salvador." Justice Sotomayor, joined
by Justices Kagan and Jackson, wrote a separate statement.

The Supreme Court's parallel ruling in Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931, on
April 7, 2025, per curiam vacated the D.D.C. TRO on venue grounds
— holding that detainees challenging removal under the Alien
Enemies Act must proceed by habeas corpus in the place of detention
(typically W.D. Tex. or S.D. Tex.), not by APA action in D.D.C.
The ruling did not address the merits of the AEA invocation; it
shifted the procedural battlefield. Subsequent litigation in W.D.
Tex. and S.D. Tex. produced rulings (including by Trump-appointed
judges) restricting AEA removals on due-process grounds.

Official story (chapter-9 reverse-scapegoating frame, drawn from
on-the-record proclamation and statements):
Proclamation 10903 characterizes Tren de Aragua's U.S. presence as
an "invasion" — adopting the Article IV, § 4 / wartime register.
Administration communications surrounding the March 15 removals
characterized the removed individuals as "terrorists," "the worst
of the worst," and "members of a foreign terrorist organization."
Subsequent statements characterized:
  - Chief Judge Boasberg as an "activist judge" enabling "violent
    criminals" to stay in the United States;
  - the plaintiffs in J.G.G. v. Trump as protecting "terrorists";
  - Judge Xinis's orders in Abrego Garcia as judicial overreach
    into foreign affairs;
  - the Supreme Court's facilitate-return order as inappropriately
    intrusive on Article II powers.
The framing recasts:
  - federal judges as enablers of foreign criminal enterprises;
  - removed individuals (most without adjudicated gang membership;
    one — Abrego Garcia — under a 2019 withholding order forbidding
    removal to El Salvador) as enemy combatants;
  - the President as goat-bearing protector of the nation acting
    under wartime statutory authority.

Blame container:
Three stacked blame containers. (A) "Tren de Aragua" as the
generalized enemy — used to attach a terrorism category to every
removed person regardless of individuated finding. (B) "Activist
judges" / "obstructionist courts" — the persons enforcing
due-process limits are blamed for hampering enforcement against
the generalized enemy. (C) The 1798 Alien Enemies Act itself —
treated as automatically operating, as if the statute's application
to peacetime gang membership were a settled feature of the law
rather than a contested executive interpretation. The chapter
must keep these three containers separate.

Actual responsibility chain:
  1. The President: signed Proclamation 10903 invoking the AEA;
     authorized removal flights. The clemency/proclamation
     authority is plenary; the AEA invocation is statutory and
     conditional on the statute's predicates being met (a
     declared war, invasion, or predatory incursion by a foreign
     nation or government — the statute's text is specific).
  2. The Attorney General and Secretary of Homeland Security:
     operational authority for the removals; signatories or
     supervisors of removal orders.
  3. ICE / ERO field officers: operational execution of
     identifications and removals.
  4. The El Salvador government (Bukele administration): received
     the removed individuals at CECOT; bilateral arrangement
     details are partially public. El Salvador is not a U.S.
     domestic-law actor and the chapter must keep its conduct
     separate from U.S. responsibility-mapping.
  5. The judiciary: Chief Judge Boasberg (D.D.C.); Judge Xinis
     (D. Md.); subsequent W.D. Tex. and S.D. Tex. judges; the
     Fourth Circuit; the Supreme Court. The judicial branch is in
     the chain not as wrongdoer but as the enforcement
     infrastructure the chapter analyzes.

Control:
  - The AEA invocation is presidential and (per the statute) the
    proclamation establishes the conditions for removal authority.
    Whether peacetime gang activity satisfies the statute's
    "invasion or predatory incursion" predicates is the merits
    question; multiple federal courts have ruled the predicates
    are not met. See Just Security analysis and the W.D. Tex.
    rulings below.
  - The Abrego Garcia removal specifically was, by the
    government's own subsequent admission, an "administrative
    error" — the operational control over individual identification
    is the agency-level question.
  - The Supreme Court has asserted controlling authority over the
    procedural channel (habeas in place of detention per Trump v.
    J.G.G.) and over the remedy for wrongful removal (the
    "facilitate" formula in Noem v. Abrego Garcia).
  - The substantive question of whether CECOT detention conforms
    to U.S. or international human-rights standards is governed
    by Salvadoran domestic law as to operations; the U.S.
    obligations arise under the Convention Against Torture
    (CAT) and the immigration withholding statute.

Benefit:
  - Political principal: rhetorical benefit from a high-visibility
    mass removal characterized as wartime enforcement; ability to
    consolidate the "invasion" frame into operational acts.
  - Bilateral benefit: the El Salvador government has used CECOT
    transfers as part of its own security narrative; the U.S.
    transfers contribute foreign nationals to the Salvadoran
    custody apparatus, on what reporting characterizes as a
    paid-detention basis.
  - Procedural benefit: invoking a wartime statute shifts the
    procedural posture of challenges from administrative-process
    review (where the government typically must show
    individualized findings) to wartime-deference review (where
    courts have historically applied substantial deference).

Knowledge:
The administration's knowledge of the relevant constraints is
documentary: the 2019 withholding order in Abrego Garcia's case
existed on file in the immigration record system; the AEA's
historical invocations and statutory predicates are matters of
public legal record; the named removed individuals' presence in
the United States with various forms of relief or pending
proceedings was known to ICE. The "administrative error"
characterization for Abrego Garcia is a documented government
admission. For other removed individuals on the March 15 flights,
the question of what individuated knowledge ICE had of gang
membership is the litigated question.

Preventability:
Each individual removal was preventable in named ways the chapter
must enumerate:
  - Honor the 2019 withholding order for Abrego Garcia.
  - Provide individuated due process — notice of designation as
    "alien enemy," opportunity to contest the designation, opportunity
    to claim withholding or asylum on protection grounds.
  - Comply with TROs issued in J.G.G. v. Trump.
  - Not transfer individuals to CECOT pending judicial resolution
    of the AEA invocation's lawfulness.
  - Adopt the procedural channel SCOTUS later identified (habeas
    in place of detention) before mass removal, not after.

Record controller:
  - DHS / ICE / ERO: hold the manifests of who was on the March
    15 flights and the agency-level documentation of identifications.
  - The U.S. government: holds the diplomatic notes and
    bilateral-arrangement records with El Salvador for the CECOT
    transfers (partially public, partially classified or
    diplomatic-channel).
  - The federal courts: hold the docket records, including the
    detailed proffers in J.G.G. and Abrego Garcia litigation.
  - El Salvador's government: holds CECOT custody records;
    Salvadoran human-rights organizations and CSO observers have
    documented conditions in CECOT to the extent permitted by the
    Salvadoran government.
  - Counsel for the removed individuals (ACLU, Democracy Forward,
    and private counsel for Abrego Garcia) hold litigation
    records.
  - Journalists: AP, Reuters, NYT, WaPo, ProPublica, El Faro
    (Salvadoran) reporting hold an independent reconstruction
    of the flights and conditions.

Cost bearer:
  - Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia: removed to El Salvador and
    detained at CECOT contrary to a 2019 withholding order; his
    return is the subject of ongoing litigation; his wife Jennifer
    Vasquez Sura and three children (U.S. citizens) bear the
    family cost.
  - Approximately 137 Venezuelan nationals removed on March 15
    flights to CECOT (count B-grade; reconstruction by ProPublica,
    AP, and CBS News). The individuated gang-membership findings
    underlying the designations are the litigated question; the
    chapter must use "alleged" until individuated findings issue.
  - The families of the removed individuals: U.S.-based families
    and home-country families bear continuing separation.
  - The institutional record of the immigration courts and
    withholding process: the Abrego Garcia case in particular
    demonstrates that a withholding order — a final administrative
    adjudication — can be overridden operationally, with the
    correction process more demanding than the underlying order.

How the alibi hardened:
  - Use of an 18th-century statute imports the rhetorical register
    of wartime; "alien enemy" carries the connotation of armed
    foreign-state combatant, applied here to alleged gang members.
  - "Invasion" framing in Proclamation 10903 maps the situation
    onto Article IV, § 4 / wartime authorities.
  - Speed of removal — within hours of the proclamation —
    foreclosed individuated process. CECOT custody outside U.S.
    territory complicates ordinary habeas remedies.
  - Public messaging characterizing all removed individuals as
    "the worst of the worst" treats individuated due-process
    challenge as procedural pedantry against an obvious enemy.
  - Recasting judicial intervention as enabling terrorism shifts
    the moral frame.

How the alibi weakens (record points pressing on the inversion):
  - Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949 (per curiam, Apr. 10, 2025) —
    the unanimous Supreme Court affirmed the District Court's
    "facilitate" remedy. The decision is the highest-grade
    rejection of the position that the removal was outside
    judicial cognizance.
  - The government's own admission that the Abrego Garcia removal
    was an "administrative error" — a confession in the
    operational record.
  - District-court rulings in W.D. Tex. and S.D. Tex. (May 2025
    and onward) by judges including Trump appointees finding that
    the AEA's predicates ("invasion or predatory incursion") are
    not met by peacetime gang activity.
  - Chief Judge Boasberg's April 16, 2025 probable-cause-for-
    contempt finding documents the procedural defiance of the
    March 15 TRO.
  - The 2019 immigration-judge withholding order in Abrego
    Garcia's case — a durable judicial finding in the immigration
    record system.
  - Reporting (ProPublica, AP, CBS News) reconstructing the
    manifests, conditions, and individual stories of the
    removed individuals.
  - El Salvador-side reporting (El Faro) on CECOT conditions.
  - International human-rights monitoring including UN OHCHR
    statements on CECOT and on the U.S. removals.

Best counterargument:
The strongest defenses of the administration position the chapter
must steelman:
(1) Executive authority over foreign affairs and immigration removal
is broad. The 1798 statute remains on the books; whether its
predicates are met is a question on which the President's view is
entitled to deference. Korematsu v. United States, 323 U.S. 214
(1944) is the often-cited (and widely criticized) precedent for
broad deference to wartime executive judgment, though Trump v.
Hawaii, 585 U.S. 667 (2018) repudiated Korematsu in the relevant
respect. The chapter concedes that the deference question is
genuinely contested.
(2) The Abrego Garcia "administrative error" is just that — an
error in a mass-operation context — and the government has stated
it will work to facilitate his return. Errors do not impeach the
general lawfulness of the program.
(3) Tren de Aragua membership IS a serious public-safety concern
that the immigration enforcement system has handled inadequately
under prior administrations; a more aggressive enforcement posture
is policy choice within executive discretion.

The book's response:
(1) The deference question does not survive the textual specificity
of the AEA: the statute's predicates require "declared war,"
"invasion," or "predatory incursion" by a "foreign nation or
government." Tren de Aragua is a criminal organization, not a
foreign government; multiple federal courts (including Trump
appointees) have so held. The "deference" defense requires
re-reading the statute to elide its predicates.
(2) The "administrative error" defense, taken seriously, is itself
an admission of the system/object-alibi structure: an automated
mass-operation is presented as the actor, with individuated mistakes
absorbed by the system's design rather than by named decision-makers.
The chapter uses this admission as evidence FOR its diagnostic, not
against it.
(3) The policy-discretion defense conflates ends and means: a
serious public-safety concern does not entail a wartime-statute
means; ordinary removal under 8 U.S.C. § 1227 with individuated
process is available. The wartime route was chosen because it
circumvents process, not because it is the only available tool.

Intercepting mechanisms (counter-case directions for beat 9):
The chapter-9 counter-case for the emergency-powers variant of
reverse scapegoating must locate a documented interception in
which courts successfully constrained an executive invocation of
wartime/emergency authority over individual liberty. Candidate
counter-cases (Loki initial scan; final selection at Shirley's
counter-case pass):
  - Boumediene v. Bush, 553 U.S. 723 (2008): the Supreme Court
    held that Guantanamo detainees had a constitutional right to
    habeas corpus, rejecting the government's claim that
    extraterritorial wartime detention was beyond Article III
    cognizance. Mechanism: judicial assertion of habeas reach.
    Limits: the Court has not always followed through on the
    Boumediene framework in subsequent cases.
  - Ex parte Endo, 323 U.S. 283 (1944): decided the same day as
    Korematsu; held that the government could not continue to
    detain a concededly loyal U.S. citizen of Japanese ancestry.
    Mechanism: judicial holding that even wartime power does not
    extend to detention without individuated finding of disloyalty.
    Limits: the holding was narrow and announced alongside
    Korematsu's broader (later repudiated) deference.
  - Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004): plurality holding
    that a U.S. citizen detained as an "enemy combatant" was
    entitled to due-process review. Mechanism: due-process
    floor under wartime detention. Limits: narrow framework;
    subsequent jurisprudence inconsistent.
  - Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952)
    (the steel-seizure case): the Supreme Court held that
    President Truman's emergency seizure of steel mills during
    the Korean War exceeded his constitutional and statutory
    authority. Mechanism: judicial limitation of asserted
    emergency executive power. Justice Jackson's concurrence
    establishing the three-zone framework remains the leading
    framework for emergency-powers analysis.
  - The German Internment redress litigation and 1988 Civil
    Liberties Act: not contemporaneous interception (the
    interception was 40+ years after the fact), but documents
    the absence of interception during WWII and the eventual
    redress mechanism. Useful as a limit case showing what
    happens when contemporaneous interception fails.
```
