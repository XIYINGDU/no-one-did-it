# Ford Pinto — Case File

Case name: Ford Motor Company 1971–1976 model-year Pinto fuel-tank
vulnerability; NHTSA defect investigation and Recall Campaign 78V-138;
*Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* product-liability judgment; *State of
Indiana v. Ford Motor Company* criminal homicide trial.
Domain: passenger-vehicle crashworthiness regulation under the National
Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act (49 U.S.C. ch. 301) and proposed
FMVSS 301 (Fuel System Integrity); California product-liability tort
including strict liability and punitive damages; Indiana criminal law
(reckless homicide under Indiana Code § 35-42-1-5 as enacted by P.L.
148-1976).
Dates and place: Ford Pinto subcompact in production from model year
1971 through model year 1980 at Ford's Dearborn, Michigan and other US
assembly facilities, with the fuel-tank vulnerability documented across
the 1971–1976 model years (the 1977 model year incorporated a partial
remediation; the 1978 recall covered 1971–1976 Pintos and 1975–1976
Mercury Bobcats). Key dates: original Pinto crash-test program inside
Ford circa 1969–1970; "Fatalities Associated with Crash-Induced Fuel
Leakage and Fires" internal Ford memorandum prepared circa 1973; Mark
Dowie, "Pinto Madness," *Mother Jones*, September/October 1977; NHTSA
defect investigation opened 1977 (DI-77-03); Ford recall announced
June 9, 1978 (Recall Campaign 78V-138, covering approximately 1.5
million vehicles); *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* tried in California
Superior Court, Orange County (Santa Ana), February 1978, jury
verdict February 6, 1978 ($2.5 million compensatory and $125 million
punitive damages; punitive reduced by trial court to $3.5 million);
California Court of Appeal published opinion, *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor
Co.* (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 757, May 29, 1981; *State of Indiana v.
Ford Motor Company* tried at Pulaski County Circuit Court, Winamac,
Indiana (the Ulrich case), Judge Harold R. Staffeldt presiding,
January–March 1980, jury verdict of acquittal returned March 13, 1980.
Manufacturer: Ford Motor Company, Dearborn, Michigan; Ford North
American Automotive Operations under President Lee A. Iacocca (Ford
president 1970–1978; left Ford July 13, 1978).
Case type: partial scapegoat
Hybrid note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`):
primary classification is **partial scapegoat** — Ford the corporation
was culpable at multiple levels of the design, knowledge, and
preventability chain, but the consequence structure stopped at the
corporation and partially at the product (the recalled fuel tank); the
named individual executives most responsible for the design and the
not-fix decision (including Iacocca) were not personally adjudicated
under either civil or criminal procedure. A secondary **system/object
alibi** layer fires at the level of the named cause — the "exploding
Pinto" became a reified blame container that absorbed the design
choice into a product-defect framing rather than a design-leadership
framing. The cost-benefit memo is the singular overlay: a primary
document in which the chain of control, knowledge, and preventability
is documented in the same artifact that quantifies the cost bearers.
This overlay is what makes Ford Pinto the older-echo demonstrator for
chapter 3 — every dimension of the eight-question diagnostic has a
primary-source answer, and the memo itself supplies the "knowledge"
dimension at uncommon clarity.

Classification note: Chapter 3 walks the eight-question diagnostic
through this case in parallel with VW Dieselgate. The two cases share
a deep structural rhyme: both involve a manufacturer that designed
around a regulatory cost (Pinto: proposed FMVSS 301 fuel-system
integrity standard, finalized 1976 with phased compliance; VW: US
Tier 2 Bin 5 NOx limits), made an internal decision that traded
human cost against unit cost, and watched the alibi reform across
multiple decades of subsequent corporate behavior. The differences
matter pedagogically: Ford Pinto is the **older, more crystalline**
demonstrator because (a) the cost-benefit memo monetized fatalities
in plain language, (b) civil and criminal interception both
occurred, (c) the public record is fully settled and 45+ years old.
Volkswagen Dieselgate is the **newer, more complex** demonstrator
because every laundering move is named in the modern procedural form
(EPA NoV, DOJ Statement of Facts, named indictments). Reading them
together teaches the reader that the diagnostic does not depend on
the technology, the era, or the regulatory architecture; it depends
only on whether the six chain dimensions can be filled.

```text
Crisis:
Between 1971 and 1978, occupants of Ford Pinto subcompact automobiles
struck from the rear at moderate closing speeds (impacts at the time
estimated at 20 to 30 mph in the literature) suffered fuel-tank
ruptures and post-collision fires in numbers that exceeded those of
comparable subcompacts. The vulnerability arose from the Pinto's
fuel-tank location aft of the rear axle and forward of the rear
bumper, in a crush zone that, on rear impact, could be penetrated
by exposed bolt heads on the differential housing or by the rear
bumper's deformation into the tank. The "Fatalities Associated with
Crash-Induced Fuel Leakage and Fires" memorandum, prepared inside
Ford circa 1973 and produced in *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.*
discovery, was Ford's own cost-benefit analysis of a per-vehicle
fuel-system fix; it quantified projected fatalities, projected serious
burn injuries, and projected vehicle burn losses against the cost of
the per-vehicle modification.

Documented harm under NHTSA's June 1978 defect-investigation summary
(reported in DI-77-03 closing materials and contemporaneous Federal
Register and Department of Transportation statements): 27 deaths
attributable to Pinto rear-impact fuel-system fires in the model
years covered by the recall. Higher figures circulated in advocacy
literature and in the Dowie *Mother Jones* piece (estimates from 500
to 900 deaths across the Pinto's production run). The peer-reviewed
academic re-examination by Gary T. Schwartz, "The Myth of the Ford
Pinto Case," 43 *Rutgers Law Review* 1013 (1991), argued that the
true death toll was closer to the NHTSA range than to the higher
advocacy estimates and that the Pinto's actual fire-fatality rate
was not statistically extraordinary against comparable subcompacts.
The chapter should state the range, not a point figure, and should
preserve both sources (see "Best counterargument" and "Open
questions"). The harm dimension stable across both sources: dozens
of preventable deaths and a documented higher number of disfiguring
burn injuries.

Official story:
Ford's external position across the 1971–1978 period framed the Pinto
as a vehicle that met all then-applicable federal safety standards.
This was technically true — FMVSS 301 (Fuel System Integrity) was
under rulemaking through the 1970s; the 30-mph rear-impact standard
that the Pinto's design did not reliably meet was finalized by NHTSA
in 1976 with phased compliance through model year 1977. Ford's
position was that the Pinto satisfied the law as written.

Three sub-stories ran in sequence:

1. **"The Pinto met the standards in effect at the time."** Ford's
   regulatory-compliance framing throughout the early 1970s.
   Technically correct as to the federal rule; silent on Ford's own
   internal crash-test results documenting the vulnerability and on
   the fact that competing subcompacts (notably the Chevrolet Vega,
   AMC Gremlin, and Toyota Corolla) had design choices that were
   less vulnerable to the same impact mode.
2. **"The driver caused the accident."** Ford's litigation posture in
   *Grimshaw* and in earlier product-liability cases emphasized
   third-party negligence (the rear-impacting driver) as the
   superseding cause of harm. This framing personalized blame onto
   the striking driver while the design vulnerability was treated
   as a passive feature of the crash environment.
3. **"This is not a safety defect; it is a design choice."** Ford's
   response to NHTSA's 1977–1978 defect investigation framed the
   fuel-tank location as an engineering trade-off rather than a
   safety defect. Ford resisted the recall determination through
   the spring of 1978, then announced the recall on June 9, 1978
   after NHTSA's preliminary determination of defect.

Each framing was undermined by the *Grimshaw* trial's introduction
of the internal cost-benefit memo into the public record. The memo
demonstrated that Ford had performed the calculation, knew the
projected fatality count, and had chosen against the per-vehicle
fix on cost grounds.

Blame container:
Three blame containers in sequence:

1. **The striking driver (1972–1978).** Across pre-Grimshaw product-
   liability litigation, Ford's posture emphasized the third-party
   driver as the proximate cause. This is the pure-scapegoat sub-
   move embedded inside the partial-scapegoat structure: the
   striking driver had real causal involvement (the impact) but
   the Pinto's design vulnerability converted ordinary collisions
   into fatal fires. The framing personalized blame at the lowest
   layer of the chain.
2. **The vehicle / the product (1977–1978 → 1981).** The
   "exploding Pinto" became, in popular coverage and in the
   recall-era language, the named cause. The car was reified as
   the agent of harm — "the Pinto explodes," "the Pinto kills" —
   in a system/object alibi sub-move that absorbed the design and
   approval decisions into a product-defect framing. The recall
   addressed the product without naming the executives who had
   approved the design and chosen against the fix.
3. **Ford the corporation (1978 onward).** *Grimshaw* delivered a
   $125 million punitive verdict (reduced) against Ford, and the
   California Court of Appeal's 1981 opinion (119 Cal.App.3d 757)
   sustained punitive liability on the ground that Ford acted in
   "conscious disregard" of safety. The corporation absorbed the
   civil judgment and the recall cost; the executives who made the
   design and approval choices did not face personal civil or
   criminal liability. Iacocca, Ford's president during Pinto
   development and the period when the cost-benefit memo was
   circulating, was never personally charged or sued. The Indiana
   criminal trial in 1980 charged Ford the corporation (the first
   US criminal homicide prosecution of an automobile manufacturer
   for a product defect); Ford was acquitted by the Pulaski
   County jury on March 13, 1980. The corporate-blame container
   thus absorbed civil punitive damages and the recall while
   shielding individual executives and ultimately surviving the
   criminal prosecution. [Evidence grade: A]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Ford Motor Company held design authority for the Pinto. The
  vehicle was developed under accelerated timelines in the late
  1960s (the program is widely reported to have been developed
  in approximately 25 months, against an industry norm of 43
  months, per *Grimshaw* trial testimony and contemporaneous
  trade reporting). The fuel-tank placement decision — aft of the
  rear axle, with limited rear-impact crush space — was a Ford
  engineering and packaging choice driven by the program's
  styling, trunk-space, and weight targets. The "Fatalities
  Associated with Crash-Induced Fuel Leakage and Fires" memo,
  prepared inside Ford circa 1973, was a Ford internal document
  performing a cost-benefit calculation on the fuel-system fix.
  Lee Iacocca, as Ford president (1970–1978), exercised
  ultimate program oversight authority during the Pinto's design
  and the period when the memo was prepared and circulated; his
  role is documented in trial testimony and in subsequent
  scholarship (Cullen et al., 1987, below). The chapter's
  control paragraph names Ford the corporation as the
  control-holder and notes Iacocca's program-oversight role
  per the trial record and Cullen et al.; individual
  engineering managers below the executive level have been
  identified in deposition records but were not named in any
  final indictment or judgment.

  NHTSA — the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration —
  held delegated regulatory authority over motor-vehicle safety
  standards under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety
  Act of 1966. NHTSA's role in the chain is contested: the
  agency had FMVSS 301 under rulemaking through the early 1970s
  and finalized the 30-mph rear-impact standard in 1976 with
  phased compliance. NHTSA opened the Pinto defect
  investigation (DI-77-03) in 1977 and reached a preliminary
  determination of defect in May 1978, leading to the June 9,
  1978 recall. The agency neither prevented the design (the
  rulemaking trailed the production decisions) nor caught the
  vulnerability before commercial sales; once the public-record
  evidence accumulated, NHTSA acted.

  Benefit:
  Ford Motor Company — through Pinto unit sales (approximately
  3 million Pintos produced across the program; the model was a
  successful market entry in the US subcompact segment in the
  early 1970s) at a price point sustained by the cost-
  containment program. The cost-benefit memo's $11-per-vehicle
  fix figure, applied across the production volume, is the
  unit-economics figure that the memo's $137 million side
  totals; Ford's revenue from the affected vehicles was several
  multiples of the avoided-fix cost. Executives whose
  compensation tracked program performance benefited; Iacocca's
  career trajectory through the Pinto period reflected program
  success. After Iacocca's departure from Ford in July 1978, he
  joined Chrysler Corporation in November 1978 and became one
  of the most prominent US automotive executives of the
  subsequent decade; the Pinto recall did not impair his
  career.

  Knowledge:
  Documented inside Ford across multiple levels:
  - Pre-production crash testing (circa 1969–1971): Ford's own
    rear-impact crash tests on Pinto prototypes documented
    fuel-tank rupture at moderate closing speeds. The crash-test
    records were introduced in *Grimshaw* discovery and are
    described in the California Court of Appeal opinion at 119
    Cal.App.3d at 777–778: "Mr. Copp's [Harley Copp, a former
    Ford engineer-witness for the plaintiffs] testimony was that
    crash tests revealed that the Pinto's fuel system as designed
    could not meet the 20-mile-per-hour proposed standard."
  - Circa 1973: "Fatalities Associated with Crash-Induced Fuel
    Leakage and Fires" memorandum prepared inside Ford. The memo
    monetized projected fatalities, serious burn injuries, and
    vehicle losses against the per-vehicle fix cost. The widely
    cited figures from the memo — 180 burn deaths, 180 serious
    burn injuries, 2,100 burned vehicles annually across the US
    light-vehicle fleet, $200,000 per fatality, $67,000 per
    serious burn injury, $700 per burned vehicle, $137 million
    benefit, against $11 per vehicle × 12.5 million vehicles =
    $137 million cost; net benefit of fix near zero or slightly
    negative — are reproduced in *Grimshaw* trial exhibits and
    in the *Mother Jones* (Dowie 1977) and Cullen et al. (1987)
    accounts. Important clarification preserved in the
    Schwartz (1991) analysis: the memo's fatality figures were
    NHTSA-influenced regulatory cost-benefit numbers prepared
    in the context of fleet-wide rollover and fuel-system
    rulemaking, not Ford's internal Pinto-specific projection.
    The chapter must preserve this nuance — the memo IS a
    cost-benefit-against-fatalities document, AND it is not the
    secret Pinto-only calculation that some popularizations
    have described. Both characterizations have appeared in the
    literature; both are partially correct.
  - 1971–1976: documented Ford-internal awareness of the fuel-
    tank vulnerability across multiple model-year refreshes.
    Modest design changes were considered and rejected on cost
    grounds in this period, per trial testimony.

  Preventability:
  Multiple preventability points:
  - At Pinto design freeze (circa 1969–1970): choose a fuel-
    tank location forward of the rear axle (the Volkswagen,
    BMC Mini, and various other small-car practices of the era)
    rather than aft. Ford's engineering team considered the
    alternative; it was rejected on packaging and trunk-space
    grounds.
  - At pre-production crash testing (circa 1970–1971): redesign
    the fuel-system protection in response to the rupture
    results. The Court of Appeal opinion describes the in-house
    crash-test results that documented the vulnerability.
  - Across each model-year refresh (1972, 1973, 1974, 1975,
    1976): incorporate the $11-per-vehicle modification (a
    plastic shield over the differential bolt heads, plus
    rerouting of fuel-system components). The fix was
    technically modest; Ford engineers identified it; cost-
    benefit analysis recommended against.
  - In May 1978 after NHTSA's preliminary determination of
    defect: announce a recall promptly rather than negotiate.
    Ford's June 9, 1978 recall came after public pressure
    intensified following the *Mother Jones* coverage and the
    *Grimshaw* verdict.

  Record controller:
  Multiple layers, with the *Grimshaw* civil docket as the
  primary record-control venue (in contrast to Bhopal's
  multi-jurisdictional fragmentation):
  - Ford Motor Company internal engineering and management
    archives, Dearborn. The Pinto crash-test records, design-
    review minutes, and the cost-benefit memo entered the
    public record through *Grimshaw* discovery and the
    subsequent appellate opinion.
  - California Superior Court, Orange County (Santa Ana) —
    docket for *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* (Case No.
    197761) trial record, February 1978.
  - California Court of Appeal, Fourth District, Division Two
    — *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 757,
    174 Cal.Rptr. 348, published opinion May 29, 1981.
    California Supreme Court denied review September 16, 1981.
    The published appellate opinion is the load-bearing
    record-control document.
  - National Highway Traffic Safety Administration — defect
    investigation DI-77-03 file; Recall Campaign 78V-138
    (June 9, 1978); Federal Register notices.
  - Pulaski County Circuit Court, Winamac, Indiana — *State of
    Indiana v. Ford Motor Company* docket, January–March 1980.
    Judge Harold R. Staffeldt; jury verdict March 13, 1980.
  - *Mother Jones* magazine — Dowie's "Pinto Madness," September
    /October 1977. This is journalism's record-control
    contribution and was a primary driver of public attention
    that preceded the NHTSA recall determination.
  - University of Michigan Law School and Harvard Law School
    case-method materials — the Pinto became a teaching case in
    product-liability law and business ethics, generating an
    extensive secondary literature including the Cullen,
    Maakestad, and Cavender study of the Indiana prosecution.
  Critically, the *Grimshaw* appellate opinion is publicly
  available and has been the most-cited document in the
  scholarly and legal record-control layer. Unlike Bhopal —
  where UCC archives remained in private custody — the Pinto
  record entered the public legal record through a successful
  product-liability action and a published appellate opinion.
  This is a load-bearing reason the case teaches the diagnostic
  cleanly for chapter 3.

  Cost bearer:
  Two layers:
  1. **Pinto occupants killed or burned in rear-impact fires.**
     The NHTSA defect-investigation summary placed attributable
     deaths at 27 across the model years covered by the recall
     (1971–1976 Pintos and 1975–1976 Mercury Bobcats).
     Contemporary advocacy estimates (Dowie 1977 and later
     popular accounts) put the figure at 500–900. Schwartz
     (1991) argued for the lower end, contending that the
     Pinto's fire-fatality rate was not statistically
     extraordinary compared to other small cars of the period.
     The chapter should state the range with the methodology
     cited: NHTSA defect-investigation count = 27; advocacy and
     popular accounts = 500 to 900; peer-reviewed academic
     re-examination supports the lower NHTSA figure as the
     defensible attribution. Separately, the count of
     disfiguring burn injuries (named *Grimshaw* plaintiff
     Richard Grimshaw was 13 years old at the time of his 1972
     accident and suffered burns over 90% of his body, losing
     his nose, left ear, and most fingers — the *Grimshaw*
     verdict was on his individual injury) is documented at
     levels several multiples of the death count.
  2. **The driver class population, more broadly.** The
     Ford-internal cost-benefit memo's fatality figures (180
     burn deaths annually across the US fleet) implied a
     cost-bearer population larger than any specific Pinto
     subset. The chapter's cost-bearer paragraph should note
     that the memo's monetization framework treated this
     population as an actuarial cost line, not as a
     responsibility object.

How the alibi hardened:
1. **Compliance-with-the-rule framing.** The Pinto satisfied
   federal motor-vehicle safety standards as enacted at each
   model year. FMVSS 301's 30-mph rear-impact provision was not
   finalized until 1976 with phased compliance through model
   year 1977. Ford's external posture was "we comply with the
   law"; the chapter must preserve this as a true statement that
   was deployed as an alibi for design choices that Ford's own
   crash testing had identified as dangerous.
2. **Cost-benefit calculation as deniability artifact.** The
   "Fatalities Associated with Crash-Induced Fuel Leakage and
   Fires" memo was, in its origin, an internal regulatory cost-
   benefit document prepared in the context of NHTSA's rulemaking
   on rollover and fuel-system standards (per Schwartz 1991).
   Ford's pre-Grimshaw deployment of the document framing was
   that the memo represented "ordinary regulatory cost-benefit
   analysis." The memo's existence as a procedural artifact gave
   Ford's not-fix decision the appearance of analytical
   discipline.
3. **Recall-as-resolution.** Ford's June 9, 1978 recall (Recall
   Campaign 78V-138) covered 1.5 million Pintos and Mercury
   Bobcats and was framed by Ford as a comprehensive remediation
   of the product defect. The recall did not name the design
   leaders, did not require Ford to acknowledge the cost-benefit
   choice, and did not address the executives who had approved
   the not-fix decisions across the 1971–1976 model years.
4. **Indiana acquittal as exoneration narrative.** The March 13,
   1980 acquittal of Ford in *State of Indiana v. Ford Motor
   Company* was widely reported as a vindication. Ford's
   defense (led by James F. Neal of Neal & Harwell) successfully
   argued that the 1973 Ulrich-vehicle Pinto pre-dated the 1977
   model-year improvements and that Ford had acted reasonably
   under the standards of the time. The acquittal narrative
   reinforced the corporate-shield layer of the alibi.
5. **Reframing in popular memory.** Across the 1980s, the
   Pinto became shorthand for "exploding car" in popular
   imagination. The product-as-cause framing persisted and
   absorbed the design-leadership question. Iacocca's 1984
   autobiography (*Iacocca: An Autobiography*, co-written with
   William Novak) addressed the Pinto only briefly; his role in
   the program's cost-target architecture was not personalized
   in popular accounts.

How the alibi weakened:
1. **Mark Dowie, "Pinto Madness," *Mother Jones***, September
   /October 1977. Dowie's investigative piece, drawing on
   leaked Ford documents (including the cost-benefit memo) and
   on Ford-internal whistleblower testimony, was the public
   journalism interception. The piece named Iacocca, named the
   $11-per-vehicle fix, and quantified the fatalities-per-cost
   calculation in language that broke from the prior trade-
   press framing. The piece is the precipitating public-pressure
   event that accelerated NHTSA's defect investigation and that
   ran in parallel with the *Grimshaw* trial. This is journalism
   functioning as record-control interception (per
   `.claude/skills/counter-case-method/SKILL.md` interception
   type 3, journalism).
2. **NHTSA defect investigation DI-77-03.** NHTSA's 1977 opening
   of the formal defect investigation, the preliminary
   determination of defect in May 1978, and the resulting
   recall (78V-138) on June 9, 1978 converted the journalistic
   record into a regulatory record-control document. NHTSA's
   role here is the regulator-enforcement interception type.
3. **Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co. trial verdict (February 6,
   1978).** The Santa Ana jury's verdict of $2.5 million
   compensatory and $125 million punitive damages was the
   largest punitive damages award in US product-liability
   history at the time. The trial court reduced punitive
   damages to $3.5 million on Ford's post-trial motion. The
   civil-litigation interception type (court — court findings
   pierce the system/object alibi) operated here at full
   strength.
4. **Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co. appellate opinion, 119
   Cal.App.3d 757 (May 29, 1981).** The California Court of
   Appeal's published opinion sustained both compensatory and
   reduced-punitive damages and articulated the "conscious
   disregard" standard for punitive liability in design-defect
   cases. The opinion's recitation of Ford's internal crash-
   test results and the Copp testimony placed the design-
   knowledge chain in the public record at appellate-court
   weight. The California Supreme Court denied review on
   September 16, 1981. The opinion is the load-bearing
   appellate primary source for the case.
5. **State of Indiana v. Ford Motor Company trial record
   (January–March 1980).** Although Ford was acquitted, the
   trial was the first US criminal homicide prosecution of an
   auto manufacturer for a product defect. The trial record —
   including expert testimony on the Pinto's design and the
   prosecution's introduction of Ford internal documents —
   contributed to the public-record corpus. Cullen, Maakestad,
   and Cavender's *Corporate Crime Under Attack: The Ford
   Pinto Case and Beyond* (Anderson Publishing, 1987) is the
   definitive academic account of the Indiana prosecution; the
   case is taught in US business-ethics and product-liability
   curricula.
6. **Federal regulatory tightening.** FMVSS 301 (Fuel System
   Integrity) was strengthened across the late 1970s. Subsequent
   automotive design practice incorporated fuel-tank protection
   as a load-bearing crashworthiness consideration; the Pinto
   case is widely credited as a precipitating event in the
   shift toward integrated crash-safety design.
7. **Gary T. Schwartz, "The Myth of the Ford Pinto Case," 43
   *Rutgers L. Rev.* 1013 (1991).** Schwartz's peer-reviewed
   law-review article, while sometimes characterized as
   exculpatory of Ford, is best read as a precision-improvement
   on the popular account: it corrected several elements of the
   Dowie/Cullen narrative (notably the absolute fatality count
   and the framing of the cost-benefit memo) while sustaining
   the core finding that Ford's design and approval choices
   could and should have prevented foreseeable harm. The
   chapter MUST engage Schwartz — citing only the popular
   account or only the academic re-examination would each
   weaken the chapter's evidence discipline. Both are surviving
   sources; both belong in the source ledger.

Best counterargument:
"Ford Pinto is the textbook product-liability success story,
not a laundering case. Civil liability succeeded — Grimshaw
delivered the largest punitive verdict in US history at the
time, the appellate opinion publicly named the design failures,
NHTSA recalled the vehicles, FMVSS 301 was strengthened, the
case is taught in every product-liability course in US law
schools. The cost-benefit memo entered the public record, was
used against Ford, and became a paradigm of what corporate
internal calculation should not do. Calling this 'partial
scapegoat' overstates the laundering: accountability did climb
to the corporation, the design choices were named in court, and
the regulatory architecture was reformed in response. The
chapter's framing risks valorizing the popular Dowie narrative
over Schwartz's more careful academic analysis."

Shirley's response: the counterargument is at its strongest at
the *Grimshaw* appellate stage (1981) and at the FMVSS 301
reform stage (late 1970s through 1980s). It is at its weakest
on the named-individual layer. The diagnostic question is not
"did the corporation pay?" — the corporation paid. The
diagnostic question is "did responsibility follow control,
benefit, knowledge, and preventability?" The chain of design
authority for the Pinto ran through named Ford engineers,
named program managers, and named executives including Lee
Iacocca, who as Ford president oversaw the program. None of
those named individuals faced personal civil or criminal
liability for the design or for the not-fix decision. The
corporation absorbed the civil punitive damages and the
recall; the corporation was acquitted on the criminal
charge in Indiana. The cost-benefit memo, in its origin a
regulatory document, became the artifact that named the
not-fix decision but did not name the deciders. The
classification is therefore **partial scapegoat (primary):
Ford the corporation as named blame container, the design-
leadership chain partially protected** + **system/object
alibi (secondary): "the exploding Pinto" as reified cause**.
The cost-benefit overlay is what makes the case load-bearing
for chapter 3 — it documents the chain at uncommon clarity
within a single primary artifact. On the Schwartz challenge:
the chapter engages Schwartz directly. The fatality range
(27 NHTSA versus 500–900 advocacy) is preserved as a range
with methodology cited. The memo's framing (regulatory cost-

sustaining of the core finding (that Ford's design choices
could and should have prevented foreseeable harm) is the
chapter's anchor on the case's substance.

What would responsibility look like if it followed control
instead of visibility?
Responsibility would attach to the Ford design-program
leaders who approved the fuel-tank location at the 1969–1970
design freeze; to the engineering managers who reviewed the
1970–1971 crash-test results and approved production
nonetheless; to the executives who approved the cost-benefit

  - Partial reversal at the corporate civil-liability level:
    Ford paid civil damages, recalled vehicles, and the
    appellate record permanently named the design and
    approval failures.
  - No criminal reversal: Indiana acquittal stood; no
    subsequent criminal prosecution of Ford or its executives.
  - Recognition without remedy at the named-individual level:
    the named executives faced no personal adjudication and
    no career consequence.
  - Regulatory tightening at the FMVSS 301 layer: a partial
    procedural remedy that does not undo the harm but
    constrains the next iteration.

  Transferable lesson (diagnostic, not motivational):
  When a manufacturer's internal cost-benefit calculation
  monetizes human lives against unit fix costs and chooses
  against the fix, the public-record interception path that
  has historically succeeded is the civil-discovery + named-
  appellate-opinion route. The criminal-procedure path has
  historically failed for corporate defendants under
  contemporary recklessness standards. The lesson generalizes
  to any case where (a) an internal calculation document
  monetizes harm, (b) the manufacturer can frame compliance
  with then-applicable standards as a complete defense, and
  (c) named individuals' authority over the not-fix decision
  is shielded by the corporate form. For chapter 3, this is
  the reader-portable form of the diagnostic's "knowledge"
  and "preventability" questions: if the internal calculation
  document existed, what does it say, and who signed it?

  Limits of the lesson:
  - The *Grimshaw* path required a catastrophic individual
    injury (Richard Grimshaw, 90% body burns at age 13), a
    plaintiff who could survive litigation, and counsel
    capable of obtaining the internal documents through
    discovery. Most cost-bearer populations cannot meet all
    three conditions.
  - The cost-benefit memo's existence as a discoverable
    document was a contingent feature of Ford's regulatory-
    response practice. Post-Pinto corporate practice has
    shifted toward less-documented decision channels. The
    diagnostic is at its strongest when the calculation is
    documented; the diagnostic still works against
    undocumented calculations but with weaker evidence.
  - The criminal-path failure (Indiana acquittal) shows that
    interception can be partial even when both civil and
    criminal venues attempt it. The chapter should not over-
    promise the interception mechanism's success rate.
```
