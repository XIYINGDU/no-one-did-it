# January 6 Mass Pardons and DOJ Record-Scrubbing — Case File

Case: Presidential mass pardon of January 6 defendants (Proclamation 10887, Jan 20, 2025) plus subsequent DOJ pattern of dismissal, case-file removal, and prosecutor termination.
Domain: federal executive clemency power (U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 1); DOJ prosecutorial independence and recordkeeping; federal personnel law (5 U.S.C. § 2302); Privacy Act and FOIA implications of removing case files.
Dates/place: Proclamation issued Washington, D.C., January 20, 2025 (first day of second Trump term). Pardons covered approximately 1,500 individuals charged or convicted in connection with the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack, plus 14 named sentence commutations to time served (Oath Keepers and Proud Boys leadership). Subsequent DOJ actions through 2025–2026.
Case type: system/object alibi
Secondary case type: pure scapegoat

Hybrid classification note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`, explicit hybrid declaration). The laundering operates in two stacked layers. (1) Primary — system/object alibi: the DOJ-as-institution is reified as the wrongful actor ("weaponized DOJ"); the institution absorbs blame so that no specific charging or trial decision needs defending on its merits. The named cause is an agency, treated as having "decided" and "persecuted" — the classic system/object-alibi structure with a procedural shell (DOJ) as the named villain. (2) Secondary — pure scapegoat: career line prosecutors who worked the January 6 docket are documented to have been terminated or reassigned; they are substantially innocent of the wrong they are functionally being punished for (executing lawful charging decisions under the prior administration). The chapter-9 *move* this case teaches is **reverse scapegoating** — the rhetorical inversion in which accountability is recast as persecution. "Reverse scapegoating" is the descriptor of how the alibi is constructed, not a fifth taxonomy label; the taxonomy itself remains the four canonical categories.

```text
Crisis:
On January 6, 2021, a crowd attacked the United States Capitol while a
joint session of Congress was certifying the 2020 electoral vote. 138
Metropolitan Police Department and U.S. Capitol Police officers were
injured (per MPD and USCP post-incident reports). One officer, Brian
Sicknick, died of strokes the following day (D.C. Office of the Chief
Medical Examiner ruling, April 2021). Four officers who responded that
day later died by suicide (Howard Liebengood, Jeffrey Smith, Gunther
Hashida, Kyle DeFreytag — public records and family statements; the
House Select Committee final report (Dec 2022) treats the four suicides
as deaths "in connection with" Jan 6 without legal causation finding).

The Department of Justice opened what became the largest federal criminal
investigation in U.S. history. By the end of 2024, the U.S. Attorney for
the District of Columbia had charged approximately 1,583 defendants;
roughly 1,270 had been convicted (per DOJ U.S. Attorney's Office for the
District of Columbia public dashboards, archived; final pre-pardon
tallies vary by source, B-grade for exact counts, A-grade for the order
of magnitude). Charged offenses ranged from misdemeanor parading
(40 U.S.C. § 5104(e)(2)(G)) to seditious conspiracy (18 U.S.C. § 2384,
under which Oath Keepers leader Stewart Rhodes and four co-defendants
were convicted) and assault on a federal officer (18 U.S.C. § 111).

On January 20, 2025, hours after the inauguration, the President signed
Proclamation 10887 ("Granting Pardons and Commutation of Sentences for
Certain Offenses Relating to the Events at or Near the United States
Capitol on January 6, 2021"). The proclamation: (a) commuted to time
served the sentences of 14 named individuals (including Stewart Rhodes,
Kelly Meggs, Kenneth Harrelson, Thomas Caldwell, Jessica Watkins, and
Roberto Minuta of the Oath Keepers; and Ethan Nordean, Joseph Biggs,
Zachary Rehl, Dominic Pezzola, and others of the Proud Boys); and
(b) granted "a full, complete and unconditional pardon to all other
individuals convicted of offenses related to events that occurred at or
near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021." DOJ subsequently
moved to dismiss pending Jan 6 prosecutions; the U.S. Attorney's
Office for D.C. has been documented by reporting (Reuters, WaPo, NYT)
to have removed Jan 6 case-tracking dashboards from public-facing pages
and terminated or reassigned career prosecutors who had worked the
docket.

Official story (chapter-9 reverse-scapegoating frame, drawn from
on-the-record proclamation and statements):
Proclamation 10887 itself recites: "This proclamation ends a grave
national injustice that has been perpetrated upon the American people
over the last four years and begins a process of national
reconciliation." Public statements from the President and senior
administration officials at signing and subsequently characterized the
defendants as "hostages" and described the prosecutions as politically
motivated. The framing recasts:
  - the prosecutors as persecutors;
  - the convictions as political imprisonment;
  - the DOJ-as-institution as the bad actor;
  - the President (as pardoner) as the goat-bearing reformer
    correcting an injustice.

Blame container:
Two blame containers operate simultaneously. (A) The institutional
container: "the weaponized DOJ" / "the political prosecutions" as
diffuse villain. (B) The personnel container: career line prosecutors
publicly named or implicitly named (through firings and reassignments)
as the human face of the institutional villain. Both containers absorb
the moral charge so that the substantive question — were the underlying
convictions lawful and supported by evidence — is not engaged.

Actual responsibility chain (for the underlying Jan 6 conduct and for
the post-pardon DOJ actions, kept separate per `02-evidence-grades.md`):

A. Underlying Jan 6 conduct (the prosecuted conduct):
  1. Individual defendants whose convictions have been judicially
     established at the trial level for violent offenses (e.g., the
     seditious-conspiracy convictions of Rhodes and co-defendants;
     assault-on-officer convictions documented by jury verdicts and
     plea agreements in the U.S. District Court for D.C.).
  2. Organizers and conduct upstream of the Capitol breach — addressed
     by the House Select Committee final report (Dec 22, 2022) and
     DOJ Special Counsel Jack Smith's federal election-interference
     indictment in United States v. Trump, No. 23-cr-257 (D.D.C.,
     Aug 1, 2023). The case was dismissed without prejudice on
     Nov 25, 2024, on DOJ's motion citing OLC policy against the
     federal prosecution of a sitting president following the
     November 2024 election outcome — the dismissal was procedural,
     not on the merits.

B. Post-pardon DOJ actions (the reverse-scapegoating apparatus):
  1. The President: issued Proclamation 10887 (clemency power under
     U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 1).
  2. DOJ leadership (Attorney General and senior political appointees,
     post-January 2025): made or directed decisions to dismiss
     pending Jan 6 prosecutions; reorganized U.S. Attorney's Office
     for D.C.; reported personnel actions against career prosecutors.
  3. U.S. Attorney for D.C. (post-January 2025): operational direction
     of dismissals, case-file presentation, and personnel decisions
     in the office.

Control:
The clemency power is plenary as to federal offenses and was exercised
within constitutional authority. The post-pardon administrative actions
— dismissal of pending cases, removal of case-tracking dashboards,
prosecutor terminations — are political-appointee decisions within
DOJ; control rests with the Attorney General and U.S. Attorney for
D.C. The substantive scope of the dismissals (which specific pending
cases, on what authority) and the personnel actions (which prosecutors,
for what stated reason) are documented through DOJ filings and court
dockets; the institutional rationale for case-file removal from public
dashboards is documented through reporting (B-grade) pending FOIA
production.

Benefit:
Three distinct beneficiaries operate at different layers:
  - Pardoned defendants: legal benefit (release, vacatur of supervision,
    relief from collateral consequences).
  - Political principal: rhetorical benefit (the proclamation's framing
    consolidates the "political-persecution" narrative used through the
    2024 campaign; recasts the pardoner as goat-bearing reformer).
  - DOJ leadership and aligned political appointees: institutional
    benefit (control of the docket; reduced visibility of conduct the
    new administration treats as politically inconvenient).

Knowledge:
The President exercised clemency with documentary knowledge of the
underlying conduct: the proclamation itself was preceded by judicial
findings, jury verdicts, the House Select Committee final report
(public Dec 2022), and the public record of the attack. The pardon
includes individuals convicted of assault on Capitol Police officers
— the public record of those convictions (per case dockets in the
U.S. District Court for D.C.) is unambiguous. (Knowledge of the
underlying conduct does not constrain the clemency power, but it
constrains the chapter's narrative: the proclamation cannot be
described as ignorant of the violent record.)

Preventability:
The reverse-scapegoating dynamic is preventable, structurally, in three
ways the chapter must name:
  - Independent record durability of the underlying convictions
    (vacatur language matters: a pardon does not vacate the
    underlying fact-findings; the trial records remain).
  - Federal records preservation: Federal Records Act 44 U.S.C.
    §§ 3101–3107 and DOJ recordkeeping policy require that the
    operational record (prosecutor work product, charging memos,
    closing memos) be preserved even when public-facing dashboards
    are removed.
  - Whistleblower protections for terminated career prosecutors
    (5 U.S.C. § 2302(b)(8) prohibits personnel actions taken in
    reprisal for protected disclosures; cases will turn on the
    documented rationale for each termination).

Record controller:
Three overlapping record controllers:
  - DOJ, U.S. Attorney's Office for D.C., and Executive Office for
    U.S. Attorneys (EOUSA): hold case files, prosecutor work product,
    charging records, dismissal motions.
  - Federal courts (PACER, D.D.C. and D.C. Circuit dockets): hold
    judicial records — these are the durable, non-DOJ-controlled
    record of the underlying convictions. The pardons do not vacate
    the underlying judicial record.
  - National Archives and Records Administration (NARA): receives
    federal records under the FRA; CREW, American Oversight,
    National Security Archive, and others are litigating preservation
    questions (see Sources).

Cost bearer:
Multiple cost bearers, in three categories:
  - Capitol Police and Metropolitan Police officers and their families:
    bear ongoing moral and reputational cost of the framing that
    recasts their assailants as "hostages." Documented statements
    from Sgt. Aquilino Gonell (ret.), Officer Daniel Hodges, Officer
    Michael Fanone (ret.), and Officer Harry Dunn (ret.), inter alia,
    on the record across 2025 reporting; surviving families of the
    deceased officers carry continuing reputational impact.
  - Career line prosecutors who worked the January 6 docket: those
    documented to have been terminated, reassigned, or pressured to
    resign absorb individual professional and reputational cost.
    Reporting in 2025 identifies several such cases by name and by
    pseudonym; civil-service appeals and Office of Special Counsel
    complaints have been filed in some matters [EVIDENCE NEEDED:

  - Future federal prosecutors and DOJ career staff: bear the
    durable cost of the precedent — the chilling effect on charging
    decisions involving conduct connected to a current or future
    administration's political allies.
  - Civic record: the broader cost — bipartisan acceptance of the
    underlying judicial record of January 6 — was, per reporting and
    polling through 2024–2026, partially absorbed before the
    proclamation and continues to be eroded by it.

How the alibi hardened:
  - The "weaponized DOJ" framing was established in campaign
    rhetoric across 2023–2024 before the pardons issued; the
    proclamation lands into an already-prepared narrative slot.
  - The proclamation's own reconciliation language ("a grave
    national injustice") states the inversion in the operative
    document — not in commentary about it.
  - Mass-scale pardoning (vs. selective) prevents individuated
    moral assessment: 1,500+ pardons treated as a single act
    obscures the difference between misdemeanor trespass and
    assault on officers.
  - Removal of DOJ case-tracking dashboards from public-facing
    pages reduces the public's ability to retrieve individual
    case facts.
  - Prosecutor terminations and reassignments produce silence
    where prior-administration prosecutorial expertise might
    have publicly contradicted the new framing.

How the alibi weakens (record points pressing on the inversion):
  - The federal court dockets remain. Trial records, jury
    verdicts, sentencing transcripts, and judicial opinions in
    D.D.C. are durable through PACER and CourtListener regardless
    of DOJ's dashboard decisions.
  - Officer testimony already exists on the record: House Select
    Committee hearings June–Oct 2022; trial testimony in U.S. v.
    Rhodes et al. and parallel prosecutions; published officer
    memoirs.
  - Judicial opinions in the Jan 6 docket — particularly Judge
    Amit Mehta's findings in U.S. v. Rhodes and Judge Tim Kelly's
    findings in U.S. v. Nordean et al. — record the seditious
    conspiracy findings that no clemency act can vacate.
  - CREW, American Oversight, and National Security Archive
    records-preservation litigation (see DOGE / records case file)
    is pressing the FRA boundary on dashboard removal and
    file-retention.
  - Affected officers' continuing public-record statements; the
    Capitol Police Labor Committee statement on the proclamation
    (January 21, 2025).
  - Whistleblower complaints by terminated career prosecutors
    (status: pending in OSC and MSPB venues as of 2026-05-26).

Best counterargument:
The strongest defense of the proclamation as a matter of constitutional
authority is unimpeachable: the clemency power is plenary as to federal
offenses and the President's authority to pardon, including pre-trial,
is established by Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333 (1866), and
not narrowed by Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79 (1915), in the
relevant respects. A scholarly defense (Mark Osler, Margaret Love, and
other clemency scholars) further argues that mass clemency has historical
precedent (Carter's 1977 Vietnam draft amnesty; Lincoln-Johnson Civil
War clemency). The book's response is not that the clemency power was
exceeded — that argument loses on text and precedent. The book's
response is to keep two questions separate: (a) was the act lawful
(yes, as to clemency); (b) does the act effect responsibility
laundering by recasting accountability as persecution (the chapter's
diagnostic question — answered by the chain above, not by the
constitutional question). The proclamation's full lawfulness is the
chapter's most uncomfortable fact and the one it must concede
explicitly; the diagnostic stands independent of it.

Second counterargument the chapter must address: many of the pardoned
were convicted of non-violent misdemeanors; clemency at scale for
non-violent offenders is defensible separate from the violent-offender
subset. Response: the proclamation does not distinguish; the chapter
must distinguish. The 14 named commutations are the violent core
(seditious conspiracy convictions); the chapter must keep that subset
separate from the misdemeanor population in any aggregate description.

Third counterargument: the subsequent personnel actions and dashboard
removals reflect ordinary post-transition reorganization, not
retaliation. Response: distinguishable case-by-case; whistleblower
proceedings will establish the documented rationale; the chapter
treats these claims at procedural-stage wording until OSC/MSPB findings
issue.

    legislative codification; the lesson is partial.
  - South Korean prosecutions of former presidents Chun Doo-hwan and
    Roh Tae-woo (1996) — multiple former heads of state convicted,
    then pardoned by successor. Interception was prosecutorial
    persistence over decades.
  - Italian "Mani Pulite" investigations and the eventual political
    return of investigated figures — partial-interception case;
    teaches limits more than escape.
```
