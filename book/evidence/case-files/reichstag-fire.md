# Reichstag Fire — Case File

Case name: The Reichstag Fire and the criminal prosecution of Marinus
van der Lubbe (the Reichstag Fire Trial, *Reichsgericht*, Leipzig,
21 September – 23 December 1933).
Domain: political history / criminal law / regime consolidation
Dates and place: Fire: night of 27 February 1933, Reichstag building,
Berlin. Reichstag Fire Decree
(*Verordnung des Reichspräsidenten zum Schutz von Volk und Staat*):
28 February 1933, signed by President Paul von Hindenburg, Chancellor
Adolf Hitler, Interior Minister Wilhelm Frick, Justice Minister Franz
Gürtner. Enabling Act
(*Gesetz zur Behebung der Not von Volk und Reich*): 23 March 1933.
Reichstag Fire Trial: 21 September – 23 December 1933,
*Reichsgericht* (Reich Supreme Court), Leipzig. Van der Lubbe's
execution by guillotine: 10 January 1934, Leipzig. Posthumous
annulment of conviction by the Federal Prosecutor General of Germany
(*Generalbundesanwalt*): announced January 2008, applying the 1998
Gesetz zur Aufhebung nationalsozialistischer Unrechtsurteile in der
Strafrechtspflege (NS-AufhG).
Case type: pure scapegoat
Secondary case type (hybrid): system/object alibi — the alibi-object
operates at the political-narrative level. The fire itself is reified
as a Communist plot against the state, with van der Lubbe serving as
the human face of an alleged conspiracy that did not factually exist.
Hybrid case, named as such: **primary pure scapegoat / secondary
system/object alibi**. See "Best counterargument" for the hybrid
decision record.

```text
Crisis:
On the night of 27 February 1933, fire broke out in the Reichstag
building in Berlin shortly after 21:00. The fire spread through the
plenary chamber and gutted much of the interior before being brought
under control. Marinus van der Lubbe, a 24-year-old Dutch council
communist and former bricklayer with poor eyesight, was apprehended
inside the building by police shortly after the fire was discovered.
He was found bare-chested, carrying matches and firelighters, and
confessed at the scene to setting the fire as a protest act intended
to rouse the German working class against the Nazi government, which
had been in office for less than four weeks (Hitler appointed
Chancellor 30 January 1933).

The fire occurred six days before the scheduled 5 March 1933
Reichstag elections.

Official story:
The Nazi government, within hours of the fire, framed the act as a
signal for a planned Communist (KPD) uprising. The official story
had three load-bearing claims:
1. Van der Lubbe was an agent of, or coordinated with, the Communist
   Party of Germany (KPD) and the Communist International (Comintern).
2. The KPD had been planning insurrection; the fire was a signal.
3. Emergency state action — including suspension of civil liberties
   — was necessary to prevent the uprising.

The same morning, the Prussian Interior Ministry (under Hermann
Göring as Reich Commissioner for the Prussian Interior) ordered
mass arrests of KPD officials, deputies, and members. By dawn of
28 February, approximately 4,000 Communists had been arrested in
Prussia alone. The Reichstag Fire Decree was promulgated that same
day.

Four communist defendants were subsequently arrested and joined to
the prosecution: Ernst Torgler (KPD parliamentary leader, who
turned himself in to clear his name), and three Bulgarians — Georgi
Dimitrov, Blagoi Popov, and Vasil Tanev — who had been in Berlin on
Comintern business.

Blame container:
At the individual level: van der Lubbe, who was tried, convicted,
and executed. At the collective level: the Communist Party of
Germany, which the official story named as the conspiracy behind
the fire. Van der Lubbe's individual confession was used as the
human-scale anchor for the collective accusation; the collective
accusation was used as the rationale for the Reichstag Fire Decree
and, six weeks later, the Enabling Act.

The blame container has a two-layer architecture:
- Outer container (the alibi-object): "the Communist conspiracy" —
  a reified collective actor used to justify the constitutional
  emergency.
- Inner container (the human face): van der Lubbe — physically
  present at the scene, confessed, executable.
The conviction of van der Lubbe (and the acquittal of the four
co-defendants) at the *Reichsgericht* in December 1933 created the
durable record on which both layers rested for the remainder of the
Third Reich.

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  The Nazi state apparatus, in office for four weeks but already
  controlling the Prussian Interior Ministry (Göring) and the Reich
  Chancellery (Hitler) and the Reich Ministry of the Interior
  (Frick). Control over the criminal investigation passed within
  hours to the Prussian political police (Section IA, predecessor
  of the Gestapo) under Rudolf Diels. The investigation, prosecution,
  and propaganda framing were not separated from the political
  leadership that benefited from the framing.
  Control over the legislative response (the 28 February decree, the
  23 March Enabling Act) sat with Hitler, Frick, and the Reich
  Cabinet, with Hindenburg's signature. The Reichstag presidency
  (Göring) controlled the parliamentary procedure under which the
  Enabling Act passed (by physically excluding the arrested KPD
  deputies from the count, and intimidating SPD deputies in the
  Kroll Opera House venue on 23 March).

  Benefit:
  The Nazi government was the unambiguous beneficiary.
  Within 24 hours of the fire it obtained:
  - Suspension by decree of articles 114, 115, 117, 118, 123, 124,
    and 153 of the Weimar Constitution (personal liberty, freedom
    of expression, freedom of the press, freedom of association,
    privacy of postal and telephonic communication, inviolability
    of property and the home).
  - Authority for Reich-level intervention in the *Länder* (federal
    states).
  - A legal basis for the preventive detention of political
    opponents — beginning with the KPD and extending in weeks to
    SPD, trade union, and other figures.
  Within four weeks: the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933, which
  conferred legislative power on the Reich Cabinet for four years
  and authorised cabinet decrees that deviated from the
  Constitution.
  Within seventeen months: the consolidation of the Hitler regime,
  including the dissolution of all non-Nazi parties (July 1933),
  the "Night of the Long Knives" (30 June 1934), and on Hindenburg's
  death (2 August 1934) the merger of the chancellorship and
  presidency.

  Knowledge:
  This is the historiographically contested dimension. Two
  scholarly positions, both internally coherent:

  Position A — "van der Lubbe alone, Nazis opportunistically seized
  the moment." Sven Felix Kellerhoff, *Der Reichstagsbrand. Die
  Karriere eines Kriminalfalls* (Berlin: be.bra Verlag, 2008/2nd
  ed. 2013), synthesising the postwar archival record and recent
  Berlin Court archive releases, concludes that van der Lubbe acted
  alone. Hans Mommsen, "The Reichstag Fire and Its Political
  Consequences," in Henry A. Turner Jr., ed., *Nazism and the Third
  Reich* (New York: Quadrangle, 1972), 109–150 [original German
  1964] — argues the same case from a different evidentiary base.

  Position B — "Nazi co-conspiracy." Fritz Tobias, *Der
  Reichstagsbrand: Legende und Wirklichkeit* (Rastatt: Grote, 1962;
  English: *The Reichstag Fire*, New York: Putnam, 1964) — actually
  argued the alone-perpetrator thesis against the prevailing Cold
  War-era assumption of Nazi conspiracy. Note: contrary to common
  shorthand, Tobias was the original "alone" thesis defender;
  Mommsen built on him. The "Nazi co-conspiracy" position is
  associated with later writers including Walther Hofer and the
  *Internationaler Komitee zur wissenschaftlichen Erforschung der
  Ursachen und Folgen des Zweiten Weltkrieges* (Hofer et al.,
  *Der Reichstagsbrand: Eine wissenschaftliche Dokumentation*,
  multiple volumes 1972–1992). [EVIDENCE NEEDED: a clean chronology
  of which scholar argued which thesis when — the Tobias/Mommsen
  vs. Hofer debate is often misremembered in summary writing.
  Delon to source the Hofer volumes and verify the historiographical
  sequence.]

  Working position for the book: the alone-perpetrator thesis is
  the current prevailing scholarly view (Kellerhoff 2008 is the
  most recent comprehensive synthesis); the Nazi co-conspiracy
  thesis is a minority view that remains in print. The
  responsibility analysis does not depend on resolving this
  question — see Preventability and Benefit, both of which load on
  the Nazi response to the fire regardless of whether the fire
  itself was set with prior Nazi knowledge.

  What is NOT contested: the Nazi leadership had been preparing
  emergency-decree legislation for weeks before the fire. The
  Prussian preventive-detention orders that went out the night
  of 27/28 February drew on prepared arrest lists. Whether or not
  the Nazis knew the fire was coming, they were ready for it.

  Preventability:
  The fire itself: the Reichstag building's security was thin on
  the night of 27 February, and van der Lubbe entered via an
  outside window. Whether better security would have prevented
  this specific act is unanswerable.
  The constitutional consequences: the Reichstag Fire Decree was
  signed by President Hindenburg under Article 48 of the Weimar
  Constitution. The decree's scope — suspending seven
  constitutional articles and authorising Reich intervention in
  the *Länder* — far exceeded prior Article 48 precedent. The
  Reichstag presidency (Göring) was the body that should have
  constrained executive overreach; instead, Göring used his
  Reichstag presidency to enable the Enabling Act. The Weimar
  judiciary (the *Reichsgericht* itself) heard the trial in
  September–December 1933 and, while acquitting Dimitrov, Popov,
  Tanev, and Torgler, convicted van der Lubbe and accepted the
  prosecution's "Communist conspiracy" framing in the judgment's
  reasoning.

  What would have prevented the decree's misuse: a Reichstag
  majority willing to reject the Enabling Act (achieved only by
  the SPD's 94 dissenting votes on 23 March 1933 — short by 287
  votes including the absent/detained KPD bloc); a presidency
  unwilling to sign the 28 February decree (Hindenburg signed);
  a judiciary unwilling to convict on the prosecution's
  conspiracy theory (the *Reichsgericht* convicted van der Lubbe
  individually but reasoned in conspiracy terms).

  Record controller:
  In 1933: the Prussian political police (Diels/Section IA, later
  Gestapo) controlled the investigative file; the
  *Reichsgericht* controlled the trial transcript; the Nazi
  Propaganda Ministry (Goebbels, from 13 March 1933) controlled
  the press framing.
  Postwar: the case file fragments survive across multiple
  archives — *Bundesarchiv* Berlin (R 3001 *Reichsjustizministerium*
  files, R 58 *Reichssicherheitshauptamt* files); the Berlin
  Landesarchiv (Berlin court records); the
  *Reichsgericht* trial record in the *Sächsisches Staatsarchiv*
  Leipzig. Captured German records held in the US National
  Archives (RG 242, T-175 Microcopy) supplement the picture.
  Critical postwar custody event: the 2008
  *Generalbundesanwalt* annulment proceedings reviewed the
  surviving *Reichsgericht* judgment and determined it qualified
  under the 1998 NS-AufhG for annulment (see "How the alibi
  weakened").

  Cost bearer:
  Direct: Marinus van der Lubbe — convicted of *Hochverrat*
  (high treason) and arson against public buildings, sentenced
  to death on 23 December 1933, executed by guillotine in the
  prison courtyard at Leipzig on 10 January 1934, three days
  before his 25th birthday. He maintained from arrest to
  execution that he had acted alone.
  Indirect (immediate): approximately 4,000 KPD officials and
  members arrested in the night of 27/28 February; thousands
  more detained in March and April 1933; the KPD Reichstag
  deputies detained or driven into exile; the SPD effectively
  silenced through detention and intimidation by 23 March.
  Indirect (long): every victim of the Nazi regime whose
  detention or worse rested on the legal architecture inaugurated
  by the 28 February decree — including the post-23 March 1933
  legal regime under the Enabling Act. The decree was renewed
  routinely and remained operative until the regime's collapse in
  May 1945. It is one of the foundational legal instruments of
  Nazi rule.

  Responsibility synthesis (per the book's seventh question — what
  would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of
  visibility?): The blame fell on van der Lubbe, who controlled
  only the matches he carried into the building. Control over the
  constitutional consequence — and over the regime that consequence
  enabled — sat with Hindenburg (signature on the decree), Hitler,
  Frick, and Göring. The visible blamed party controlled almost
  nothing; the unblamed controllers received virtually unconstrained
  legislative authority within four weeks. The gap between visible
  blame and actual control in this case is approximately the gap
  between a single foreign drifter with matches and the executive
  apparatus of a state of 65 million people.

How the alibi hardened:
1. **Hours-fast framing.** Within hours of the fire, Göring
   announced a Communist conspiracy. The Prussian arrest orders
   went out before the trial of facts began. The framing
   preceded — and therefore conditioned — the investigation.
2. **The Reichstag Fire Decree, 28 February 1933.** The decree
   gave the framing immediate legal force: emergency powers
   based on the named threat (Communist insurrection) were
   exercised before the alleged threat had been adjudicated.
3. **The Reichstag Fire Trial verdict, 23 December 1933.** The
   *Reichsgericht* acquitted Dimitrov, Popov, Tanev, and Torgler
   for want of evidence — a result that, paradoxically, hardened
   the alibi in the international press: the trial was held to
   be "fair" because the four co-defendants walked, while van
   der Lubbe was convicted alone. The international narrative
   that "the Communists were acquitted but the arsonist
   convicted" allowed the regime's domestic consolidation to
   proceed under cover of apparent judicial process. (Dimitrov's
   self-defence at the trial became internationally famous and
   he was deported to the USSR in February 1934.)
4. **Van der Lubbe's execution, 10 January 1934.** Execution
   foreclosed any further evidentiary contest. From January 1934
   onward, the alleged perpetrator was no longer available to
   be questioned.
5. **The Enabling Act, 23 March 1933.** The Enabling Act
   converted the constitutional emergency into ordinary
   legislative practice; the original justification (the fire
   as Communist conspiracy) faded into the background as the
   regime's legal architecture matured. The alibi did not need
   to be remembered — the regime built on it had moved on.
6. **The 1933 Lex van der Lubbe.** A retroactive amendment to
   the Reich Penal Code (Article 5 of the *Gesetz über
   Verhängung und Vollzug der Todesstrafe*, 29 March 1933)
   raised the penalty for arson against public buildings from
   *Zuchthaus* (penitentiary) to death and applied
   retroactively — specifically so that van der Lubbe could
   be executed under the new penalty. The retroactive law
   itself is a separate, smaller alibi-hardening device:
   it formalised what was already politically determined.

How the alibi weakened:
1. **Postwar historiographical reconstruction.** Tobias 1962
   challenged the dominant Cold War-era assumption of Nazi
   co-conspiracy by arguing van der Lubbe alone — a thesis
   uncomfortable for both Western anti-fascist orthodoxy and
   East German doctrine. Mommsen 1964/1972 built on Tobias.
   The debate has continued; Kellerhoff 2008 is the most
   recent comprehensive synthesis on the alone-perpetrator
   side. The debate, regardless of which side is correct,
   weakens the original alibi because both positions agree:
   the immediate, hours-fast framing of "KPD conspiracy" was
   not supported by the actual evidence the regime had at
   the time.
2. **1981 Berlin court rehabilitation.** The Berlin
   *Landgericht* in 1981, on the petition of van der Lubbe's
   brother, set aside the death sentence on procedural
   grounds (the retroactive Lex van der Lubbe); the broader
   conviction was not at that time fully annulled.
3. **The 1998 NS-AufhG.** The
   *Gesetz zur Aufhebung nationalsozialistischer
   Unrechtsurteile in der Strafrechtspflege* (Law on the
   Annulment of National Socialist Unjust Judgments in the
   Administration of Criminal Justice), passed by the
   Bundestag on 25 August 1998 and amended in 2002 and 2009,
   provides for the automatic annulment of National Socialist
   criminal judgments that conflicted with elementary
   principles of justice.
4. **The 2008 Generalbundesanwalt annulment.** In January
   2008, the *Generalbundesanwalt* (Monika Harms then in
   office) announced the annulment of van der Lubbe's
   conviction under the 1998 NS-AufhG, on the ground that
   the conviction rested on a retroactive criminal statute
   contrary to elementary justice principles. The annulment
   is the load-bearing partial-interception event for the
   chapter's beat-9 framing (see Book function below). It is
   partial because: (a) it came 75 years after van der
   Lubbe's execution; (b) it did not, on its own, restore
   the names of the 4,000+ KPD arrestees of the night of
   27/28 February or the millions of subsequent victims of
   the regime built atop the decree; (c) the annulment is
   formal — it does not constitute an evidentiary
   reopening of the underlying historical question.
5. **Continuing scholarly access.** The
   *Bundesarchiv* holdings and the captured German records
   in the US National Archives have been open to researchers
   for decades. New documentary syntheses (Kellerhoff 2008)
   continue to refine the picture. The historiographical
   alibi does not survive the archives.

Best counterargument:
"This is not pure scapegoat — van der Lubbe was at the scene, he
carried matches and firelighters, he confessed, and the
*Reichsgericht* convicted him on his own confessions and the
physical evidence. He was not innocent of the act for which he was
convicted; the death sentence was the legal scandal (retroactive
law), not the fact-finding. The case is therefore at most partial
scapegoat — the act was real, only the consequence was
disproportionate and the conspiracy framing fabricated."

Shirley's response: the four-category taxonomy turns on the
relationship between the BLAMED party and the BLAME ASSIGNED, not
only on whether the blamed party did something. Van der Lubbe set
the fire (per the prevailing scholarly view); the blame assigned
was for *Hochverrat* (high treason) and for an alleged Communist
conspiracy that — even by the regime's own subsequent admissions
when convenient — he did not actually represent. He was assigned
the blame for an event (the alleged KPD uprising) that did not
exist, executed under a retroactive law passed specifically for
him, and his execution served as anchor for a state-power
consolidation he could not have intended. Classification as
**pure scapegoat** is justified by the gap between his actual act
(setting a fire as a one-man protest) and the blame he was made
to bear (architect of a Communist conspiracy threatening the
state). The taxonomy hybrid layer — system/object alibi — is the
reification of "the Communist conspiracy" as the alibi-object that
justifies the decree. Both layers are necessary to teach the case
in chapter 1.

Counter-counterargument (steel-manning the steelman): a careful
reader may argue that "pure scapegoat" should be reserved for
the innocent (e.g., Dreyfus), and that van der Lubbe's actual act
of arson disqualifies the category. The book's working answer:
the category turns on the SUBSTITUTION (what is the blame doing?),
not on whether the substitute did some related thing. The taxonomy
should be tested on this case in chapter 2's introduction of
categories. Bonnie to decide whether to maintain "pure scapegoat"
or move the case to "partial scapegoat" with explicit reasoning;
the chapter 1 narrative function (the modern echo of the literal
ritual) is intact under either classification.
```
