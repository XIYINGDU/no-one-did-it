# Abu Ghraib — "Bad Apples" and the Stopped Chain

Case: Abu Ghraib detainee abuse (Tier 1A — partial scapegoat)
Domain: war / statecraft / military detention policy
Dates/place: October 2003 – early 2004 (documented abuse window); investigations 2004–2008; Abu Ghraib prison ("Hard Site," Tier 1A), Iraq.
Case type: partial scapegoat

Classification note: The military police soldiers convicted were genuinely culpable for documented abuses on the night shift of Tier 1A. The laundering is the "bad apples" framing that prevented blame from climbing to interrogation-doctrine designers (OLC memos), the Office of the Secretary of Defense, the Joint Staff, and theater commanders who authorized or tolerated coercive techniques migrated from Guantanamo to Iraq.

```text
Crisis:
  In late April 2004, CBS 60 Minutes II (Apr 28) and Seymour Hersh in The
  New Yorker (May 10) published photographs taken inside Tier 1A of Abu
  Ghraib showing US Army military police posing with stripped, hooded,
  and restrained detainees. The photographs included the hooded detainee
  on a box with wires attached to his hands (Abdou Hussain Saad Faleh, aka
  "Gilligan"), the human pyramid, dog-threat scenes, and forced-nudity
  tableaux. The crisis was global; the images circulated in the Arab press
  within hours and became the defining image of the US occupation of Iraq.

Official story:
  "A few bad apples on the night shift." On May 7, 2004, Secretary of
  Defense Donald Rumsfeld told the Senate Armed Services Committee that
  the abuse was the work of a small number of soldiers acting outside
  their orders. President George W. Bush echoed the framing publicly.
  The MAJ Gen Antonio M. Taguba report (completed Mar 2004, leaked
  early May 2004) was characterized in public statements as documenting
  the failures of the 800th MP Brigade and the night-shift soldiers. The
  framing collapsed responsibility into a small set of low-rank uniformed
  personnel at one prison.

Blame container:
  The night-shift MPs of the 372nd Military Police Company, 320th MP
  Battalion: SPC Charles Graner, PFC Lynndie England, SSG Ivan
  "Chip" Frederick II, SPC Sabrina Harman, SPC Megan Ambuhl, SPC Jeremy
  Sivits, SPC Armin Cruz, SGT Javal Davis, and others. Eleven soldiers
  were court-martialled; convictions and pleas spanned 2004–2006. BG
  Janis Karpinski (commander, 800th MP Brigade) was demoted to colonel
  and relieved (May 2005) — a partial climb that still stopped well
  below the doctrine and policy actors.

Actual responsibility chain:
  1. Office of Legal Counsel (DOJ): Jay S. Bybee / John Yoo "Standards
     of Conduct for Interrogation under 18 U.S.C. §§ 2340–2340A" memo,
     Aug 1, 2002 — re-defined torture narrowly; later withdrawn by OLC
     (Levin memo, Dec 30, 2004).
  2. Secretary of Defense Rumsfeld: Dec 2, 2002 approval of Category I
     and II techniques (and select Category III with case-by-case
     approval) for Guantanamo; rescinded Jan 15, 2003; Apr 16, 2003
     re-approved a reduced list. Working Group Report Apr 4, 2003.
  3. SOUTHCOM / Guantanamo command: techniques operationalized at GTMO.
  4. MAJ Gen Geoffrey D. Miller: visited Iraq Aug 31–Sep 9, 2003;
     recommended "Gitmo-izing" Abu Ghraib interrogation operations.
     (Schlesinger Report Annex; Taguba Report.)
  5. Coalition Joint Task Force-7 (CJTF-7), LTG Ricardo S. Sanchez:
     Sep 14, 2003 interrogation-and-counter-resistance policy
     authorizing techniques (later partly rescinded Oct 12, 2003);
     Schlesinger and Fay/Jones found the policy created ambiguity that
     migrated into MP behavior.
  6. Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence
     (Stephen Cambone, Lt Gen William G. Boykin): pushed expanded
     interrogation authorities; SASC Inquiry (Levin–McCain) details.
  7. Military Intelligence brigade at Abu Ghraib (205th MI Bde, COL
     Thomas M. Pappas; LTC Steven L. Jordan): de facto operational
     control of Tier 1A interrogation conditions; Fay/Jones Report
     attributed responsibility to MI as well as MP personnel.
  8. The 372nd MP Company night-shift MPs: executed the documented
     abuses, photographed them, in conditions shaped by all of the
     above.

Control:
  Day-to-day cellblock control sat with the 372nd MP Company night shift
  and the MI personnel directing interrogation conditions. Operational
  control of CJTF-7 sat with LTG Sanchez. Detention-and-interrogation
  doctrine was set by OSD (Rumsfeld), enabled by OLC (Bybee/Yoo).
  Karpinski commanded the 800th MP Brigade administratively but the
  Fay/Jones Report found that MI exercised effective control over Tier
  1A. (Taguba Report §§ on chain of command; Fay/Jones Report Section I.)

Benefit:
  Career and policy benefit accrued to the architects who could claim
  interrogation success without owning interrogation conduct. After the
  scandal, several senior actors continued in or were promoted to senior
  positions; the Bybee memo's author Jay Bybee was already a federal
  appeals court judge (Ninth Circuit) by August 2003. The Senate Armed
  Services Committee Inquiry (Dec 11, 2008) concluded that senior
  officials' decisions "conveyed the message that physical pressures
  and degradation were appropriate treatment for detainees" (Conclusion
  19, SASC Report).

Knowledge:
  The ICRC reported abuse concerns to Coalition authorities throughout
  2003; the ICRC's Feb 2004 confidential report (leaked May 2004 to
  the Wall Street Journal) documented systematic mistreatment and was
  delivered to the Coalition Provisional Authority. CID had opened
  investigations in January 2004 after SPC Joseph M. Darby turned over
  the photographs (Jan 13, 2004). The SASC Inquiry (2008) documented
  senior knowledge of "enhanced" technique migration from GTMO to Iraq.

Preventability:
  Multiple inflection points where the chain could have been stopped:
  withdrawal of the Bybee memo (only occurred Dec 2004, post-scandal);
  refusal of the Miller "Gitmo-ization" recommendation (Aug–Sep 2003);
  enforcement of FM 34-52 interrogation doctrine rather than ad-hoc
  SOPs; response to ICRC complaints in 2003. The Schlesinger Panel
  (Aug 2004) found "fundamental failures throughout all levels of
  command."

Record controller:
  Initially DoD (CID investigations, command-directed AR 15-6
  inquiries). The decisive break was outside-DoD control: SPC Darby's
  decision to turn the photos over to CID; CBS's possession and
  airing of the images; Hersh's reporting; the ICRC's leaked report.
  Senate Armed Services Committee (Levin–McCain) subpoenaed and
  declassified the executive-branch decision chain (Inquiry into the
  Treatment of Detainees in U.S. Custody, Nov 20, 2008, declassified
  Apr 21, 2009).

Cost bearer:
  Iraqi detainees subjected to the documented abuses (named individuals
  including Manadel al-Jamadi, who died in CIA custody at Abu Ghraib
  Nov 4, 2003 — homicide ruled by AFIP, no senior conviction). The
  US Army's reputation among Iraqis and the wider Arab and Muslim
  world; soldiers who served honorably and were tarred by association.
  Eleven convicted MPs and NCOs served sentences from no time
  (Ambuhl) to 10 years (Graner, sentenced Jan 15, 2005). No senior
  policy or doctrine actor was criminally prosecuted; no Bybee/Yoo
  memo author faced bar discipline that survived (the OPR report
  recommending discipline was overridden by AAG David Margolis, 2010).

How alibi hardened:
  - Rumsfeld's "few bad apples" framing on May 7, 2004 set the public
    template before the Taguba Report was fully understood.
  - Court-martial proceedings, conducted at unit level, produced
    individual convictions that visibly closed the case for each
    soldier; the absence of parallel proceedings against doctrine
    actors made the visible accountability look complete.
  - The Bybee/Yoo memos were withdrawn (Dec 2004) and replaced (Levin
    memo) without prosecutions, allowing "policy was changed" to
    substitute for "policy actors faced consequences."
  - Classification of the SASC Inquiry until April 2009 delayed the
    public chain map by years.

How alibi weakened:
  - Taguba Report (Article 15-6 investigation by MAJ Gen Antonio M.
    Taguba, completed Mar 9, 2004; leaked early May 2004): documented
    "systemic and illegal abuse" and named command failures above the
    night shift.
  - Schlesinger Panel (Independent Panel to Review Department of
    Defense Detention Operations, Aug 24, 2004): "fundamental failures
    throughout all levels of command" and identified the migration of
    techniques from GTMO.
  - Fay/Jones Report (MG George R. Fay and LTG Anthony R. Jones, Aug
    2004): MI personnel as well as MP personnel responsible; specific
    failures by COL Pappas and LTC Jordan; doctrinal confusion from
    Sanchez's Sep 14, 2003 memo.
  - ICRC report (Feb 2004, leaked May 2004): prior notice to the
    Coalition that mistreatment was systematic.
  - SASC Inquiry (Nov 20, 2008; declassified Apr 21, 2009): linked OLC
    memos, Rumsfeld decisions, SOUTHCOM/GTMO, the Miller visit, the
    Sanchez memo, and Abu Ghraib in a single documentary chain.
  - OPR Report on Bybee/Yoo (released Feb 2010 with Margolis
    memorandum): even after Margolis declined to find professional
    misconduct, the OPR factual findings stood.

Best counterargument:
  "The soldiers really did the acts in the photographs; they were not
  ordered to do these specific things; their commanders did not see
  the photographs in advance; therefore the convictions match the
  offenders." This argument is partially correct — and it is
  precisely what makes the case a **partial** scapegoat rather than a
  pure one. The book's claim is not that the convicted soldiers were
  innocent (they were not), but that legitimate convictions at the
  bottom of the chain were treated as if they completed
  accountability for the chain above them. The counterargument's
  weakness is documented by the Schlesinger, Fay/Jones, and SASC
  findings that doctrinal and command decisions materially shaped
  the conditions under which the abuses occurred.

  A second counterargument: "Karpinski WAS demoted, so the chain did
  climb." Response: demotion of a brigade commander still stops far
  below the OSD-OLC-SOUTHCOM chain that the Schlesinger and SASC
  documents trace. Karpinski herself maintained, in published and
  Congressional statements, that MI controlled Tier 1A; the Fay/Jones
  Report partially supports her on operational control. Whether her
  demotion was appropriate accountability or itself a partial-scapegoat
  step is contested — flagged as an open question rather than resolved.

  **Stronger counter-version (must be answered, not dismissed):** the
  absence of senior-actor prosecution reflects the legal cover the OLC
  memos (Bybee/Yoo Aug 1 2002; Yoo Mar 14 2003) provided to the chain
  of command at the time, plus the absence of US ratification of the
  Rome Statute — not a successful blame-laundering operation. Under
  UCMJ Art. 92 and the *Calley* line on manifest illegality,
  prosecutors had a credible *mens rea* defence to navigate before
  they could prosecute senior actors; the OLC memos may have made that
  defence durable enough to deter charging decisions independent of
  laundering.

  **Rebuttal (per IHL doctrine, not UCMJ):** This counter conflates
  UCMJ prosecutability with command-responsibility doctrine under
  AP I Article 87 and Rome Statute Article 28(a)–(b). Receiving OLC
  legal advice does not discharge the superior's *independent* duty
  to prevent, repress, and punish; Rome Statute Article 33(2) deems
  orders to commit genocide or crimes against humanity manifestly
  unlawful. Although torture is not enumerated in Art 33(2)'s text,
  the gravity of torture as a grave breach (Geneva Conventions Common
  Art 3 / CAT) and as a war crime (Rome Statute Art 8) supports the
  doctrinal view that orders to commit torture are manifestly
  unlawful, foreclosing the superior-orders defence under Art 33(1)(c).
  The Nuremberg line (IMT Charter Art. 8;
  reaffirmed Rome Statute Art. 33(1)(c)) was specifically designed
  to prevent this kind of legal-advice-as-shield framing. The
  counter has force on UCMJ but does not reach the doctrinal level
  on which the book's responsibility claim rests.
```
