# UK Post Office Horizon — Case File (ch-7 record-control anchor)

Case name: The Post Office Horizon IT scandal — Fujitsu-supplied accounting software, the Post Office's private prosecution authority, and the institutional control of the diagnostic record that produced more than 700 sub-postmaster prosecutions between 1999 and 2015.
Domain: public administration; commercial accounting software; English civil and criminal procedure (Post Office as private prosecutor); statutory inquiry under the Inquiries Act 2005; record-control as instrument of prosecution.
Dates and place: 1996–1999 (Horizon system development and rollout by ICL Pathway, later Fujitsu Services Ltd, under the Pathway contract with the Post Office) → 1999–2015 (period of disputed prosecutions of sub-postmasters by the Post Office, acting as private prosecutor under English law) → May 11, 2009 (*Computer Weekly* exposé by Rebecca Thomson, the first public aggregation of dispersed sub-postmaster accounts) → May 2009 (JFSA — Justice for Subpostmasters Alliance — founded by Alan Bates) → 2012–2015 (Second Sight Support Services investigation, terminated by the Post Office in March 2015) → March 2017 (Group Litigation Order in *Bates and Others v Post Office Ltd*, High Court of England and Wales) → March 15, 2019 (Fraser J, *Common Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 606 (QB)) → December 16, 2019 (Fraser J, *Horizon Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB)) → December 2019 (*Bates* settlement at GBP 57.75 million, of which substantial proportion absorbed by litigation funder costs) → September 22, 2020 (Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry under Sir Wyn Williams established by the Government as a non-statutory inquiry) → June 1, 2021 (Inquiry converted to statutory under the Inquiries Act 2005) → April 23, 2021 (Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), *Hamilton & Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 577, quashing 39 convictions on the principal Horizon ground) → January 1–4, 2024 (*Mr Bates vs The Post Office*, ITV four-part drama, broadcast as political amplifier) → May 24, 2024 (Royal Assent of the Post Office (Horizon System) Offences Act 2024, statutory exoneration without case-by-case appeal) → 2024–ongoing (Inquiry oral hearings continuing; final report expected after 2024). Setting: England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland; sub-postmasters as ostensibly self-employed agents operating under contracts that made them personally liable for branch shortfalls regardless of cause; Fujitsu-controlled diagnostic system as the sole evidentiary record.

Case type: system/object alibi
Layer of the alibi: artifact + record-control (the Horizon software output treated as evidence; Post Office Ltd and Fujitsu Services Ltd control of the engineering record).
Secondary case type (hybrid, per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`): cost-bearing goat. The sub-postmasters bore the harm (prosecution, bankruptcy, imprisonment, suicide in at least four documented cases, communities deprived of post office services) while the actual responsibility-holders avoided the cost. Per the *Horizon Issues* judgment ([2019] EWHC 3408 (QB), Fraser J), the Post Office and Fujitsu were found to have institutional notice of relevant Horizon bugs across the period of the prosecutions; per *Hamilton & Others* ([2021] EWCA Crim 577), the Post Office's failure to disclose what it knew about Horizon's unreliability was an "affront to the conscience of the Court." Both classifications are load-bearing: the system/object alibi is the mechanism by which the laundering operated (the software output treated as evidence); the cost-bearing-goat is the outcome at the cost-distribution layer.

Note on chapter-7 scoping: This case file is built for Chapter 7 ("The Record Is the Battlefield") with the record-control framing as primary. The same case is referenced in `therac-25-counter.md` as the counter-case for Therac-25 in Chapter 2's system/object alibi slot, where the *interception* (Fraser J's judgments + Coyne's reverse-engineering + statutory exoneration) is the load-bearing framing. The two files are complementary by design: ch-2 uses Horizon to teach interception of a software-artifact alibi (beat 9 of ch-2); ch-7 uses Horizon as the primary anchor for institutional record-control laundering (the entire chapter's structural spine). Both files must remain consistent on facts; the framing differs by chapter need.

```text
Crisis:
Between 1999 and 2015, the Post Office prosecuted approximately
700 sub-postmasters and sub-postmistresses — and pursued
civil-recovery action against approximately 4,000 more — on the
basis of accounting shortfalls displayed by the Horizon system
at their branches. Sub-postmasters were charged with theft, false
accounting, and fraud. Conviction rates in the Post Office's
private prosecutions were high (the Post Office reported
approximately 95% conviction or guilty-plea rates in internal
documents later disclosed to the Inquiry). Sub-postmasters who
disputed the Horizon shortfalls were told the system was reliable;
that no other postmaster had reported similar problems (a claim
the Post Office knew or should have known to be false from at
least 2010 when Computer Weekly's reporting and the JFSA's
coordination had aggregated the dispersed accounts); and that
their personal liability under contract required them to make
good the shortfall or face prosecution. Many sub-postmasters
pleaded guilty to lesser charges (false accounting) to avoid the
prison sentences associated with theft conviction, on advice that
the alternative was a contested trial against the Post Office's
control of the technical record. At least four sub-postmaster
suicides are documented as connected to Horizon prosecutions; the
inquiry has heard evidence regarding additional deaths. Hundreds
of sub-postmasters were declared bankrupt, lost their homes, and
lost their livelihoods. Hundreds died before exoneration or
compensation was completed. The crisis is, simultaneously, a
twenty-five-year miscarriage of justice on a scale unprecedented
in modern English legal history and a case study in how
institutional control of a diagnostic record can convert an
accounting system into a prosecution engine.

Official story:
The Post Office's official story across the period of the
laundering had three layered components, each load-bearing for
the prosecution model and each false in the operative sense
later established by Fraser J's *Horizon Issues* judgment of
December 16, 2019:

(a) Horizon is reliable. Post Office Ltd represented to
    sub-postmasters, to courts, and to Parliament that the
    Horizon system was "robust" and that accounting shortfalls
    displayed at branch level represented genuine cash or
    stock discrepancies attributable to the sub-postmaster.
    The common-law presumption of computer reliability —
    derived from *Castle v Cross* [1984] 1 WLR 1372 and the
    post-1999 statutory regime — was invoked to place the
    evidentiary burden on the defense to prove the system
    had erred, a burden no individual sub-postmaster could
    practically discharge without disclosure of Fujitsu's
    engineering records.

(b) No other sub-postmaster has reported similar problems.
    The Post Office repeatedly represented to individual
    sub-postmasters under investigation that their case was
    isolated. The representation was false from no later than
    2010 (the JFSA had been founded in May 2009 and Computer
    Weekly had published the aggregated account on May 11,
    2009), and was made knowingly false in many documented
    individual instances per Inquiry transcripts.

(c) Remote access to branch accounts is not possible without
    the sub-postmaster's knowledge. Fujitsu engineers — and
    Post Office Ltd officials briefed by Fujitsu — had
    capability to make remote edits to branch accounts via
    "OCRC" (Operational Change Request Control) and
    "remediation" processes. The capability was denied or
    minimised in evidence given by Fujitsu engineer Gareth
    Jenkins in multiple Post Office prosecutions, including
    the prosecution of Seema Misra (R v Misra, 2010), in which
    Misra was convicted in part on the strength of Jenkins's
    evidence that Horizon could not be remotely accessed. The
    denial was later established as untrue per the Horizon
    Issues judgment and subsequent Inquiry disclosure.

Blame container:
The sub-postmasters themselves — as the named cause of the
shortfalls — were the cost-bearers of the laundering. The
operative alibi at the institutional layer is the Horizon
system itself, plus the record-control structure that converted
its output into evidence:

(1) Horizon-as-system. The accounting software was the named
    "neutral" agent — the institutional output that
    represented the truth of branch cash and stock positions.
    The system/object alibi reified the software as the
    diagnostic authority; the sub-postmaster who disputed the
    output was treated as disputing reality.

(2) The record-control architecture. Post Office Ltd, as
    contracting principal, owned the Horizon contract with
    Fujitsu, owned the prosecutorial function (as private
    prosecutor under English law), owned the disclosure
    process for the prosecutions it brought, and possessed
    a contractual relationship with Fujitsu that controlled
    the technical record (Horizon error logs, the KEL — Known
    Error Log — Fujitsu's internal incident-tracking record,
    remote-access logs, source code access). The Post Office
    was therefore simultaneously the (i) deployer of the
    diagnostic tool, (ii) sole party to the audit trail of the
    diagnostic tool's behavior, (iii) complainant in the
    prosecution, (iv) prosecutor in the prosecution, and (v)
    party responsible for disclosure to the defense. The
    structural conflation of these five roles — what the book
    will call record-control laundering — is the load-bearing
    institutional mechanism.

(3) The legal presumption of computer reliability. English
    common law (and post-1999 statutory practice) treated
    computer output as presumptively reliable. The defense
    had to overcome the presumption to dispute the Horizon
    output, but the defense could not access the Fujitsu
    engineering record to do so without disclosure — which
    the prosecutor (the Post Office) controlled and did not
    provide.

The convergence of (1), (2), and (3) is the record-control
alibi. The chapter-7 thesis: where one institution controls
the diagnostic tool, the technical record of the tool's
behavior, the prosecutorial process that uses the record, and
the disclosure obligation that should release the record to
the defense, the record itself becomes the battlefield — and
the institution that controls the record wins by default
until an outside fact-finder with subpoena power compels
disclosure.

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Post Office Ltd held control across the architecture: the
  Horizon contract (originally with ICL Pathway, then Fujitsu
  Services Ltd after the Fujitsu acquisition of ICL); the
  branch-network governance under which sub-postmasters
  operated as agents; the audit and investigation function
  (the Post Office Security team conducted internal
  investigations of branch shortfalls and acted as prosecution
  investigators); the private prosecution authority (under
  English law, the Post Office could bring private criminal
  prosecutions without a Crown Prosecution Service charging
  decision); and the disclosure function (decisions about what
  to disclose to defense in those prosecutions). The Post
  Office Board, the Chief Executive (Paula Vennells, CEO
  2012–2019; predecessor David Mills, CEO 2010–2012; and prior
  CEOs going back to the Horizon rollout period), and the
  General Counsel function (Susan Crichton, Chris Aujard, Jane
  MacLeod successively) exercised institutional control. UK
  Government Investments Ltd (UKGI), and before it the
  Shareholder Executive, held the Government shareholding and
  appointed Government-side board representatives but did not
  exercise day-to-day operational control.

  Fujitsu Services Ltd held control of the Horizon engineering
  record. Fujitsu maintained the source code, the error logs,
  the KEL, and the remote-access capability. Fujitsu's senior
  technical staff — most prominently engineer Gareth Jenkins,
  who gave expert evidence in multiple Post Office prosecutions
  — held knowledge of Horizon bugs and remote-access processes
  that was not transmitted in actionable form to the courts in
  which Fujitsu personnel testified, per the Inquiry's
  transcript record.

  Benefit:
  Post Office Ltd avoided the cost of investigating and
  remedying Horizon's underlying defects by routing branch
  shortfalls to sub-postmasters under their personal-liability
  contractual obligation. The financial recovery from
  sub-postmasters across the period of the laundering exceeded
  GBP 100 million by some estimates (precise figure under
  reconstruction by the Inquiry). Post Office Ltd executives
  retained their compensation packages through the period:
  Paula Vennells received approximately GBP 5 million in
  remuneration across her tenure as CEO and was appointed CBE
  in the 2019 New Year Honours (the CBE was returned in
  January 2024 following the ITV drama broadcast and
  consequent public-petition pressure).

  Fujitsu Services Ltd retained the Horizon contract across
  the period and continued to bid for and win further UK
  Government IT contracts during the period when its
  engineering knowledge of Horizon defects was concealed from
  the courts. The estimated total value of Fujitsu UK
  Government contracts during the period exceeds GBP 4 billion.

  UK Government departments avoided the political cost of
  Post Office Ltd reform across the period; the Post Office
  branch network is politically significant and the Government's
  shareholding made any visible Post Office crisis a Government
  reputational matter.

  Knowledge:
  Per the *Horizon Issues* judgment and the Inquiry disclosure
  record, Post Office Ltd and Fujitsu Services Ltd both held
  knowledge of relevant Horizon bugs and remote-access
  capability across the period of the laundering. Specific
  documented knowledge points:

  - The KEL (Known Error Log) maintained by Fujitsu recorded
    Horizon bugs that "had the potential to cause apparent or
    alleged shortfalls in branch accounts," per Fraser J's
    *Horizon Issues* judgment finding.
  - Fujitsu engineers conducted remote remediation of branch
    accounts during the period in which Post Office Ltd
    represented to courts that remote access was not possible.
  - Second Sight Support Services, commissioned by the Post
    Office in 2012 to conduct an independent investigation,
    began producing findings critical of the Post Office's
    position; the Post Office terminated the Second Sight
    contract in March 2015.
  - Internal Post Office documents disclosed to the Inquiry
    include "Clarke advice" — legal advice from barrister
    Simon Clarke to the Post Office in 2013 that there were
    serious problems with prosecutions that had relied on
    Gareth Jenkins's evidence, given Jenkins's failure to
    disclose Horizon issues; the Post Office did not act on
    the Clarke advice to systematically review or stop
    affected prosecutions.

  The chain-of-command knowledge inside Post Office Ltd —
  who at executive level knew what and when — is being
  reconstructed by the Inquiry. The chapter must report what
  the Inquiry transcript record has established and treat the
  final report as a documented forthcoming event that will
  reshape the precise knowledge attribution.

  Preventability:
  The laundering was preventable at multiple architectural
  and procedural points:
  - Pre-deployment (1996–1999): A diagnostic-record
    architecture that gave the Post Office, Fujitsu, AND the
    independent regulator-equivalent access to error logs and
    remote-access logs — rather than vesting all three in the
    Post Office / Fujitsu relationship — would have prevented
    the disclosure asymmetry. The architectural choice was
    made at deployment.
  - Prosecution-stage (1999–2015): Disclosure compliance with
    the Criminal Procedure and Investigations Act 1996 (CPIA)
    would have required the Post Office to disclose to defense
    counsel the Horizon engineering record including the KEL
    and the remote-access logs. CPIA disclosure failures are
    the load-bearing prosecutorial finding in *Hamilton &
    Others* [2021] EWCA Crim 577 — the convictions were
    quashed because the Post Office's failure to disclose
    "was an affront to the conscience of the Court."
  - Post-2009: After *Computer Weekly*'s May 2009 aggregation
    and the JFSA's founding, the Post Office had institutional
    notice that branch shortfalls were not isolated. The
    Second Sight commission in 2012, and its termination in
    2015, document a window in which the Post Office could
    have systematically reviewed prosecutions but did not.
  - Post-2013: The Clarke advice gave the Post Office's
    senior legal function explicit notice that prosecutions
    relying on Jenkins's evidence had problems. The decision
    not to act on the Clarke advice is one of the
    institutional preventability gaps that the Inquiry is
    reconstructing.

  Record controller:
  The record-control structure is the central diagnostic
  finding for ch-7 and warrants explicit enumeration:

  - Horizon source code: Fujitsu Services Ltd (sole control;
    Post Office had contractual rights but did not exercise
    independent engineering oversight).
  - Horizon error logs (operational record of Horizon
    behavior): Fujitsu Services Ltd (primary); Post Office
    Ltd (contractual access).
  - KEL (Known Error Log — Fujitsu's internal record of
    known bugs and their effects): Fujitsu Services Ltd (sole
    control until disclosure in the *Bates* litigation).
  - Remote-access logs (Fujitsu engineer access to branch
    terminals and account remediation): Fujitsu Services Ltd
    (sole control until disclosure).
  - Branch accounting records (Horizon output at branch
    level): Post Office Ltd via Horizon.
  - Sub-postmaster contract files (the contracts that made
    sub-postmasters personally liable): Post Office Ltd.
  - Investigation files (Post Office Security team
    investigations of branch shortfalls that fed into
    prosecutions): Post Office Ltd.
  - Prosecution files (the Post Office's private-prosecution
    record): Post Office Ltd.
  - Disclosure files (the records of what was disclosed and
    what was withheld in each prosecution): Post Office Ltd.
  - Second Sight investigation reports: contested control;
    the Post Office terminated the contract and asserted
    confidentiality; subsequently disclosed via Inquiry.
  - Inquiry transcript and exhibit record: Post Office Horizon
    IT Inquiry, Sir Wyn Williams; statutory under the Inquiries
    Act 2005 since June 2021; publicly available via the
    Inquiry's official website.
  - *Bates* litigation court file: High Court of England and
    Wales; subsequently the BAILII reports of the *Common
    Issues* and *Horizon Issues* judgments.
  - CCRC referral files: Criminal Cases Review Commission;
    the CCRC referred the principal block of cases to the
    Court of Appeal.

  The defining feature of the record-control alibi: every
  record that could have falsified the Post Office's case
  against an individual sub-postmaster was in the Post Office's
  or Fujitsu's exclusive control until the *Bates* Group
  Litigation's disclosure phase (2017–2019) and the Inquiry's
  evidence-gathering phase (2021–). The structural
  asymmetry between record-holder (Post Office / Fujitsu) and
  defendant (sub-postmaster) is the institutional fact the
  chapter installs.

  Cost bearer:
  - Approximately 700 sub-postmasters prosecuted; the exact
    figure is part of what the Inquiry is reconstructing.
    Approximately 100 convictions overturned by appellate
    judgment 2021–2023; hundreds more covered by the 2024
    statutory exoneration.
  - At least four sub-postmaster suicides documented as
    connected to Horizon prosecutions (Martin Griffiths is
    among those named in public reporting); the inquiry has
    heard evidence regarding additional deaths attributed by
    relatives.
  - Hundreds of sub-postmasters died before exoneration or
    compensation was completed.
  - Seema Misra was sentenced to 15 months in November 2010
    while pregnant; her case is among the most-cited individual
    instances of the prosecutorial harm.
  - Bankruptcies, lost homes, lost livelihoods, family
    ruptures documented across hundreds of accounts compiled
    in the Inquiry record.
  - Communities deprived of post office services where
    branches closed after sub-postmaster removal.
  - Litigation funder (Therium) absorbed a substantial share
    of the December 2019 *Bates* settlement of GBP 57.75
    million, reducing the per-claimant distribution; this
    is a recurring limitation of class-action redress in the
    English costs regime.

How the alibi hardened:
1. The contractual architecture. Sub-postmaster contracts
   made the sub-postmaster personally liable for branch
   shortfalls regardless of cause. The contract converted
   any Horizon-displayed shortfall into a recoverable debt
   against the sub-postmaster, structurally placing the
   evidentiary burden on the sub-postmaster to disprove the
   Horizon output.

2. The common-law presumption of computer reliability. From
   *Castle v Cross* [1984] 1 WLR 1372 onward, English law
   treated computer output as presumptively reliable; the
   defense bore the burden of rebutting the presumption.
   Without disclosure of the Horizon engineering record, no
   individual sub-postmaster could rebut the presumption in
   practice.

3. The Post Office as private prosecutor. English law
   permitted the Post Office to bring private criminal
   prosecutions without CPS charging review. The Post Office's
   simultaneous role as complainant, investigator, prosecutor,
   and disclosure-controller eliminated the structural
   distance that public prosecution provides.

4. The dispersion of victims. Sub-postmasters were
   geographically dispersed across the UK, one per branch.
   Without external coordination, the individual sub-postmaster
   could not know that their case was not isolated. The Post
   Office's representation that no other sub-postmaster had
   reported similar problems exploited the dispersion.

5. The "guilty plea" pressure. Sub-postmasters were
   commonly offered a guilty plea to lesser charges (false
   accounting) in exchange for the Post Office not pursuing
   theft charges (and associated prison sentences). Many
   sub-postmasters pleaded guilty to charges they disputed
   in fact, on legal advice that the alternative was a
   contested trial against the Post Office's control of
   the technical record. The high guilty-plea rate then
   reinforced the institutional narrative that the
   sub-postmasters had stolen.

6. The reputation cost. A sub-postmaster prosecuted and
   convicted faced not only the legal consequences but the
   social consequences in the small communities the branch
   served. The reputation harm operated as a deterrent to
   organising against the Post Office.

7. The litigation-cost barrier. Until the JFSA's coordination
   with litigation funder Therium produced the funding for
   the *Bates* Group Litigation in 2017, no individual
   sub-postmaster could afford the disclosure litigation
   that the *Bates* Group Litigation eventually compelled.

8. The professional-witness asymmetry. Fujitsu engineer
   Gareth Jenkins gave expert evidence in multiple Post
   Office prosecutions across the period. The Post Office
   relied on Jenkins's evidence to establish Horizon
   reliability; the defense lacked the resources and
   technical access to challenge the evidence.

9. The auditor and regulatory absence. Horizon was a
   commercial accounting system used by the Post Office,
   not a regulated software product subject to independent
   audit. No regulator-equivalent had authority to compel
   disclosure of the engineering record.

How the alibi weakened:
1. The *Computer Weekly* aggregation, May 11, 2009. Rebecca
   Thomson's "Bankruptcy, prosecution and disrupted lives —
   postmasters tell their story" was the first public
   document to assemble the dispersed sub-postmaster
   accounts into a recognisable pattern. The publication
   broke the "no other sub-postmaster has reported similar
   problems" representation as a load-bearing factual
   claim.

2. The JFSA's founding, May 2009. Alan Bates founded the
   Justice for Subpostmasters Alliance the same month as
   the *Computer Weekly* publication. JFSA's coordination
   of the dispersed sub-postmaster community across the
   following decade — assembling documentary evidence,
   securing litigation funding via Therium, surviving
   attempts at settlement that would have closed the case
   without the disclosure phase — is the load-bearing
   civil-society precondition for the *Bates* litigation.

3. Second Sight investigation, 2012–2015. Second Sight
   Support Services was commissioned by the Post Office
   in 2012 to conduct an independent investigation in
   response to MP pressure (notably James Arbuthnot, MP
   for North-East Hampshire). Second Sight's findings
   began to indict the Post Office; the Post Office
   terminated the contract in March 2015. The Second
   Sight reports — once disclosed via the Inquiry —
   established that the Post Office had institutional
   notice of Horizon defects no later than 2013–2014.

4. Group Litigation Order, March 2017. The High Court of
   Justice (Queen's Bench Division) granted a GLO
   consolidating the dispersed sub-postmaster claims into
   one managed action. The GLO is the procedural
   innovation that made disclosure litigation
   institutionally possible.

5. Fraser J, *Common Issues* judgment, March 15, 2019,
   [2019] EWHC 606 (QB). The first institutional finding
   that the sub-postmaster contracts could not be
   construed as making sub-postmasters strictly liable for
   shortfalls of unknown cause. The judgment was the legal
   precondition for the *Horizon Issues* trial.

6. Fraser J, *Horizon Issues* judgment, December 16, 2019,
   [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB). The load-bearing institutional
   act of the entire counter-laundering process. The
   313-page judgment found, in operative language, that
   the Horizon IT system was not "remotely robust" in the
   relevant respects, that bugs, errors, and defects "did
   have the potential to cause apparent or alleged
   shortfalls in branch accounts," and that Fujitsu and
   the Post Office had known of relevant bugs. The
   judgment rested on detailed examination of the KEL and
   Fujitsu-disclosed engineering records — material the
   Post Office and Fujitsu had not disclosed in the prior
   prosecutions.

7. *Hamilton & Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim
   577, April 23, 2021. The Court of Appeal (Criminal
   Division) quashed 39 convictions on the principal
   ground that prosecutions based on Horizon evidence had
   been conducted as "an affront to the conscience of the
   Court" where the Post Office had failed to disclose
   what it knew about Horizon's unreliability. Subsequent
   appellate judgments quashed further convictions; the
   total of judicially-overturned convictions by the date
   of the 2024 statutory exoneration was approximately
   100.

8. Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry. Established by the
   Government as a non-statutory inquiry in September
   2020; converted to statutory under the Inquiries Act
   2005 in June 2021. Sir Wyn Williams chair. Oral
   hearings have continued through 2024. Inquiry
   disclosure has reconstructed the institutional
   knowledge chain inside the Post Office and Fujitsu in
   detail unavailable from the *Bates* litigation alone.
   The final report is not yet published as of May 2026
   and is expected to reshape several findings about
   chain-of-command knowledge.

9. *Mr Bates vs The Post Office*, ITV four-part drama,
   broadcast January 1–4, 2024, written by Gwyneth Hughes.

Book function:
Primary anchor for Chapter 7 ("The Record Is the
Battlefield"). The Horizon case installs the chapter's
load-bearing diagnostic finding: where one institution
controls (i) the diagnostic tool, (ii) the technical record
of the diagnostic tool's behavior, (iii) the prosecutorial
process that uses the record, and (iv) the disclosure
obligation that should release the record to the defense,
the record itself becomes the battlefield. The institution
that controls the record wins by default until an outside
fact-finder with subpoena power compels disclosure.

The chapter uses Horizon as the canonical instance to teach
the reader to recognise record-control laundering across
domains: (a) the algorithmic-decision systems contemporary
to the book's argument that produce decisions (loan denials,
benefits cuts, child-welfare interventions, predictive
policing flags) without an audit trail accessible to the
decision-subject; (b) the corporate-record control architectures
that put the dispute-resolution evidence in the hands of one
party (employment arbitration, consumer-credit dispute, EHR
record access in clinical malpractice); (c) the law-enforcement
practices that vest record control of body-cam, dash-cam, and
investigation files in the institution being challenged.
Horizon is the canonical case because the record-control
asymmetry is documented in court (Fraser J's *Horizon Issues*
judgment) at a level of specificity that the analogues lack.

beats walk through (1) the accusation scenes — Seema Misra and
others arrested on Horizon evidence; (2) the official story —
Horizon is reliable, no other sub-postmaster has reported
```
