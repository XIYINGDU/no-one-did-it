# DOGE / White House Records-Preservation Fight — Case File

Case: Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) / U.S. DOGE Service (USDS) operating with contested archival obligations under the Presidential Records Act (PRA) and Federal Records Act (FRA); reported use of Signal and other auto-deleting messaging; litigation by CREW, American Oversight, National Security Archive, and others on records preservation and FOIA access.
Domain: federal recordkeeping (PRA 44 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2209; FRA 44 U.S.C. §§ 3101–3107); FOIA (5 U.S.C. § 552); the Federal Advisory Committee Act question if applicable; agency-status questions for USDS.
Dates/place: USDS established by Executive Order 14158, "Establishing and Implementing the President's 'Department of Government Efficiency'" (signed Jan 20, 2025; 90 Fed. Reg. 8441 (Jan. 29, 2025)). Litigation: CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service, No. 1:25-cv-00511 (D.D.C., Judge Christopher R. Cooper, filed Feb 20, 2025); American Oversight v. DOGE, Musk, et al. (D.D.C., 2025, alleging Signal/Google Docs use to circumvent FRA); additional National Security Archive and other plaintiff suits filed across 2025.
Case type: system/object alibi
Secondary case type: cost-bearing goat

Hybrid classification note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`, explicit hybrid declaration). (1) Primary — system/object alibi: DOGE itself is reified as the operational agent. Public framing presents "DOGE" as the entity making cuts, exposing waste, and (when challenged) being obstructed. The named-but-not-agency entity absorbs both the credit for its acts and the legal-procedural shielding from those acts: when subpoenaed or FOIA'd, its status is contested ("not an agency"); when celebrated, its outputs are claimed as governmental. The procedural shell (agency-status ambiguity) is the alibi. (2) Secondary — cost-bearing goat: terminated federal employees whose jobs DOGE acted to eliminate absorb economic and reputational cost without being publicly accused of misconduct; some are publicly named in DOGE communications as the human face of "waste" while the upstream decisions and their authors remain procedurally opaque. The chapter-9 *move* this case teaches is **reverse scapegoating** in its record-control variant: record-keepers, judges enforcing preservation orders, and oversight litigants are recast in administration messaging as the obstructionist villains — accountability infrastructure as enemy. "Reverse scapegoating" is the descriptor of how the alibi is constructed, not a fifth taxonomy label.

Crisis:
On January 20, 2025, the President signed Executive Order 14158
establishing the U.S. DOGE Service (USDS) by reconstituting the
United States Digital Service. The executive order created a
"DOGE Team" structure with placement of DOGE personnel inside
component agencies, an "Administrator" reporting to the White House
Chief of Staff, and a sunset date of July 4, 2026 (per the EO text).
Elon Musk served as a "special government employee" associated with
USDS through 2025; his exact legal role, length of service, and
duties were the subject of public and litigated dispute. Reporting
through 2025 documented:
  - Significant federal personnel actions attributed to DOGE
    decisions (workforce reductions at multiple agencies including
    the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, USAID, and the
    Department of Education).
  - DOGE personnel access to federal data systems including (per
    reporting and litigation) Treasury payment systems, Social
    Security Administration databases, and IRS systems.
  - Use of the encrypted messaging application Signal by DOGE
    personnel for official business, including the use of
    auto-delete functions; use of personal Google accounts and
    Google Docs for what appeared to be official deliberations.
  - Removal or non-publication of records that would ordinarily
    document agency deliberations and decisions.
  - Aggressive public characterization of inspectors general, FOIA
    officers, archivists, and oversight litigants as bureaucratic
    obstacles to DOGE's mission.

Separate from DOGE specifically, the "Signalgate" episode (March 24,
2025) — in which The Atlantic's editor-in-chief Jeffrey Goldberg was
inadvertently added to a Signal group chat involving senior national-
security officials discussing planning for U.S. military strikes on
Houthi targets in Yemen — independently documented the use of Signal
for sensitive official communications at the highest level of the
administration. The episode is a separate case file from DOGE
records, but the underlying records-evasion pattern is the same and
the chapter must distinguish.

Official story (chapter-9 reverse-scapegoating frame, drawn from
on-the-record EO and statements):
EO 14158 frames USDS as a modernization and efficiency program;
public administration communications characterize DOGE's mission as
exposing waste and fraud that "the bureaucracy" had concealed. When
litigation or FOIA requests challenge records practices, the
administration position has variously been:
  - USDS is not an "agency" subject to FOIA (litigated and rejected
    in part: see Judge Cooper's rulings).
  - The records of DOGE personnel embedded in component agencies
    belong to those agencies, not to a separate USDS records
    system.
  - Signal use is for personal coordination, not official business
    requiring preservation.
  - Judicial preservation orders are an "obstruction" of the
    administration's reform agenda.
The framing recasts:
  - inspectors general, archivists, and FOIA litigants as
    obstructionist villains;
  - the FRA / PRA itself as a tool of the prior regime to be
    routed around;
  - the President / Administrator / DOGE Team as goat-bearing
    reformers struggling against a recalcitrant records apparatus.

Blame container:
Two stacked blame containers. (A) "The bureaucracy" — diffuse,
unnamed, treated as the entity DOGE is "fighting." Specific civil
servants whose jobs are eliminated absorb economic cost without
being individually accused. (B) "The obstructionist judiciary" /
"activist FOIA litigants" — the record-keepers and the courts
enforcing preservation are blamed for hampering reform. The chapter
must keep these two containers separate.

Actual responsibility chain (for the records-evasion conduct and
the workforce-reduction decisions; the EO authority and the
records-preservation obligations are separately legal questions):

A. Records / FRA / PRA conduct:
  1. The President: signed EO 14158 establishing USDS; the executive
     authority for the establishment is not in serious dispute. The
     subsequent records-handling decisions of DOGE personnel are
     attributable, in part, to the chain of command USDS sits
     within (Office of the President / Chief of Staff).
  2. The Administrator of USDS and senior DOGE personnel: operational
     authority over USDS records practices.
  3. Component-agency heads receiving DOGE Teams: have agency-level
     FRA recordkeeping obligations for records created or received
     by DOGE personnel acting in agency capacity.
  4. The Archivist of the United States and NARA: enforcement
     authority over FRA records-preservation obligations; can issue
     guidance and refer matters to DOJ for enforcement.
  5. Inspectors general of affected component agencies: have
     statutory authority to inquire into recordkeeping compliance
     within their agencies.

B. Workforce-reduction decisions:
  1. The President and OMB Director: ultimate authority over
     reductions-in-force at federal agencies, subject to statutory
     constraints (5 U.S.C. ch. 35; OPM regulations 5 C.F.R. pt. 351).
  2. Component-agency heads: nominal decisional authority for RIFs
     within their agencies, subject to documented White House /
     DOGE direction.
  3. Federal labor relations: collective-bargaining-agreement and
     MSPB procedural rights for affected employees.

Control:
The legal status of USDS — whether it is an "agency" for FOIA,
FRA, APA, and FACA purposes — is the load-bearing control question
the litigation has been contesting. Judge Christopher Cooper's
preliminary ruling in CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service (D.D.C., March 10,
2025) found USDS likely subject to FOIA on the basis that it
"wields substantial authority independently of the President"
and issued preservation orders. Subsequent rulings in 2025 have
elaborated the agency-status analysis. The administration's
position that USDS is purely advisory has been contested
case-by-case. The chapter must use the procedural-stage formula
("the District Court ruled that USDS is likely subject to FOIA;
the matter is on appeal" or similar) accurate to the verification
date.

Benefit:
  - Political principal: rhetorical benefit from a centralized
    cost-cutting narrative; ability to attribute reductions to
    "DOGE" rather than to specific cabinet or White House
    decisions; insulation from agency-by-agency legal challenge.
  - DOGE personnel: opportunity and resume benefit; in Musk's
    case, the special-government-employee status enabled
    significant influence on federal operations without the
    confirmation process applicable to Senate-confirmed positions.
  - Procedural benefit: agency-status ambiguity creates a
    structural advantage in FOIA and litigation timelines —
    records that may eventually be disclosed under court order
    are not produced in real time, by which point operational
    decisions are irreversible.

Knowledge:
The President signed the EO that established USDS and is the
political head of the chain. Public knowledge of records
obligations is documented: the Federal Records Act and PRA are
publicly enacted statutes; FRA training is standard for federal
employees; the "Signalgate" episode established that senior
national-security officials were using Signal for sensitive
business by March 2025. Whether specific DOGE personnel had
actual knowledge of FRA obligations varies; the "newly arrived"
defense (used in some litigation briefs and reflected in Judge
Cooper's ruling that DOGE "may not fully appreciate its
obligations to preserve federal records, especially for staffers
who are reported to have recently joined the federal government")
is a documented mitigation, not exculpation. The decree-level
knowledge — that the chain-of-command knew federal records
preservation obligations exist — is A-grade public.

Preventability:
Records-preservation failures are structurally preventable in
named ways the chapter must enumerate:
  - Use of FRA-compliant communications channels (agency email,
    captured chat, agency-managed devices) instead of personal
    Signal accounts;
  - Disabling auto-delete on messaging platforms used for
    official business;
  - FRA-mandated capture of communications when conducted on
    personal accounts (44 U.S.C. § 2911 — the post-Clinton-email
    statute requiring federal employees using non-official
    electronic messaging accounts to copy or forward to an
    official account within 20 days);
  - Affirmative compliance by component agencies hosting DOGE
    personnel;
  - NARA enforcement authority;
  - Inspector General inquiries.

Record controller:
This is the central irony the chapter must dramatize: the case
IS about who controls the record. The contesting record
controllers:
  - USDS / White House / component agencies: hold the original
    records that may or may not be preserved.
  - NARA: holds statutory authority for FRA enforcement and
    receives federal records by transfer.
  - The federal courts (D.D.C. in particular): have issued
    preservation orders requiring USDS to retain records pending
    litigation.
  - FOIA and oversight litigants (CREW, American Oversight,
    National Security Archive, Democracy Forward, ACLU, individual
    journalist plaintiffs): hold an institutional interest in
    eventual disclosure and have built independent records of
    what was sought, when, and how the requests were treated.
  - Whistleblowers: hold contemporaneous knowledge of records
    practices; some have made disclosures to OIGs and to
    congressional oversight as of 2026-05-26.

Cost bearer:
Three categories of cost bearer:
  - Terminated federal employees: workforce reductions attributed
    to DOGE actions removed an estimated [EVIDENCE NEEDED: anchor
    final 2025 personnel-action count from OPM data or GAO
    follow-up] federal employees; affected workers include
    inspectors general fired in January–February 2025, USAID
    staff (the agency was substantially dismantled across early
    2025), CFPB staff, Department of Education staff, and others.
    Named officials publicly characterized by administration
    communications include several IGs (the January 2025 IG
    firings are a separate case file).
  - Beneficiaries of federal programs whose service was
    interrupted: in the USAID dismantling, recipients of foreign
    humanitarian assistance bear the program-level cost; the
    chapter must distinguish institutional cost from the
    downstream human cost.
  - Civic record itself: durable federal records that, if not
    preserved, are unrecoverable. The cost of records destruction
    is borne by future investigators, historians, and the
    institutional capacity for accountability.

How the alibi hardened:
  - The agency-status ambiguity is built into EO 14158's
    structure (USDS reconstitutes USDS / sits within EOP / has
    Teams deployed into agencies — a deliberately blurred
    institutional shape).
  - Use of encrypted communications with auto-delete is itself a
    technical hardening.
  - Public messaging that frames record-keepers as obstructionists
    primes the audience to discount preservation litigation.
  - Mass firings of inspectors general at component agencies
    (January 24-25, 2025) removed the most institutionally
    independent records-and-conduct oversight from within agencies.
  - Speed: by the time records litigation reaches court, the
    underlying operational actions (terminations, program
    eliminations, data-system access) may already be effectively
    irreversible.

How the alibi weakens (record points pressing on the evasion):
  - Federal court preservation orders in CREW v. USDS and
    companion cases are durable and enforceable; contempt
    authority is available.
  - Judge Cooper's preliminary finding that USDS is likely subject
    to FOIA establishes a foundational rejection of the
    "not-an-agency" position.
  - 44 U.S.C. § 2911 (the non-official electronic-messaging
    statute) is on its face applicable to Signal use for official
    business; violations are documentable.
  - The "Signalgate" episode independently established Signal use
    by senior officials; the inadvertent inclusion of a journalist
    placed the practice into the durable public record before any
    administrative narrative could close it.
  - National Security Archive, CREW, and American Oversight have
    institutional FOIA-litigation memory across multiple
    administrations and litigated through the Clinton/Bush/Obama/
    Trump-I/Biden cycles — their record-keeping discipline is
    independent of the agency under litigation.
  - Congressional oversight letters and hearings (House Oversight,
    Senate HSGAC minority) build an institutional record of what
    was asked and what answers were given (or refused).
  - Whistleblower disclosures channeled through OSC, IGs, and
    congressional channels accumulate independent of DOGE's
    cooperation.

Best counterargument:
The strongest defense of the administration position has three
prongs the chapter must steelman. (1) Executive authority: the EO
establishing USDS is within the President's constitutional and
statutory authority to organize the Executive Office of the
President; reorganizations are not new and are not per se illegal
even when they reduce the workforce or eliminate functions.
(2) Agency-status genuinely ambiguous: the line between EOP
component (not an agency under FOIA per Kissinger v. Reporters
Committee, 445 U.S. 136 (1980) and successor cases) and a
substantially independent operation IS legally contested; the
administration position has serious legal support, and Judge
Cooper's preliminary ruling is preliminary. (3) Modernization
defense: federal records practices ARE outdated; modern
communications platforms ARE used widely in the private sector;
some friction in transition is unavoidable.

The book's response: the chapter must concede (1) entirely — the
EO authority is intact. (2) is conceded as legally contested at the
verification date; the chapter uses procedural-stage wording. (3)
is the chapter's pivot: the modernization defense COULD be made
consistent with FRA preservation (agency-controlled platforms with
capture exist; the question is operational discipline, not
technological possibility). The reverse-scapegoating diagnostic
does NOT depend on resolving (2): even if USDS is ultimately
adjudicated non-agency, the records practices of DOGE personnel
acting within component agencies are still FRA-bound, and the
recasting of record-keepers as obstructionists operates
independent of the agency-status legal question.

Intercepting mechanisms (counter-case directions for beat 9):
The chapter-9 counter-case for the records-control variant of
reverse scapegoating must locate a documented interception in which
record-preservation infrastructure survived deliberate evasion.
Candidate counter-cases (Loki initial scan; final selection at
Shirley's counter-case pass):
  - The Pentagon Papers / NYT v. United States, 403 U.S. 713 (1971):
    record-durability through external custody (the leaked copies
    survived government efforts to prevent publication). Interception
    mechanism: journalism-as-custodian plus First-Amendment doctrine.
    The lesson is partial — the suppression effort failed, but the
    interception required individual risk (Daniel Ellsberg) and a
    Supreme Court holding.
  - The Hillary Clinton email FOIA litigation cycle (2014–2017):
    institutional FOIA litigants succeeded in producing a durable
    record of personal-email use for official business; the
    technical capture mechanism (server retrievals; State OIG
    audit) was the load-bearing interception. The relevance to
    DOGE/Signal is direct: the same statutory framework (FRA
    § 2911) is operative, and the same kind of institutional
    plaintiff is litigating now.
  - U.S. v. North (Iran-Contra) records survival: contemporaneous
    PROFS email backups outlived their deleting authors and were
    eventually produced through litigation and congressional
    inquiry. Mechanism: backup-system-as-record-controller
    independent of the deleting actor.
  - Watergate tapes: external-record-controller (the White House
    taping system, Alexander Butterfield's disclosure, and the
    Supreme Court's order in U.S. v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974))
    intercepted the destruction effort. The Nixon Library and
    NARA's institutional capacity to receive and curate presidential
    records is itself a downstream consequence of the Watergate
    intercept.
  Absence-finding caveat: the full counter-case for the DOGE
  specific pattern (Signal auto-delete + agency-status ambiguity)
  has no perfect historical analogue. The closest are the
  Iran-Contra PROFS backups and the Watergate tapes. The
  chapter's beat 9 will likely combine record-durability
  mechanisms (PROFS) with external-custodian mechanisms (Pentagon
  Papers) and judicial enforcement (U.S. v. Nixon) into a
  composite escape rather than a single named counter-case.
