# Family Separation / Zero Tolerance — Case File

Case name: DHS-implemented separation of migrant children from parents at the southern border under the "Zero Tolerance" policy, 2017–2018.
Domain: federal immigration enforcement; 8 U.S.C. § 1325 (improper entry by an alien — misdemeanor); DHS Customs and Border Protection (CBP), Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR); DOJ U.S. Attorney's Office referral process.
Dates and place: El Paso sector pilot ("Phase I"), July 2017 – November 2017 (per DHS OIG OIG-20-06 and DOJ OIG Jan 2021); Attorney General Sessions memorandum directing "zero tolerance" prosecution of § 1325 referrals, April 6, 2018; DHS Secretary Nielsen signs the separation memo, May 4, 2018; public announcement, May 7, 2018; Executive Order 13841 ("Affording Congress an Opportunity to Address Family Separation"), June 20, 2018; Ms. L. v. ICE preliminary injunction, S.D. Cal., June 26, 2018 (Judge Dana Sabraw). Site: U.S.–Mexico border, primarily Texas, Arizona, California, New Mexico ports of entry and Border Patrol sectors.
Case type: partial scapegoat
Hybrid note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`): primary is partial scapegoat — the § 1325 prosecution-referral mechanism is genuinely culpable at the execution layer (the misdemeanor charge does in fact produce a removal of the adult from family-unit custody) but the attribution stops at "the statute," shielding the named decision-makers who chose separation as the operational lever. A secondary system/object alibi layer fires at the procedural-shell level: "the law required it" reified the statute as the agent.

```text
Crisis:
Between roughly July 2017 (the El Paso "Phase I" pilot, per DHS OIG
OIG-20-06) and June 2018, the U.S. government separated at least 5,500
children from their parents at the southern border, with the figure
later revised upward by court-supervised review under Ms. L. v. ICE to
include earlier and broader cohorts. Children as young as infants were
removed from their parents; tracking systems between DHS (which held
the adults) and HHS-ORR (which held the children) were not designed to
preserve the parent-child link. As of January 2026, the Ms. L. v. ICE
Steering Committee reported that several hundred separated children had
not yet been reunited with their families; final tallies remain
contested. Reporters and inspectors documented children sleeping under
mylar blankets on concrete floors in CBP holding facilities and at the
HHS-contracted "tender age" shelters; audio of separated children
crying for their parents (ProPublica, June 18, 2018) became the
public-recognition artifact that broke the policy's media containment.

Official story:
The administration's stated rationale, advanced consistently from
April through June 2018, was a clean legal-pretext claim: the
Department of Justice was enforcing 8 U.S.C. § 1325 (improper entry —
a misdemeanor on first offense). Because the statute requires adult
defendants to be transferred to federal criminal custody for
prosecution, the children could not lawfully accompany them. Family
separation was characterised by the Attorney General, the DHS
Secretary, and the White House as a consequence of prosecution, not a
policy. AG Sessions, May 7, 2018: "If you cross this border unlawfully,
then we will prosecute you. It's that simple. If you smuggle illegal
aliens across our border, then we will prosecute you. If you are
smuggling a child, then we will prosecute you and that child will be
separated from you as required by law." DHS Secretary Nielsen, June
17, 2018 (Twitter): "We do not have a policy of separating families at
the border. Period." The legal pretext rested on the claim that
separation was the mechanical consequence of a prosecution decision
the executive branch was required to enforce.

Blame container:
Two layered containers, in this sequence:

1. The statute (8 U.S.C. § 1325). The misdemeanor charge was named as
   the cause: "the law required it." This is the legal-pretext alibi
   in its purest form — a clean statutory referent absorbs the moral
   weight of the operational choice. The container worked because §
   1325 had existed in the U.S. Code since 1929 and had been used (at
   far lower volume) by prior administrations. The container failed
   because (a) prior administrations had not adopted blanket
   referral for parents traveling with children, (b) the El Paso
   pilot documented in OIG-20-06 showed the deterrent intent
   pre-dating the public April 2018 announcement, and (c) internal DHS
   emails released via FOIA showed senior officials acknowledging
   separations as an intended consequence rather than an incidental
   one.
2. Procedural breakdowns at CBP / ICE / HHS-ORR. After the policy
   was publicly disavowed, the secondary container became "the
   tracking systems failed" — the parent-child link broke because
   the IT systems were not designed for it. DHS OIG OIG-18-87
   (September 2018) and OIG-20-06 (January 2020) documented the
   tracking failures and confirmed they were known in advance.

[Evidence grade: A — DHS OIG OIG-18-87 (2018); DHS OIG OIG-20-06 (2020);
DOJ OIG Report on Zero-Tolerance (January 2021); Ms. L. v. ICE filings;
Senate PSI report (October 2020).]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Attorney General Jeff Sessions issued the April 6, 2018 memorandum
  directing U.S. Attorneys along the southwest border to "adopt
  immediately a zero-tolerance policy for all offenses referred for
  prosecution under section 275(a) of the INA." DHS Secretary Kirstjen
  Nielsen signed the May 4, 2018 memorandum directing CBP to refer all
  amenable adults — explicitly including those traveling with children
  — for § 1325 prosecution. The El Paso "Phase I" pilot (July–November
  2017) operated under DHS authority prior to the AG memo. White House
  policy coordination ran through Stephen Miller (Senior Advisor) and
  the Domestic Policy Council. Per DOJ OIG (January 2021), then-Deputy
  Attorney General Rod Rosenstein and senior DOJ officials participated
  in coordination meetings about prosecution capacity and operational
  scope.

  Benefit:
  The deterrent objective. Internal communications cited in the Senate
  Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations report (October 2020) and
  the DOJ OIG report (January 2021) document that senior officials —
  including AG Sessions, in oral statements characterised in the DOJ
  OIG report — described family separation as an intended deterrent to
  Central American migration. The political audience was the
  administration's base; the operational audience was prospective
  migrants. AG Sessions, May 7, 2018, to a Border Patrol audience:
  "If you don't want your child separated, then don't bring them
  across the border illegally."

  Knowledge:
  The DHS OIG (OIG-18-87, September 2018) found that DHS "was not
  fully prepared to implement the Administration's Zero Tolerance
  Policy or to deal with some of its after-effects." OIG-20-06
  (January 2020) found that CBP had conducted the El Paso pilot in
  2017 — months before the public April 2018 announcement — and that
  DHS leadership was aware of the tracking-system limitations before
  scaling. The Senate PSI report (October 2020) documents that senior
  DHS and DOJ officials knew separations would occur, knew the
  tracking infrastructure was inadequate, and proceeded anyway. The
  DOJ OIG report (January 2021) found that the AG and senior DOJ
  officials were aware that the policy would result in family
  separations and that this was an intended consequence of the
  prosecution-referral approach.

  Preventability:
  Preventable at three documented points:
  - Pre-April 2018: the El Paso pilot's documented separation rates
    and tracking failures (per OIG-20-06) provided a sufficient
    evidentiary basis to halt rather than scale the program.
  - April–May 2018: building a working parent-child tracking system
    before scaling was operationally feasible; DHS leadership chose
    not to require it.
  - June 2018: the policy ended within days of sustained public and
    legal pressure (EO 13841 on June 20, 2018; Ms. L. preliminary
    injunction June 26, 2018), demonstrating that the operational
    lever was discretionary, not statutorily compelled.

  Record controller:
  DHS (CBP, ICE) controlled the adult-detention record; HHS-ORR
  controlled the child-shelter record; the two systems were not
  designed to share parent-child link data. DOJ controlled the
  prosecution referral record. DHS OIG and DOJ OIG controlled the
  inspector-general investigative records. The Ms. L. v. ICE
  Steering Committee, established by court order, controls the
  reunification tracking record post-2018 (and continues to do so as
  of 2026). Plaintiffs' counsel (ACLU) controlled significant FOIA-
  acquired internal-communications material released through
  litigation.

  Cost bearer:
  The separated children and their parents. Approximately 5,500
  children separated under the formal Zero Tolerance period
  (April–June 2018), with the Ms. L. court-supervised count
  including earlier and broader cohorts. Documented harms include
  acute and chronic traumatic stress, developmental disruption, and
  in several cases inability to reunify because parents had been
  deported without their children. As of January 2026, the Ms. L.
  Steering Committee reported that several hundred children remained
  unreunified. Civil claims under the Federal Tort Claims Act are
  ongoing; the Biden administration commenced settlement discussions
  in 2021 that broke down publicly and have been periodically
  revisited.

How the alibi hardened:
1. The statutory-pretext form. 8 U.S.C. § 1325 is a real statute that
   had been on the books since 1929. The "we are enforcing the law"
   framing is the cleanest possible legal pretext: it cites a specific
   statutory provision and characterises the operational outcome as a
   mechanical consequence. The form was difficult to attack head-on
   because each component (the statute exists; prosecution is a DOJ
   prerogative; prosecution requires adult detention) is true in
   isolation.
2. The deterrence theory's plausibility. Deterring unauthorised
   migration is a recognised policy objective with a long history in
   U.S. immigration enforcement (consequence delivery, expedited
   removal, prosecutorial referral). Framing separation as deterrence
   placed it within an existing policy vocabulary rather than as a
   novel act of harm.
3. The IT-systems-as-failure secondary framing. After the policy was
   publicly disavowed in June 2018, the administration shifted to
   characterising the reunification difficulties as a technical
   failure of legacy systems — language that reified the IT systems
   as the agent of harm.

How the alibi weakened:
1. ProPublica audio recording, June 18, 2018 ("Listen to Children Who
   Just Have Been Separated From Their Parents at the Border" —
   Ginger Thompson). The audio became the public-recognition artifact
   that broke media containment.
2. Ms. L. v. ICE, S.D. Cal., Case No. 18-cv-428 (Judge Dana Sabraw).
   The June 26, 2018 preliminary injunction ordered reunification
   within 14 days for children under 5 and within 30 days for older
   children. The court's continuing supervision through the Steering
   Committee preserved the documentary record across administrations.
3. Executive Order 13841 (June 20, 2018). The administration's own
   executive order ending separations is itself evidence that the
   operational lever was discretionary — that "the law required it"
   was not, in fact, true at the level of administrative choice.
4. DHS OIG OIG-18-87 (September 2018) — the first official
   institutional account documenting that DHS was not prepared to
   implement the policy or its after-effects.
5. DHS OIG OIG-20-06 (January 2020) — documented the El Paso pilot
   pre-dating the public April 2018 announcement.
6. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations report (October
   2020), "Separated: The DHS Policy of Family Separation." Documents
   the policy genesis, decision-makers, and the gap between public
   statements and internal communications.
7. DOJ Office of the Inspector General report (January 2021), "Review
   of the Department of Justice's Planning and Implementation of Its
   Zero Tolerance Policy and Its Coordination with the Department of
   Homeland Security and Health and Human Services." Documents the
   DOJ side of the planning, including AG Sessions's and senior
   officials' awareness that family separations would result.

Best counterargument:
"This is not a partial-scapegoat case. The administration changed the
policy under EO 13841 within weeks; Ms. L. v. ICE forced reunification
on a court timeline; multiple Inspector General reports produced an
unusually thorough public record; the policy did not survive politically
or operationally. Accountability climbed: from line officers, to the
DHS and DOJ secretaries, to the policy as a whole, in less than two
months. Calling this 'laundering' overstates how successfully the
statutory pretext absorbed responsibility; it was rejected, and
quickly."

Loki's response: The counterargument is strongest at the
political-survival layer (the policy did not survive) and weakest at
the accountability-for-named-decision-makers layer. The policy ended;
the named decision-makers (Sessions, Nielsen, Rosenstein) did not face
federal prosecution, professional discipline by their bar associations,
or civil liability in their individual capacities. The Bivens-style
damages action against named officials in K.O. v. United States and
related cases has faced repeated qualified-immunity dismissals. The
laundering claim in this case is not "the policy survived" — it
clearly did not — but "the policy was advanced under a statutory
pretext that allowed named decision-makers to characterise harm as
a mechanical legal consequence, and the named decision-makers retained
their professional standing while several hundred children remain
unreunified as of 2026." Partial scapegoat sits at the procedural-
referral layer: the § 1325 referral mechanism was named (correctly,
at the execution level) as the proximate cause, while the policy
choices to scale referrals to parents with children, to do so without
adequate tracking, and to use the resulting separations as deterrent
were not personally adjudicated against named officials.
```
