# Volkswagen Dieselgate — Case File

Case name: Volkswagen AG defeat-device emissions fraud (2.0L EA189 and 3.0L V6 TDI diesel engines); EPA Notice of Violation; CARB notice; DOJ criminal plea; subsequent civil settlements; named-individual indictments and convictions across the United States and Germany.
Domain: automotive manufacturing; passenger-vehicle emissions regulation under the US Clean Air Act (CAA) and California air-quality law; federal criminal fraud (Title 18 conspiracy; obstruction of justice; importation by false statements); corporate compliance and engineering-management failure across an OEM and its tier-1 software supplier.
Dates and place: Defeat-device software developed and deployed across model years 2009–2015 across approximately 11 million vehicles worldwide (approximately 600,000 in the United States). Independent detection: International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT) commissioned testing performed by West Virginia University's Center for Alternative Fuels, Engines and Emissions (CAFEE) on road-trip routes in May 2014; final report published May 15, 2014. CARB and EPA investigation runs May 2014 → September 2015. EPA Notice of Violation issued September 18, 2015 (Wolfsburg-headquartered Volkswagen AG; Audi AG; Volkswagen Group of America, Inc.). Martin Winterkorn resigned as CEO September 23, 2015. Volkswagen AG was convicted on its own plea before the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, *United States v. Volkswagen AG*, No. 16-cr-20394 (E.D. Mich.), plea agreement entered March 10, 2017, before Judge Sean F. Cox. German prosecutorial actions in Braunschweig and Munich, 2018 onward. Manufacturer: Volkswagen AG, Wolfsburg, Germany; Volkswagen Group of America, Herndon, Virginia; Audi AG, Ingolstadt; Porsche AG, Stuttgart-Zuffenhausen; tier-1 software supplier Robert Bosch GmbH, Stuttgart.
Case type: system/object alibi
Secondary case type (hybrid): partial scapegoat — James Liang (engineer) and Oliver Schmidt (compliance executive) were convicted and incarcerated in the United States; Martin Winterkorn (CEO) was indicted in the United States in 2018 but never extradited; the German Braunschweig fraud trial of Winterkorn was severed for medical reasons in 2021. The partial-scapegoat layer fires because individual engineers and a mid-level compliance manager bore criminal consequences while the executives and the supervisory board faced markedly weaker accountability under US procedure. See "Best counterargument" and "Open questions" for the explicit hybrid decision record.
Overlay: deliberate fraud. The DOJ Statement of Facts (filed January 11, 2017, attached to the plea agreement in *United States v. Volkswagen AG*, No. 16-cr-20394) establishes that Volkswagen AG, by and through its employees, willfully designed and concealed the defeat device — Volkswagen AG admitted to a § 371 conspiracy to defraud the United States, to obstruction of justice (§ 1512(c)(1)), and to importation by false statements (§ 542). The overlay matters for taxonomy because, unlike Therac-25 (where the system failed and was then blamed) or Boeing (where the certification process shielded named people), at Volkswagen the underlying conduct was a willful design choice with the corporation's own pleaded admission. The alibi structure still ran in three stages: "the software did the cheating" + "a small group of engineers acted alone" + "the corporation didn't know." All three sub-alibis are documented in the public record and all three were pierced. That makes Dieselgate the model demonstrator for the diagnostic — every laundering move is named, every interception is named, and the procedural record allows clean A-grade attribution at each step.

Crisis:
Between approximately 2006 and 2015, engineers at Volkswagen AG (and at
its US subsidiary Volkswagen Group of America) developed, deployed, and
maintained engine-control software in approximately 11 million diesel
vehicles worldwide that detected when the vehicle was undergoing
laboratory emissions testing and modified engine behavior to pass that
testing while emitting nitrogen oxides (NOx) at up to 40 times the US
Clean Air Act limit during normal road use. The software (a "defeat
device" under 42 U.S.C. § 7522(a)(3)(B) and 40 C.F.R. § 86.1854-12)
was installed across the 2.0L EA189 diesel engine family from model
year 2009 through 2015 and across 3.0L V6 TDI engines used in Audi,
Porsche, and Volkswagen platforms. The DOJ Statement of Facts filed
with the January 2017 plea agreement describes the device's mode
detection: it recognized the federal test procedure (FTP-75) steering-
wheel-stationary, throttle-position, and ambient-condition signatures
and activated a low-NOx engine map only during that mode. The estimated
human-health cost varies by methodology; peer-reviewed estimates (MIT,
Harvard) place the excess-NOx exposure attributable to the US fleet
alone at approximately 60 premature deaths and several hundred cases of
chronic bronchitis and respiratory hospitalization. Worldwide excess-
NOx estimates run an order of magnitude higher.

Official story:
Three stories ran in sequence and each was pierced before the next was
deployed.
1. **Technical issues / misunderstanding (May 2014 → September 2015).**
   After the ICCT-WVU report surfaced the road-vs-lab discrepancy in
   May 2014, Volkswagen responded to CARB and EPA with explanations
   framed as "technical issues" with the after-treatment system and
   later as candidate software fixes that would be issued via a recall.
   A December 2014 recall ("service action 23U1") was issued; CARB
   follow-up testing in spring 2015 confirmed that the recall did not
   close the road-vs-lab gap. Across this 16-month window, Volkswagen
   did not disclose the defeat-device architecture.
2. **A small group of engineers acted alone (September 2015 onward).**
   When Volkswagen confessed to CARB and EPA staff in a September 3,
   2015 meeting, the framing shifted to a containment narrative: a
   limited group of engineers had built the software, executives had
   not known, and the corporation would cooperate. This framing appears
   in the company's initial public statements after the September 18,
   2015 EPA Notice of Violation, and in early Winterkorn statements
   denying personal knowledge.
3. **The software is the cause.** Across the consumer-facing settlement
   period (2016–2017) and into the criminal plea, public-facing
   language repeatedly named "the defeat device" or "the software" as
   the cause. The reified-software framing is the system/object alibi
   layer — the same shape Bhopal teaches at the "plant" layer and
   Boeing teaches at the "certification process" layer.

Blame container:
The defeat-device software itself, in early framing, then a small group
of engineers (with James Liang named in the first criminal information,
filed September 2016), then a single compliance manager (Oliver Schmidt,
arrested January 2017 in Miami), then a small group of subsequent
indictees and the corporate entity. The fraud-overlay structure means
that the blame container partly *deserves* to be named — these
individuals did design and conceal the defeat device — but the container
stops at the engineering and compliance layers and protects the
strategic decisions made at the executive and supervisory-board level.
The criminal information against Winterkorn was filed by the US
Department of Justice in May 2018 (indictment unsealed against
Winterkorn and four other executives); Winterkorn has not been
extradited and remains in Germany. Of the corporate executives charged
by US authorities, only Oliver Schmidt was tried in the United States
(convicted December 2017, sentenced to seven years' imprisonment plus
$400,000 fine; released and deported to Germany in 2020). [Evidence
grade: A]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Volkswagen AG, Wolfsburg, holds the engineering authority for the
  EA189 and EA288 diesel families and the V6 TDI platforms. The DOJ
  Statement of Facts (attached to the January 2017 plea) identifies
  named VW engineering managers as the approvers of the original
  defeat-device strategy in 2006 when VW concluded it could not meet
  US NOx limits with cost-acceptable after-treatment hardware. Robert
  Bosch GmbH developed the underlying engine-control software
  framework; Bosch personnel are identified in DOJ filings as having
  warned VW in 2007–2008 that what Bosch was being asked to enable
  could constitute an illegal defeat device under US law, and Bosch
  paid $327.5 million in a US settlement in 2017. Volkswagen Group of
  America held US-market compliance authority and was the entity that
  filed certificates of conformity with EPA, including the false COCs
  for model years 2009–2015. The day-to-day decisions to retain and
  re-deploy the defeat-device software across model-year refreshes
  (and to bring it forward into 3.0L V6 platforms) were made inside
  VW AG engineering.

  Benefit:
  Volkswagen AG — through diesel-vehicle sales volume that VW could
  not have achieved at compliant NOx levels using the hardware
  architectures VW had chosen for cost reasons. The DOJ plea
  documents the $11–$15 billion in 2016 consumer settlement plus the
  $2.8 billion criminal penalty plus $1.5 billion civil penalty;
  these figures are downstream of more than a decade of US diesel
  sales that depended on the false COCs. The "clean diesel"
  marketing campaign (2008–2015) positioned VW diesels as a
  consumer-friendly low-emission alternative to gasoline and
  hybrid vehicles; that market position was a strategic differentiator
  for VW in the US. Executives, including Winterkorn, benefited
  through performance-linked compensation tied to US-market growth.
  Audi AG and Porsche AG benefited through the V6 TDI program.

  Knowledge:
  Documented inside Volkswagen across multiple levels and over a
  decade:
  - 2006: defeat-device strategy adopted as the engineering response
    to the EA189's inability to meet US Tier 2 Bin 5 NOx limits with
    the hardware architecture VW had budgeted. DOJ Statement of Facts.
  - 2007–2008: Bosch warns VW in writing that the requested software
    behavior could be an illegal defeat device. Bosch's communication
    is referenced in DOJ filings and in the Bosch settlement.
  - May 2014: ICCT–WVU report (Thompson, Carder, Besch, Thiruvengadam,
    Kappanna, "In-Use Emissions Testing of Light-Duty Diesel
    Vehicles in the United States," CAFEE, West Virginia University,
    May 15, 2014) surfaces the road-vs-lab discrepancy. VW's internal
    response is the "technical issues" framing.
  - 2014–2015: VW engineering and management discuss possible
    explanations; the German management board and supervisory board
    receive briefings. The exact dates and content of executive-level
    briefings are the subject of the Braunschweig and Munich
    prosecutions and the still-private portions of the Jones Day
    work product.
  - September 3, 2015: VW confesses to CARB and EPA staff in a meeting
    in El Monte, California.
  - September 18, 2015: EPA Notice of Violation issued.
  The 16-month gap between independent detection (May 2014) and
  confession (September 2015) is itself the most legible knowledge
  evidence — it documents what Volkswagen knew, when it knew, and
  what it chose to say in the interim.

  Preventability:
  - At 2006: choose to engineer to the compliance standard rather
    than to a defeat-device workaround. The original engineering
    decision is the root preventability point.
  - At 2007–2008: heed the Bosch warning. The supplier raised the
    illegality risk in writing; VW chose to proceed.
  - At any model-year refresh between 2009 and 2015: redesign the
    after-treatment system to meet NOx limits without defeat-device
    operation. VW's diesel competitors (notably BMW and Mercedes
    diesel vehicles on the same routes in the ICCT-WVU testing)
    did meet limits, demonstrating that compliant hardware existed.
    The ICCT-WVU report names a BMW X5 diesel as the compliant
    reference vehicle.
  - In May 2014: respond truthfully to CARB and EPA inquiries. The
    "technical issues" response is the proximate preventability point
    for the 16-month investigation window.
  - On the broader chain: install internal compliance controls
    capable of detecting that the corporation was running a
    multi-year fraud across an 11-million-vehicle fleet. The Jones
    Day internal investigation, commissioned by Volkswagen in
    September 2015, was a retrospective acknowledgment of the
    absence of those controls.

  Record controller:
  Multiple layers, jurisdictionally fragmented but cleaner than
  Bhopal:
  - Volkswagen AG internal engineering and management archives,
    Wolfsburg. Volkswagen has consistently resisted disclosure of
    the full Jones Day work product; portions appear in the German
    criminal proceedings under judicial protective order.
  - US Department of Justice — DOJ Statement of Facts (January 11,
    2017) and superseding plea documents; PACER docket *United States
    v. Volkswagen AG*, 4:16-cr-20394 (E.D. Mich.). Volkswagen AG and
    related entities have the corporate plea agreement on record.
  - Environmental Protection Agency — EPA Notice of Violation
    (September 18, 2015) and the EPA's enforcement docket.
  - California Air Resources Board — CARB September 18, 2015 notice
    and subsequent CARB enforcement materials.
  - US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations — 2016
    hearing record on the VW Group's German-tax and US-emissions
    practices.
  - US Securities and Exchange Commission — March 14, 2019 civil
    complaint against Volkswagen AG and Winterkorn for securities
    fraud (settled August 2020).
  - German prosecutors in Braunschweig (Lower Saxony) hold the
    Winterkorn fraud-prosecution record; Munich prosecutors hold
    related Audi indictments (including Rupert Stadler).
  Critically, the US criminal docket is *public* and *primary*, in
  contrast to Bhopal where the most important corporate documents
  remained in private US archives. This is a load-bearing record
  advantage and the reason Dieselgate teaches the diagnostic
  cleanly.

  Cost bearer:
  Three layers of cost bearers:
  1. **Excess-NOx exposure populations.** Communities along high-
     traffic corridors bear elevated NOx exposure; peer-reviewed
     estimates (Barrett et al., MIT/Harvard, *Environmental Research
     Letters*, 2015 and 2017) attribute approximately 60 premature
     US deaths and several hundred cases of chronic bronchitis to
     the US-fleet excess-NOx exposure across the 2009–2015 period.
     European-fleet estimates are higher, reflecting greater diesel
     penetration and higher population density along urban
     corridors. These are the unaccused, uncompensated cost bearers
     — they did not buy the vehicles and did not appear in the
     settlements.
  2. **US consumers who bought 600,000 affected vehicles.** Received
     compensation under the 2016 settlement (buyback or fix plus
     a per-vehicle compensation payment, total approximately $14.7
     billion). Compensation is the cleanest layer.
  3. **VW workers and dealers.** Bore reputational, business, and
     employment cost; Wolfsburg-area production schedules were
     disrupted; some VW dealers reported sales declines through 2017.

How the alibi hardened:
1. **Compliance-as-paperwork.** VW Group of America filed certificates
   of conformity with EPA for each model year. The COC architecture
   assumes manufacturer truthfulness; EPA does its own confirmatory
   testing on a sample basis but does not road-test every powertrain.
   The procedural shell allowed the fraud to scale across an 11-
   million-vehicle fleet.
2. **"Clean diesel" market story.** VW's US marketing campaign
   (2008–2015) framed diesel as the environmentally responsible
   alternative to hybrids. Trade-press coverage amplified the
   framing. Consumer NGOs did not have independent road-testing
   capacity to challenge the marketing.
3. **Compliance-test architecture.** The federal test procedure
   (FTP-75) is a standardized laboratory dynamometer protocol.
   Vehicles know they are on a dynamometer because of detectable
   conditions (steering-wheel motion absent; throttle profile
   matches the FTP-75 schedule; constant ambient conditions). The
   defeat-device's mode detection exploited this architecture. The
   alibi-hardening structural feature is that EPA's testing program
   was *the* compliance pathway and lacked independent in-use
   confirmation.
4. **"Technical issues" framing during investigation.** Across the
   16-month window from May 2014 to September 2015, VW's response
   to CARB and EPA framed the discrepancy as a technical anomaly
   to be addressed by a software recall. The December 2014 recall
   ("service action 23U1") performed the appearance of remediation
   while leaving the defeat-device architecture intact.
5. **"Rogue engineers" containment story (September 2015).** When
   confession became unavoidable, the corporation initially framed
   the fraud as a small-group action without executive knowledge.
   The Liang plea (September 2016) and Schmidt arrest (January
   2017) gave the framing operational weight: the criminal docket
   moved against engineers and one compliance manager before it
   moved against executives.

How the alibi weakened:
1. **ICCT–WVU 2014 road testing.** The load-bearing interception.
   The International Council on Clean Transportation commissioned
   CAFEE at West Virginia University to perform on-road portable-
   emissions-measurement-system (PEMS) testing of three light-duty
   diesel vehicles in May 2014 (a VW Jetta, a VW Passat, and a BMW
   X5). The Jetta and Passat exceeded US NOx limits by factors of
   15–35 in actual driving while the BMW X5 met limits. The CAFEE
   report (Thompson, Carder, Besch, Thiruvengadam, Kappanna, "In-Use
   Emissions Testing of Light-Duty Diesel Vehicles in the United
   States," May 15, 2014) provided the empirical anomaly that CARB
   and EPA pursued for 16 months. Independent technical
   verification is the canonical interception type for system/object
   alibis (per `.claude/skills/counter-case-method/SKILL.md`).
2. **CARB persistence (May 2014 → September 2015).** CARB ran
   confirmatory testing, demanded responses, ran the December 2014
   recall, then re-tested, then escalated. CARB's persistence
   converted the ICCT-WVU empirical anomaly into a regulator-
   actionable case.
3. **VW September 3, 2015 confession.** A meeting in El Monte,
   California between CARB staff, EPA staff, and Volkswagen
   representatives in which VW confessed to the defeat-device
   architecture. The confession was driven by CARB's announcement
   that without an explanation it would withhold model-year 2016
   certifications. The confession is the moment the defeat-device
   alibi collapsed.
4. **EPA Notice of Violation, September 18, 2015.** The public
   procedural action that converted the confession into a record.
   Press coverage and Winterkorn's resignation (September 23, 2015)
   followed.
5. **DOJ criminal plea, January 11, 2017.** Volkswagen AG entered a
   guilty plea (entered before Judge Sean F. Cox in the U.S.
   District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan on March 10,
   2017, with the plea agreement and Statement of Facts filed
   January 11, 2017) to three felony counts — conspiracy to defraud
   the United States (18 U.S.C. § 371), obstruction of justice (18
   U.S.C. § 1512(c)(1)), and importation by false statements (18
   U.S.C. § 542). The Statement of Facts is the public document
   establishing the chain of internal decisions.
6. **Named-individual indictments and convictions.**
   - James Liang (engineer): pleaded guilty September 9, 2016
     (E.D. Mich., No. 16-cr-20393); sentenced August 25, 2017 to
     40 months' imprisonment and $200,000 fine.
   - Oliver Schmidt (compliance executive, former head of VWGoA's
     Engineering and Environmental Office in Auburn Hills,
     Michigan): arrested January 7, 2017 in Miami; pleaded guilty
     August 4, 2017; sentenced December 6, 2017 to seven years'
     imprisonment and $400,000 fine.
   - Martin Winterkorn (CEO): indicted in the United States May 3,
     2018 (E.D. Mich., No. 18-cr-20275) on charges of conspiracy
     to defraud the United States, wire fraud, and Clean Air Act
     violations. Indicted alongside four other VW executives.
     Has not been extradited. Indicted separately in Germany in
     Braunschweig (April 2019) on charges of commercial fraud,
     securities-market manipulation, and breach of trust. The
     Braunschweig fraud trial against Winterkorn opened September 16,
     2021 but was severed for medical reasons. A second proceeding
     opened September 2024 and was suspended due to his health and
     a subsequent home accident; as of May 2026, Winterkorn has not
     been adjudicated on the German charges. He continues to deny
     involvement.
   - Rupert Stadler (Audi AG CEO): indicted in Munich July 2019;
     convicted June 2023 of fraud by negligence; received a
     suspended 21-month sentence and €1.1 million fine.
7. **Jones Day internal investigation and Volkswagen's own
   admissions.** Volkswagen commissioned Jones Day to conduct an
   internal investigation in September 2015. The full report has
   never been publicly released; redacted portions and witness
   accounts have entered the US and German court records. The
   internal investigation's existence and the company's resistance
   to its disclosure are themselves evidence of the record-control
   layer of the chain.
8. **Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations 2016.** The
   PSI hearing record documents the chain of knowledge across VW
   Group of America and Wolfsburg engineering.

Best counterargument:
"This is pure deliberate fraud, not laundering. Naming this as a
system/object alibi softens the moral case by abstracting individual
criminal acts into a 'pattern.' Liang and Schmidt are in prison;
Winterkorn was indicted and is being prosecuted in Germany; Bosch
paid $327 million; the corporation paid almost $30 billion across
settlements and penalties; this is what working accountability looks
like. The book's framing of 'laundering' obscures rather than
illuminates what happened here."

Shirley's response: the test under the book's taxonomy is whether
the public-facing causal language and the procedural shell
*temporarily protected* responsibility-holders, not whether
accountability eventually reached them. Three sub-alibis ran in
sequence: the "technical issues" framing protected VW for 16 months;
the "rogue engineers" framing protected VW executives for at least
the first six months of the criminal docket; the "defeat-device
software" framing protected the design and approval chain across the
consumer settlement period. Each sub-alibi was eventually pierced —
that is the diagnostic chapter's point. The piercing is what beat 9
("the escape") teaches. The fact that some named individuals went to
prison while the CEO has not been adjudicated in the United States,
that the Jones Day work product remains substantially private, and
that Bosch's responsibility was settled rather than tried, all
demonstrate that even in the cleanest fraud case in modern
automotive history, the chain stopped short of full attribution. The
hybrid classification (system/object alibi with deliberate-fraud
overlay; partial-scapegoat secondary) is the honest one.

What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead
of visibility?
Responsibility would attach to the engineering managers who
authorized the 2006 defeat-device strategy; to the executives who
were briefed across 2014–2015 and chose the "technical issues"
response; to the supervisory-board members who received briefings
and authorized the multi-year continuation; to Bosch personnel who
wrote the warning memos and continued the contract; to the VW Group
of America compliance staff who filed false certificates of
conformity. It would attach less heavily to James Liang, the
engineer who pleaded guilty and served 40 months. Liang is in the
chain — he wrote code that did what it did — but the chain runs
upward through the people who told him what the engine had to do
and accepted what they were told it did.

  Interception mechanism:
  - Type: independent technical verification (primary) + regulator

  - Named institutional actor: the International Council on Clean

    motivating hypothesis was that the US cars were "clean";
    finding that two of three were not was the surprise. The PEMS
    methodology converted a research question into an enforcement-

  Cost of escape (the chapter's beat 9 cost enumeration):
  - Time from defeat-device deployment (model year 2009) to
    interception (May 2014): approximately five model years.
  - Time from interception (May 2014) to confession (September
    2015): 16 months. During this window, VW continued selling
    affected 2015 model-year vehicles in the US and Europe.
  - Time from confession to first individual conviction (Liang,
    September 2016): one year. To first executive incarceration
    (Schmidt, December 2017): two years and three months. To
    failed extradition outcome on Winterkorn (ongoing as of
    2026-05-25): more than 10 years.
  - Direct financial cost to Volkswagen: approximately $30 billion
    across criminal penalty ($2.8B), civil penalty ($1.5B), US
    consumer settlement ($14.7B), and subsequent global
    settlements.
  - Cost to ICCT and CAFEE: the program lead at CAFEE later
    described receiving career-threatening pressure during the
    investigation window; the WVU testing budget was small
    (approximately $50,000 for the on-road study, per CAFEE
    accounts in subsequent journalism) — disproportionate to the
    enforcement impact, which is why this counter-case teaches a
    lesson about *the leverage of independent verification* rather
    than a lesson about its abundance.
  - Cost absorbed by cost bearers who could not wait: excess-NOx
    exposure across 2009–2015 model years cannot be recovered;
    peer-reviewed estimates of excess mortality stand.
  - Cost to Bosch: $327.5 million settlement; reputational damage.

  Outcome:
  - Partial reversal at the corporate and procedural level:
    corporate plea, financial settlement, regulatory tightening
    (the in-use compliance regime under the European RDE — Real
    Driving Emissions — rulemaking, effective 2017, is a direct
    response).
  - Partial accountability at the named-individual level: Liang
    and Schmidt convicted and incarcerated in the US; Stadler
    convicted in Munich (suspended sentence); Winterkorn indicted
    in both jurisdictions but with US extradition failed and German
    proceedings severed for medical reasons. The supervisory-board
    members briefed across 2014–2015 face no individual
    adjudication.
  - Recognition without full remedy: the cost bearers in the
    excess-NOx exposure population are uncompensated.

  Transferable lesson (diagnostic, not motivational):
  When the compliance-test architecture for a regulated technology
  is laboratory-only and the manufacturer controls the certification
  filings, independent in-use testing by a financially disinterested
  technical body is the interception mechanism that can break the
  alibi. The lesson generalizes to any compliance regime where the
  regulator depends on the regulated entity's self-report. For
  chapter 3, this is the reader-portable form of the diagnostic's
  "knowledge" and "preventability" questions: if independent
  verification existed and was funded, what would it find?

  Limits of the lesson:
  - The ICCT/CAFEE intervention required a financially independent
    body with technical capacity. Many regulated domains have no
    such body.
  - The 16-month enforcement window between detection and
    confession is the *fast* version of this pattern. In the
    historical record (Bhopal, Therac-25, Boeing 737 MAX), the
    detection-to-attribution window runs years to decades.
  - Independent verification works against fraud that produces
    *physically measurable* discrepancies (NOx emissions, radiation
    dose, software inputs). It does not work directly against fraud
    in domains where the outcome is contested or non-measurable.
