# Boeing 737 MAX — Case File

Case name: Boeing 737 MAX MCAS crashes; FAA certification failure; DOJ deferred prosecution agreement and subsequent non-prosecution agreement.
Domain: commercial aerospace; software-driven flight control (MCAS); FAA Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) regime; federal criminal fraud (18 U.S.C. § 371 — conspiracy to defraud the United States).
Dates and place: Crashes: Lion Air Flight JT 610, Java Sea off Karawang, Indonesia, October 29, 2018 (189 fatalities); Ethiopian Airlines Flight ET 302, Bishoftu near Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, March 10, 2019 (157 fatalities; aircraft destroyed; total 346 deaths across the two crashes). FAA grounding of US 737 MAX fleet: March 13, 2019 (third day after ET 302, last major regulator to ground after CAAC, EASA, CAA-UK). DOJ DPA: filed January 7, 2021, United States v. The Boeing Company, N.D. Tex. (case no. 4:21-cr-00005-O, Judge Reed O'Connor). DOJ breach finding: communicated May 14, 2024. Boeing plea agreement: July 2024 (rejected by Judge O'Connor, December 2024, on monitor-selection grounds). DOJ non-prosecution agreement (NPA): announced May 23, 2025. Fifth Circuit appellate ruling (rejecting victim-family CVRA appeal): March 31, 2026. Manufacturer: The Boeing Company, Commercial Airplanes division, Renton, Washington (737 MAX final assembly).
Case type: system/object alibi
Hybrid note (per `.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`): primary is system/object alibi; a secondary partial-scapegoat layer fired briefly in Boeing's early framing of the Lion Air and Ethiopian flight crews, but that layer collapsed within months when the ET 302 crew (executing the post-Lion-Air procedure as written) was still unable to recover. The primary classification holds because the load-bearing alibi across the full lifetime of the case is the certification process and its subsequent DPA-NPA procedural shell, not the pilots.

Classification note: The Boeing case teaches system/object alibi at the *certification-process* layer, in contrast to Therac-25's machine-layer alibi. The reified non-human agent here is not the MCAS software alone but the FAA's Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) regime — the delegated-certification framework under which Boeing employees, acting as "Authorized Representatives" of the FAA, signed off on Boeing-built systems. When the crashes came, the public-facing causal language pointed first to pilot error (the Lion Air and Ethiopian crews "didn't follow runaway-stabilizer procedure"), then to a software anomaly ("MCAS misfired on a single AOA sensor input"), and only under sustained congressional and journalistic pressure to the certification choices made by named people inside Boeing and the FAA. The DOJ DPA of January 2021 names two Boeing 737 Chief Technical Pilots — Mark Forkner and Patrik Gustavsson — as the named individuals who allegedly deceived the FAA Aircraft Evaluation Group about MCAS's expanded control authority. Forkner was acquitted at trial in March 2022. The institutional decisions that approved MCAS's single-sensor architecture, that removed the speed-only authority limitation, and that kept MCAS out of the pilot training manual were never personally adjudicated. The alibi the book teaches readers to recognise here is the *delegated-certification regime itself* — a procedural shell that converted Boeing's own engineers into FAA authority, then absorbed the blame when the safety judgment failed.

```text
Crisis:
On October 29, 2018, Lion Air Flight JT 610 — a 737 MAX 8 — crashed
into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff from Jakarta. All 189
people aboard were killed. The KNKT (Indonesia's National Transportation
Safety Committee) final report (October 2019) found that the Maneuvering
Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) had repeatedly trimmed the
horizontal stabilizer nose-down based on erroneous angle-of-attack (AOA)
data from a single, miscalibrated AOA sensor, ultimately exceeding the
crew's ability to recover. Boeing's initial public response emphasized
pilot procedure ("the crew did not follow the existing runaway-
stabilizer procedure") and Lion Air's maintenance record.

On March 10, 2019 — four months and twelve days later — Ethiopian
Airlines Flight ET 302, also a 737 MAX 8, crashed near Bishoftu six
minutes after takeoff from Addis Ababa. All 157 people aboard were
killed. The ECAA (Ethiopian Civil Aviation Authority) interim report
(March 2020) and final report (December 2022) documented the same MCAS
nose-down trim sequence triggered by erroneous AOA data, this time on a
flight crew that had received post-Lion-Air guidance on the runaway
stabilizer procedure. The MCAS architecture had not been changed in the
intervening period; the FAA-mandated software fix was not yet deployed.

The two crashes killed 346 people. The 737 MAX was grounded globally by
March 13, 2019, with the FAA grounding last among major regulators —
twelve days after the FAA had publicly affirmed continued airworthiness
on March 11, 2019. The grounding lasted twenty months in the United
States; the FAA cleared the MAX to return to service on November 18,
2020, after design changes including dual-AOA-sensor input to MCAS,
single-activation limits, crew-controllable disengagement, and revised
flight-crew training.

Official story (Boeing's initial framing, Oct 2018 – Mar 2019):
Boeing's communications across the four-and-a-half months between the
crashes followed a template that named two non-Boeing causes:

(a) Foreign-airline pilot training and procedural adherence. Boeing
officials including then-CEO Dennis Muilenburg told investors and the
press that the existing runaway-stabilizer memory item should have been
sufficient to recover from any MCAS-induced trim runaway. Muilenburg's
November 2018 statements emphasized that "we provide all the information
that's needed to safely fly our aircraft" (Muilenburg, conference call,
Nov. 2018, per Seattle Times reporting).

(b) Sensor failure (single AOA vane) and isolated maintenance. Boeing's
Operations Manual Bulletin (TBC-19), issued November 6, 2018 after the
Lion Air crash, characterised MCAS as an isolated edge-case interaction
with erroneous AOA input — language that treated the AOA sensor failure
as the proximate cause and the MCAS architecture as functioning within
design parameters.

Between the two crashes, neither Boeing nor the FAA grounded the
fleet. The "pilot error" framing persisted in Boeing's external
communications through ET 302; Muilenburg's first statement after ET
302 (March 10, 2019) said Boeing had "full confidence in the safety of
the 737 MAX." Boeing did not formally acknowledge MCAS's role in the
ET 302 sequence until April 4, 2019 (Muilenburg statement). The FAA
grounding came March 13, 2019 — after CAAC (March 11), EASA, and the
UK CAA had already grounded.

Blame container:
Three sequential blame containers, in this order:

1. Foreign-airline pilots. Boeing's initial communications named the
   Lion Air and (briefly) the Ethiopian crews as causal — characterising
   the runaway-stabilizer procedure as sufficient and the crews as
   failing to execute it. This framing collapsed for the Ethiopian
   accident within weeks (the crew DID execute the procedure as written
   and were still unable to recover) and was the first container to
   crack. It never moved beyond partial: the crews remained named in
   trade-press coverage and Boeing internal communications through 2019.

2. MCAS-as-system. After the pilot-error framing failed, Boeing's
   public-facing language shifted to "MCAS architecture" as the named
   cause — language that reified the software as the agent. This is the
   system/object alibi in its midstream form: the software "took inputs
   from a single sensor," "commanded repeated trim inputs," "failed
   safely." The agency is placed on the artifact. The named individuals
   who chose the single-sensor input, who removed the earlier low-speed-
   only authority limitation in 2016, and who decided not to disclose
   MCAS in the pilot training manual were not personally named in
   Boeing's public account.

3. The FAA's delegated-certification process. The third and most
   load-bearing alibi is procedural rather than mechanical: the FAA's
   ODA framework under which Boeing employees, acting with delegated
   FAA authority, certified Boeing's own aircraft. The Seattle Times
   reporting (March 17, 2019, "Flawed analysis, failed oversight" by
   Dominic Gates, Mike Baker, and others) and the House Committee on
   T&I final report (September 2020) document that FAA technical staff
   were excluded from key MCAS reviews; that the System Safety Analysis
   for MCAS understated its control authority by a factor of roughly
   four; and that Boeing's ODA Authorized Representatives signed off on
   the analysis without independent FAA technical review. The blame
   container here is "the certification process" — a procedural shell
   that absorbs responsibility while the named individuals who designed
   the process, the named Boeing employees who acted as FAA authority,
   and the named FAA managers who chose not to escalate are individually
   un-prosecuted.

House Committee on T&I Final Report Sept 2020; DOJ DPA Statement of
Facts (Jan 2021); Seattle Times Pulitzer-winning series Mar 2019.]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Boeing Commercial Airplanes — design and certification of the 737 MAX
  including MCAS. The 737 MAX program was launched in 2011 as a
  derivative of the 737 NG, with the larger and more forward-mounted
  LEAP-1B engines requiring an augmentation system to maintain
  longitudinal stability at high angle-of-attack. The MCAS design was
  modified in 2016 to expand its authority to a wider flight envelope
  (low-Mach-number authority was added; the speed-only limit was
  removed) — this modification was not flagged to FAA reviewers as a
  safety-significant change, per the House Committee 2020 report.
  Single-AOA-sensor architecture for the MCAS input was a Boeing
  engineering choice; the redundant-AOA option existed in the cockpit
  hardware (the AOA Disagree alert) but was inadvertently linked to an
  optional add-on package, a fact Boeing did not disclose to operators
  until after the Lion Air crash. The pilot training package omission of
  MCAS was driven by Boeing's stated objective to qualify the MAX for
  the same Type Rating as prior 737 generations, avoiding simulator
  training — a commercial differentiator Boeing had committed to
  Southwest Airlines (per DOJ DPA Statement of Facts, ¶¶ 17–28).

  FAA Aircraft Certification Service and the ODA framework under FAA
  Order 8110.4C — the regulatory authority that delegated MCAS-related
  certification decisions to Boeing employees acting as FAA Authorized
  Representatives. FAA technical staff who flagged concerns were, per
  the House Committee 2020 report, overruled or excluded from key
  reviews. The FAA Aircraft Evaluation Group (AEG), the body that
  approved the pilot training and flight-crew operating manual content
  for the MAX, was the locus of the alleged Boeing fraud per the DOJ
  DPA: Boeing's 737 Chief Technical Pilots Mark Forkner and Patrik
  Gustavsson are alleged in the Statement of Facts to have misled the
  AEG about MCAS's expanded authority.

  Benefit:
  Boeing Commercial Airplanes — the 737 MAX was Boeing's response to
  the Airbus A320neo and was projected (at the 2017–2018 peak of order
  intake) to generate hundreds of billions of dollars in revenue
  across the program's life. Avoiding simulator training was a
  commercial differentiator that Boeing internally valued; the DPA
  Statement of Facts cites the Southwest Airlines commitment as a
  motivating factor in the AEG-deception conduct (DPA ¶¶ 17–28).
  Boeing's senior executives during the period of the certification
  decisions (Muilenburg as CEO from 2015; Kevin McAllister as Boeing
  Commercial Airplanes CEO during the crashes) retained substantial
  compensation through the events; Muilenburg's separation package on
  his December 2019 ouster was reported at approximately $62 million in
  stock and pension benefits.

  Knowledge:
  Per the DOJ DPA Statement of Facts, Boeing's 737 Chief Technical
  Pilots knew by November 2016 that MCAS had been modified to operate
  at a wider flight envelope including low-speed conditions. The
  Statement of Facts alleges that Forkner became aware MCAS could
  activate at low speeds during a simulator session and did not
  communicate this expansion to the FAA AEG. Boeing engineering
  knowledge of MCAS's single-sensor architecture and its control
  authority was distributed across the program; FOIA'd internal
  communications reported by the Seattle Times (Gates and Baker, March
  17, 2019, and subsequent pieces) include Boeing engineer text
  messages expressing safety concern about MCAS prior to the Lion Air
  crash. Boeing's senior leadership knowledge prior to the Lion Air
  crash is contested in the litigation record; the DPA does not allege
  C-suite knowledge of the AEG deception, a fact the House Committee
  report treats critically.

  After Lion Air (Oct 2018), Boeing's knowledge of MCAS's role was
  not contested. The decision not to ground the fleet between Lion Air
  and Ethiopian — and Boeing's continued public characterisation of
  MCAS as compliant with existing flight-crew procedures — was made
  with full knowledge of the prior overdose-pattern equivalent.

  Preventability:
  The crashes were preventable at multiple architectural and procedural
  points:
  - Pre-certification (2012–2017): A dual-AOA-sensor input to MCAS would
    have prevented single-sensor-failure triggering. The DPA Statement
    of Facts and House Committee report document that the dual-sensor
    architecture was technically available and was, in fact, the design
    Boeing implemented post-grounding for the MAX return to service.
  - Pre-certification: Disclosure of MCAS to operators and inclusion in
    flight-crew training would have permitted crews to recognise and
    respond to MCAS-induced trim without the runaway-stabilizer
    diagnostic gap.
  - Post-Lion Air (Nov 2018 – Mar 2019): Grounding the 737 MAX fleet
    after the Lion Air crash would have prevented the Ethiopian crash.
    Both Boeing and the FAA had access to the KNKT preliminary findings
    (November 2018) and the Boeing internal MCAS analysis by January
    2019. The decision not to ground was a discretionary call made by
    named FAA officials (in coordination with Boeing); the House
    Committee report (Sept 2020) documents this as a critical
    preventability gap.

  Record controller:
  Boeing controlled (i) the 737 MAX engineering record, (ii) the
  pre-2018 internal communications including the Forkner-Gustavsson
  text messages later cited by DOJ, (iii) the certification submission
  documents that Boeing's ODA Authorized Representatives signed.
  FAA controlled (i) the type certification record, (ii) the AEG
  approval files, (iii) the post-crash investigative reports and the
  return-to-service determinations. KNKT (Indonesia) controlled the
  Lion Air investigation; ECAA (Ethiopia) controlled the Ethiopian
  investigation; the NTSB participated as accredited representative in
  both under ICAO Annex 13. The House Committee on T&I controlled the
  congressional investigative record (interviews, document subpoenas,
  September 2020 final report). DOJ controlled the criminal docket,
  including the Statement of Facts in the DPA and the 2024 breach
  determination.

  Cost bearer:
  346 named victims across the two crashes — 189 on Lion Air JT 610
  (October 29, 2018) and 157 on Ethiopian ET 302 (March 10, 2019).
  Victims' families have pursued civil litigation against Boeing (largely
  consolidated in the N.D. Illinois MDL and Cook County state court);
  Boeing reached settlements with most families though precise terms
  vary. The victim families' Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA) claim
  before Judge O'Connor — that the original DPA was negotiated without
  CVRA-required victim consultation — was the basis for ongoing
  judicial scrutiny of the case; the Fifth Circuit's March 31, 2026
  ruling held that the families could not use CVRA to undo the 2025
  NPA. FAA technical staff who raised internal concerns about MCAS and
  were overruled bore institutional and professional cost. The Lion Air
  and Ethiopian Airlines flight crews — particularly the Lion Air crew —
  bore reputational cost during the months when Boeing's framing
  attributed the crash to procedural failure.

How the alibi hardened:
1. The ODA framework's procedural opacity. Authorized Representatives
   acting "as the FAA" are an institutional fiction that converts
   commercial-firm employees into regulatory authority. The framework
   was designed in part to address the agency's technical-staffing
   limitations, and it has produced safe certifications across most
   transport-category aircraft. The system/object alibi here is that
   the *procedure* becomes the named cause when the substantive
   judgment fails — the "ODA process worked as designed" framing
   protects the named individuals who exercised the delegated authority.
2. The Type Rating commercial pressure. Boeing's commitment to
   Southwest Airlines that the MAX would not require simulator training
   was, per the DOJ DPA Statement of Facts, a commercial constraint that
   shaped how Boeing presented MCAS to the FAA AEG. The pressure was
   contractual and therefore difficult to attribute to any single
   decision-maker; it diffused responsibility across the 737 program.
3. The "pilot error" framing's plausibility window. The Lion Air crew's
   maintenance and procedural execution had documented gaps; per the
   KNKT final report, the AOA sensor that triggered MCAS had been
   replaced with a miscalibrated unit by maintenance. This created a
   legitimate causal layer (the maintenance failure) that Boeing's
   framing exploited to draw attention away from the architectural
   question of why a single AOA sensor input could repeatedly trim the
   stabilizer to crash.
4. The first-grounding inertia. Between Lion Air and Ethiopian, neither
   Boeing nor the FAA grounded the fleet. The decision is documented in
   the House Committee report and in FOIA'd internal FAA communications.
   The "we issued a service bulletin" framing absorbed the institutional
   response within the existing certification regime, deferring the
   broader architectural question.

How the alibi weakened:
1. The Ethiopian Airlines crash itself (March 10, 2019). The ET 302
   crew, having received the post-Lion-Air guidance, executed the
   procedures Boeing's framing had said would be sufficient. The
   recurrence of the MCAS trim sequence on a procedurally-correct flight
   crew collapsed the pilot-error framing within weeks.
2. Gates, Dominic, Mike Baker, Steve Miletich, and Lewis Kamb.
   "Flawed analysis, failed oversight: How Boeing, FAA certified the
   suspect 737 MAX flight control system." *Seattle Times*, March 17,
   2019. This is the Pulitzer-winning piece (Pulitzer for Investigative
   Reporting, 2020) that first publicly documented the FAA-ODA
   certification gaps, including FAA technical staff being overruled
   and the System Safety Analysis understatement of MCAS authority by
   approximately a factor of four. The subsequent series across 2019–
   2020 documented the internal Boeing engineer concerns, the
   Forkner-Gustavsson text messages, and the AEG-deception pattern.
3. House Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure. *The Boeing
   737 MAX Aircraft: Costs, Consequences, and Lessons from its Design,
   Development, and Certification — Final Committee Report*. September
   2020. The 245-page report, chaired by Rep. Peter DeFazio, documents
   five "broken processes" at Boeing and the FAA: production pressures,
   faulty design and performance assumptions, culture of concealment,
   conflicts of interest within the ODA process, and FAA's grossly
   insufficient oversight. The report is the load-bearing institutional
   account.
4. KNKT (Komite Nasional Keselamatan Transportasi, Indonesia). *Aircraft
   Accident Investigation Report: PT Lion Mentari Airlines Boeing 737-8
   (MAX); PK-LQP; Tanjung Karawang, West Java, Republic of Indonesia,
   29 October 2018*. KNKT.18.10.35.04. Released October 2019. The
   primary tier-1 source for Lion Air causation.
5. ECAA (Ethiopian Civil Aviation Authority). *Aircraft Accident
   Investigation Bureau Final Report on Accident to the Boeing 737-8
   MAX Registered ET-AVJ Operated by Ethiopian Airlines, 10 March 2019*.
   Released December 2022 (final; interim March 2020). The primary
   tier-1 source for Ethiopian causation.
6. United States v. The Boeing Company. Deferred Prosecution Agreement,
   N.D. Tex., Case No. 4:21-cr-00005-O, filed January 7, 2021. Statement
   of Facts ¶¶ 1–63. Boeing admits to conspiracy to defraud the United
   States (18 U.S.C. § 371) through the conduct of two 737 Chief
   Technical Pilots in their dealings with the FAA AEG. Boeing agreed
   to pay $2.5 billion ($243.6 million criminal fine; $1.77 billion in
   compensation to airline customers; $500 million to the crash-victims
   beneficiaries' fund).
7. DOJ breach finding, May 14, 2024. The Department of Justice notified
   the court that Boeing had breached the 2021 DPA by failing to
   "design, implement, and enforce a compliance and ethics program to
   prevent and detect violations of the U.S. fraud laws throughout its
   operations." The breach finding followed the January 2024 Alaska
   Airlines Flight 1282 door-plug blowout — an event outside the DPA's
   covered conduct but cited by DOJ as evidence of continued compliance
   failure. The DPA alibi began to weaken here: the procedural shell
   Boeing had entered into was itself failing on its own terms.

Best counterargument:
"Boeing 737 MAX is the textbook ODA-regulation case, not a system/object
alibi. The DOJ DPA convicted Boeing in fact (though not in formal
guilty plea), the House Committee report named specific procedural
failures at Boeing and the FAA, Congress passed the Aircraft
Certification, Safety, and Accountability Act in December 2020 that
overhauled the ODA framework, and Boeing paid $2.5 billion. The named
people — Muilenburg, McAllister, Forkner, Gustavsson — were named in
the public record. Calling this 'system/object alibi' overstates the
laundering: accountability did climb, the process did work (slowly),
and the named cause was correctly assigned to Boeing's certification
choices. The remaining question is whether the 2025 NPA was an
appropriate disposition, not whether the underlying responsibility was
laundered."

Shirley's response: The counterargument is its strongest at the
DPA-stage of the institutional response (January 2021) and weakest at
the 2025 NPA-stage. Between these two stages, the documented sequence
is: (a) Boeing agreed to a DPA without a guilty plea, (b) DOJ found
Boeing in breach of that DPA in May 2024 — the procedural shell failed
on its own terms — (c) Boeing then agreed in July 2024 to plead guilty
to the same fraud conspiracy charge, (d) Judge O'Connor rejected the
plea agreement in December 2024 over the plea agreement's monitor-
selection provisions, (e) after the January 2025 change of administration
the DOJ replaced the plea with a non-prosecution agreement (announced
May 23, 2025) under which Boeing paid more than $1.1 billion in
additional commitments under the NPA, including $487.2 million in total
criminal monetary penalty (of which $243.6 million had been paid in 2021
under the DPA), an additional $444.5 million for a fund for the heirs
and beneficiaries of crash victims, and approximately $445 million in
additional compliance, safety, and quality investments — and is *not* convicted of
the conspiracy. The system/object alibi diagnosis sits at the procedural
level: the certification process was named, the corporation was named,
the DPA-then-NPA *process* became the named bearer of responsibility,
but the named individuals at the engineering and executive level who
chose the single-sensor architecture, who removed the speed-only
authority limit in 2016, and who maintained the pilot-error framing for
five months between the crashes did not face individual federal
prosecution to verdict. (Forkner was the one named individual to face
trial; he was acquitted in March 2022.) Accountability climbed to the
corporation and then stopped at the procedural disposition; the
corporation is a legal-form alibi for the people inside it.
```
