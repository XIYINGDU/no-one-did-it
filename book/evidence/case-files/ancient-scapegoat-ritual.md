# Ancient Scapegoat Ritual — Thematic Context

This file is **not** a case-file-method case file. The ancient ritual is openly
substitutionary: the goat carries the iniquities of the community by explicit
ritual designation. There is no hidden chain to map, no laundering, no
counter-case to pair. What the chapter needs is anchored textual material —
verbatim primary text + scholarly synthesis — that Wayne can lift to install
the master metaphor: civilisation did not stop sacrificing substitutes; it
changed the altar.

Purpose: install the literal-to-figurative hinge. The English word
*scapegoat* begins as Tyndale's 1530 translation of a religious term; by the
19th century it names the modern social function. The chapter walks the
reader across that hinge.

Thematic load:
The ancient ritual is the literal pole of the chapter's central image. The

Section 1 — Leviticus 16 (Yom Kippur, Hebrew Bible)

Textual anchor: Leviticus 16:7–22.
Translations to use:
- Hebrew Bible primary text (Masoretic): consult Biblia Hebraica
  Stuttgartensia (BHS) for the textual base.
- JPS Tanakh (1985) — load-bearing modern English translation for the
  book; cite as "Jewish Publication Society, The Tanakh: The Holy
  Scriptures (Philadelphia: JPS, 1985)."
- NRSV — acceptable parallel; cite as "New Revised Standard Version,
  National Council of the Churches of Christ in the USA, 1989."

The ritual sequence (verses 7–22, paraphrase of the textual order — Wayne
will quote the verbatim passages he selects; this is the chronological
spine):

  1. Two goats are brought to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting (v. 7).
  2. Aaron casts lots upon the two goats — one lot "for the LORD" (la-YHWH)
     and one lot "for Azazel" (la-Azazel) (v. 8).
  3. The goat upon which the LORD's lot fell is offered as a sin offering
     (v. 9).
  4. The goat designated for Azazel is "set alive before the LORD, to make
     atonement over it, to send it away into the wilderness to Azazel"
     (v. 10, JPS 1985).
  5. Aaron lays both hands upon the head of the live goat, "confesses over
     it all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their
     transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the
     goat" (v. 21, JPS 1985).
  6. The goat is sent away "by the hand of a man that is in readiness" (JPS:
     "a man designated for the task"; NRSV: "someone designated for the
     task") into the wilderness (v. 21).
  7. "And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land
     not inhabited" (v. 22, JPS 1985).
  8. The ritual is annual — performed on the tenth day of the seventh
     month, Yom Kippur, "a statute forever" (v. 29, 34).

Key textual features Wayne can use verbatim:
- The two-goat structure: one killed, one sent away alive. The substitute
  that LIVES is the one that carries the sins out. The chapter's metaphor
  rides on this — the scapegoat is not the slaughtered victim but the
  expelled live bearer.
- Named-actor architecture: a designated priest (Aaron) performs the
  confession; a designated "ready man" leads the goat into the
  wilderness. The ritual has named officiants and witnesses; nothing is
  anonymous.
- Annual repetition: the ritual is recurrent — it does not solve the sin
  problem permanently, only purges the community for the current cycle.
  This is the structural feature that will rhyme with the chapter's
  argument that civilisation keeps performing substitution.
- Azazel: the destination/recipient of the live goat. Translation history
  is contested (a demon name, a place name, a compound noun meaning
  "rugged cliff"). For the chapter, the textual ambiguity is itself
  load-bearing — the ritual sends iniquity "away" to a destination the
  text does not fully specify. The metaphor reproduces this: laundered
  responsibility flows to a destination — committee, machine, market,
  proxy — that is rarely audited.

Etymology — the word "scapegoat":
- William Tyndale, in his 1530 English translation of the Pentateuch,
  coined "scapegoat" (originally "(e)scape goat") as a calque for the
  Hebrew. He read Azazel as a compound ('ez ozel) meaning "the goat
  that goes away" / "escape-goat", following a tradition traceable to
  early rabbinic and patristic commentary.
- The KJV (1611) inherited Tyndale's "scapegoat", entrenching it in
  English usage.
- By the early 19th century, "scapegoat" had migrated from a strict
  religious term to a general English noun for any person made to bear
  the blame of others. The OED records the figurative sense from the
  early 1800s (verify the specific dated quotation when sourcing the
  exact entry — see Sources needed).
- Modern scholarly view (e.g., Jacob Milgrom, Anchor Bible Leviticus
  17–22, 2000) reads Azazel as the name of a demonic being inhabiting
  the wilderness, not as "escape-goat" etymologically. Tyndale's
  reading is now considered linguistically dubious but historically
  load-bearing — the English word *is* the calque, even if the calque
  was wrong.

The literal/literary hinge — Wayne's load-bearing point:
The English word "scapegoat" carries its religious origin in its
morphology. Every modern political, legal, or algorithmic case that the
book examines uses a word that began as a translation of a Bronze Age
purgation ritual. The metaphor is not introduced by the author — it is
embedded in the language. Chapter 1 makes that embedding visible.

Section 2 — Athenian pharmakos

Textual anchors (chronological):

1. Hipponax of Ephesus, frr. 5–11 West (6th century BCE). The earliest
   surviving Greek references. Hipponax describes the pharmakos — a
   marginal, often disfigured or destitute person — being expelled from
   the city, beaten with branches of squill (skilla) and figs, in a
   ritual of communal purification.

2. Hellenistic and Roman-era descriptions:
   - Plutarch, *Quaestiones Convivales* / *Symposiacs* and
     *Quaestiones Romanae* — Plutarch describes Greek expulsion
     rituals in comparative contexts.
   - Tzetzes (12th c. Byzantine grammarian), *Chiliades* 5.726–761,
     preserves an account citing Hipponax: the pharmakos was fed at
     public expense, then led through the city, beaten on the
     genitals with squill branches, and driven out (or, in some
     accounts, killed). Tzetzes is late but is one of the most
     detailed surviving accounts; he is quoting earlier sources.
   - The Thargelia festival (6th day of Thargelion, May/June)
     featured the pharmakos rite at Athens, Colophon, and other
     Ionian cities.

3. Modern scholarly synthesis (load-bearing for the chapter):
   - Jan Bremmer, "Scapegoat Rituals in Ancient Greece," *Harvard
     Studies in Classical Philology* 87 (1983): 299–320. The
     standard modern synthesis; integrates Hipponax, Tzetzes, and
     the comparative ANE material.
   - Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion*, trans. John Raffan
     (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1985), pp. 82–84 and
     references; *Homo Necans*, trans. Peter Bing (Berkeley:
     University of California Press, 1983), pp. 64–71. Burkert
     reads scapegoat rituals through a comparative ritual-theory
     frame.
   - Dennis Hughes, *Human Sacrifice in Ancient Greece* (London:
     Routledge, 1991), chs. 5–6, specifically on whether pharmakos
     rites involved death or merely expulsion — a contested
     question Wayne should NOT resolve in chapter 1 (note the
     scholarly debate, do not flatten it).

Architecture of the pharmakos rite (per Bremmer 1983, synthesised):
- The pharmakos is typically a marginal person — destitute, criminal,
  disfigured — fed at public expense in the period before the rite.
- Selection: the city, through its officiants, chooses (or in some
  accounts the person presents themselves for a stipend).
- The rite: ritual procession through the city, beating with vegetal
  branches (squill, figs), then expulsion (some sources: killing —
  contested).
- Timing: annual at the Thargelia, or ad hoc in times of plague,
  famine, civil crisis.
- Function: the pharmakos absorbs the city's *miasma* (ritual
  pollution) and removes it by leaving.

Why the pharmakos matters for the chapter:
- Unlike Leviticus's goat, the pharmakos is HUMAN. The continuity
  between ancient substitution and modern political scapegoating
  runs directly through this human form.
- The pharmakos has named officiants, a documented selection
  procedure, a public ritual sequence. The modern social function —
  blaming a marginal actor for a systemic crisis — preserves the
  named-officiant + selection-procedure + ritual-sequence
  architecture, even when the modern actors are unaware of the
  ancient template.
- The ritual is consensual or coerced depending on the source — a
  feature Wayne can use without flattening.

Section 3 — Roman and Babylonian parallels (briefer)

Roman *Februa*:
- The Februa purifications gave their name to the month of February
  (Latin februare, "to purify"). Performed by the Luperci in the
  Lupercalia (Feb 15) and in surrounding lustration rites; involved
  expiatory whipping (Plutarch, *Romulus* 21; Ovid, *Fasti* 2.31ff.).
- Less directly substitutionary than the pharmakos, but part of the
  same purgation grammar — the community periodically expels
  pollution through ritual acts performed by named officiants.
- Use sparingly in the chapter — the Roman material is supporting,
  not load-bearing.

Babylonian *kuppuru*:
- Akkadian *kuppuru* / *kapāru* ("to wipe off, purify") is
  etymologically related to the Hebrew *kipper* ("to atone"). The
  root is shared across Northwest and East Semitic.
- The Babylonian New Year festival (Akitu) included substitution
  rituals: a temple was ritually cleansed by a *mašmaššu* priest
  using a goat or sheep whose head was symbolically substituted for
  the temple's pollution; the carcass was then disposed of in the
  Euphrates.
- Anchor: *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, ed. David Noel Freedman (New
  York: Doubleday, 1992), entries on "Atonement" (vol. 1) and "Day
  of Atonement" (vol. 2). The ABD entries trace the comparative
  Semitic background of Leviticus 16.
- Anchor: Walter Burkert, *Structure and History in Greek Mythology
  and Ritual* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1979),
  ch. 3, on Greek scapegoat rituals' Near Eastern background.

What the Babylonian parallel gives Wayne:
The ritual vocabulary of substitution and purgation is not
specifically Israelite; it is a regional grammar of the ancient Near
East. Leviticus 16 is one survival of a wider practice. The chapter
can use this depth to argue that substitution is not parochial — it
is a recurring civilisational technology for managing harm and
community guilt.

Section 4 — The literal/literary hinge

Load-bearing claim for chapter 1 (S0 → S0.5):
The ritual is openly substitutionary — the priest names the goat as
substitute, the community knows it is a substitute, the goat goes
into the wilderness with the sins ON IT BY DECLARATION. The modern
case is figuratively substitutionary — the substitute is named cause
without being named substitute. The chapter's recognition-reversal
hinges on this: what we have today is the ancient practice WITHOUT
THE NAMING. The altar moved. The substitution did not.

The word "scapegoat" is the literal-to-figurative hinge made
audible. Tyndale's 1530 calque carries the religious sense into
modern English; the 19th-century semantic shift extends it to
generic blame-bearing. The chapter walks the reader through:
(a) the ritual as it is described in Leviticus 16 (literal
    substitution, named substitute);
(b) the word "scapegoat" entering English as a translation of that
    ritual term;
(c) the word's modern usage to name what is no longer a literal
    substitution but functionally still one.
