# Sacco and Vanzetti — Case File

Case name: Commonwealth v. Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti
Domain: criminal justice / immigration politics / labor history
Dates and place: April 15, 1920 (South Braintree robbery-murder) → May 31–July 14, 1921 (Dedham trial) → August 23, 1927 (execution at Charlestown State Prison, Massachusetts). Governor Michael S. Dukakis proclamation, July 19, 1977 (Boston).
Case type: pure scapegoat

```text
Crisis:
On April 15, 1920, paymaster Frederick Parmenter and guard Alessandro
Berardelli were shot and killed during the robbery of the Slater & Morrill
shoe factory payroll in South Braintree, Massachusetts. The killings
landed in the middle of the First Red Scare: the Palmer Raids (Nov 1919
– Jan 1920), the deportation of Luigi Galleani and other Italian
anarchists (June 1919), and the bombing of Attorney General A. Mitchell
Palmer's home (June 2, 1919). Two Italian-immigrant anarchists — Nicola
Sacco, a shoemaker, and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, a fish-peddler — were
arrested on May 5, 1920 on a streetcar in Brockton. Both were Galleanists;
both were armed; both lied to police on the night of arrest about their
politics and associations.

Official story:
A jury convicted Sacco and Vanzetti of first-degree murder on July 14,
1921, after a six-week trial before Judge Webster Thayer. The verdict
rested on (a) eyewitness identifications, (b) ballistics testimony
linking a bullet recovered from Berardelli's body ("Bullet III") and a
spent shell to Sacco's .32 Colt automatic, and (c) the defendants'
"consciousness of guilt" — the lies told at arrest. Seven motions for a
new trial, all denied by Judge Thayer, ran from 1921 through 1924. After
condemned-killer Celestino Madeiros confessed in 1925 that the Morelli
gang of Providence — not Sacco and Vanzetti — had done the South
Braintree job, a final motion was denied by Thayer in October 1926. On
April 9, 1927 Thayer sentenced both men to death. Governor Alvan T.
Fuller appointed an advisory committee chaired by Harvard president
A. Lawrence Lowell; the Lowell Committee report (July 27, 1927) endorsed
the verdict. Both men were electrocuted shortly after midnight on August
23, 1927.

Blame container:
Two named immigrant anarchists, framed in court and in press as the
embodiment of the foreign-radical menace. The defendants' politics —
not their evidentiary guilt — carried the trial. Judge Thayer's
extrajudicial statements about "those anarchist bastards" (reported by
multiple witnesses, including Robert Benchley's affidavit) made the
container explicit. [Evidence grade: A]

Actual responsibility chain:

  Control:
  Massachusetts criminal-justice machinery — Norfolk County DA Frederick
  G. Katzmann (prosecution), Judge Webster Thayer (trial conduct + every
  post-trial motion), Governor Alvan T. Fuller (clemency authority), the
  Lowell Committee (advisory cover). Federal Bureau of Investigation
  participation in surveilling and discrediting Galleanists is documented
  in FBI files later released under FOIA (Avrich 1991, chs. 7–8).

  Benefit:
  Political class needing a conviction to close a high-profile case
  during the Red Scare; the Wilson and Harding Justice Departments
  prosecuting Galleanists; Massachusetts Republican establishment
  protecting its judiciary against the procedural critique that the
  Frankfurter essay made explicit. The actual robbers (the Morelli gang,
  per Madeiros's 1925 confession and Avrich's chain of corroborating
  evidence) benefited by escaping prosecution.

  Knowledge:
  The state had access by 1925 to Madeiros's detailed confession, to the
  Morelli gang's prior record of payroll robberies on the same line, and
  to ballistics doubts about whether "Bullet III" had been substituted
  during trial (Young & Kaiser 1985). Judge Thayer reviewed and rejected
  all of this. The Lowell Committee saw the Madeiros confession and
  dismissed it.

  Preventability:
  Every named control-holder above could have stopped the execution. A
  new trial after the Madeiros confession, a commutation by Governor
  Fuller, or removal of Judge Thayer for documented bias would each have
  prevented the August 1927 outcome. None acted.

  Record controller:
  Norfolk County clerk's office (trial transcript); Massachusetts
  Governor's office (Fuller decision file, Lowell Committee report);
  Boston Public Library Sacco-Vanzetti Collection; Harvard Law School
  Library (Felix Frankfurter Papers); Boston University Howard Gotlieb
  Archival Research Center (defense counsel papers). The state controlled
  the trial record; the defense controlled the appeals record; the press
  (Boston Herald in particular, after the Frankfurter essay) controlled
  the procedural-critique record.

  Cost bearer:
  Nicola Sacco (executed Aug 23, 1927). Bartolomeo Vanzetti (executed
  Aug 23, 1927). Their families. Italian-American immigrant communities
  in Massachusetts subjected to surveillance and reputational stigma.
  Celestino Madeiros (executed the same night for an unrelated murder,
  his confession unrelied-upon).

How the alibi hardened:
1. Atmospheric pressure of the Red Scare: jury, judge, and Lowell
   Committee operated inside an environment in which "Italian anarchist"
   functioned as evidentiary shorthand for "guilty".
2. Procedural insulation: Massachusetts at the time vested all post-trial
   motions in the original trial judge. Thayer reviewed his own bias for
   seven years and found none.
3. The Lowell Committee's institutional weight: a Harvard president, a
   former probate judge (Robert Grant), and the president of MIT
   (Samuel W. Stratton) provided establishment cover. Their report
   asserted the trial was fair "on the whole".
4. Suppression of the Madeiros lead: state never investigated the Morelli
   gang as suggested by the 1925 confession.

How the alibi weakened:
1. Frankfurter, Felix. "The Case of Sacco and Vanzetti." *Atlantic
   Monthly*, March 1927, pp. 409–432. Procedural critique by a sitting
   Harvard Law professor, immediately published as a book.
2. Sustained press attention: *Boston Herald* editorial "We Submit"
   (Oct 26, 1926, by F. Lauriston Bullard) — Pulitzer-winning reversal
   from a previously pro-conviction paper.
3. Avrich, Paul. *Sacco and Vanzetti: The Anarchist Background*.
   Princeton UP, 1991. Established (i) that Sacco and Vanzetti were
   committed Galleanist anarchists and probable bomb-conspiracy
   participants in adjacent acts, but (ii) that the specific South
   Braintree robbery-murder was, by the chain of evidence, the work of
   the Morelli gang.
4. Young, William, and David E. Kaiser. *Postmortem: New Evidence in the
   Case of Sacco and Vanzetti*. University of Massachusetts Press, 1985.
   Documents the ballistics substitution and prosecutorial-misconduct
   evidence.
5. Dukakis, Michael S. Proclamation of August 23, 1977 (Massachusetts
   Executive Department), declaring that "any disgrace should be forever
   removed from their names" and that the trial was "permeated by
   prejudice against foreigners and hostility toward unorthodox political
   views." The proclamation is a record-level recognition by the
   sovereign of Massachusetts that the trial was prejudicial; it stops
   short of vacating the conviction (which would require judicial
   action), but it is the state's own admission.

Best counterargument:
"This is at most partial scapegoat, not pure scapegoat. Sacco's pistol
was a .32 Colt automatic matching the South Braintree caliber. Modern
re-tests of Bullet III (Boston Globe / Selectron consortium, 1961; and
follow-up examinations) have not cleared Sacco of the specific shooting.
Avrich himself argues Sacco and Vanzetti were Galleanist anarchists
involved in adjacent bombings. If Sacco fired the fatal bullet, the
trial's outcome was correct even if its conduct was prejudicial." This
is the strongest reading and Bonnie should weigh it before chapter
placement.

Shirley's response: the case-type question is not "did Sacco shoot
someone, in some incident, ever?" but "was the South Braintree
conviction supported by evidence that would survive a non-prejudicial
trial?" Avrich, Young & Kaiser, the Frankfurter essay, the Lowell
Committee record, the Bullard editorial, and the Dukakis proclamation
together establish that on the specific charge for which they were
executed, the evidence was insufficient and the process was prejudicial.
That is the pure-scapegoat condition under the book's taxonomy. But the
"partial" reading is preserved in open questions below for Bonnie's
final call.
```

## Assumptions

- Treating the Avrich (1991) chain — Sacco and Vanzetti as Galleanists
  involved in adjacent radical activity but not the South Braintree
  shooting — as the working scholarly consensus. The Russell revision
  is preserved as a counter-reading, not adopted.
- Treating the Dukakis 1977 proclamation as the state-level "alibi
  weakening" record, with the explicit caveat that it does not vacate
  the conviction.
- Treating "pure scapegoat" as the correct category given the chapter's
  taxonomy, while flagging the partial-scapegoat reading for Bonnie.
