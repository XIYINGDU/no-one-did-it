# UK Post Office Horizon — Counter-Case to Therac-25

Counter-case name: *Bates and Others v Post Office Ltd*, and the Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry
Paired scapegoat case: therac-25
Domain: public administration / commercial software / English civil and criminal procedure / statutory inquiry
Dates and place: 1999 (Horizon system rolled out by ICL/Fujitsu under the Pathway contract) → 1999–2015 (period of disputed prosecutions of sub-postmasters by the Post Office, acting as private prosecutor) → 2017 (Group Litigation Order in *Bates and Others v Post Office Ltd*, High Court of England and Wales) → March 15, 2019 (Fraser J, *Common Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 606 (QB)) → December 16, 2019 (Fraser J, *Horizon Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB)) → April 23, 2021 (Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), *Hamilton & Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 577, quashing 39 convictions on the principal Horizon ground) → September 22, 2020 onward (Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry under Sir Wyn Williams; converted to statutory inquiry June 1, 2021 under the Inquiries Act 2005; oral hearings continuing through 2024) → May 24, 2024 (Royal Assent of the Post Office (Horizon System) Offences Act 2024, the statutory exoneration). Setting: England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland; sub-postmasters as ostensibly self-employed agents of the Post Office.

```text
Laundering attempt:
  Pattern (from Cross_Pattern_Matrix.md): System/object alibi by
  diagnostic-tool capture — the institution (Post Office Ltd) is the
  same body that (i) deploys the diagnostic tool (Horizon accounting
  software supplied by Fujitsu under the ICL/Fujitsu contract),
  (ii) owns the prosecutorial function (the Post Office's private
  prosecution authority under English law), and (iii) controls the
  technical record of the diagnostic tool's behavior (Horizon error
  logs, remote-access records, KEL — Known Error Log). The
  laundering rested on the institutional reification of the
  computer's output as evidence: "the computer says you stole"
  treated as a load-bearing evidentiary claim, supported by the
  common-law presumption of computer reliability (the so-called
  "presumption" derived from *Castle v Cross* [1984] 1 WLR 1372 and
  the post-1999 statutory regime).

  Goat-candidate: Approximately 700 sub-postmasters and sub-postmistresses
  prosecuted by the Post Office between 1999 and 2015 on charges of
  theft, false accounting, and fraud, on the basis of accounting
  shortfalls displayed by the Horizon system at their branches. The
  total convicted figure has been cited in the inquiry record at
  "more than 700" and in some sources at over 900; the precise
  count is part of what the statutory inquiry is reconstructing.
  The 39 named appellants in *Hamilton & Others* (April 2021) are
  the documentary anchor for the load-bearing figure.

  Alibi being constructed: A system/object alibi in which the
  Horizon software's accounting output was treated as evidence of
  theft, while the human chain — Fujitsu engineers who knew of
  software bugs, Post Office Ltd executives who knew or should have
  known of the bugs, and prosecuting solicitors who failed to
  disclose Fujitsu's knowledge to the defense as required by the
  *Criminal Procedure and Investigations Act 1996* — was shielded.
  The alibi was hardened by (a) the common-law presumption of
  computer reliability, (b) the Post Office's institutional
  position as both prosecutor and complainant, (c) the practical
  difficulty for individual sub-postmasters of obtaining
  independent technical examination of a proprietary system to
  which only Fujitsu had access, (d) the contract terms requiring
  sub-postmasters to be personally liable for shortfalls regardless
  of cause, and (e) the dispersion of victims (one per branch,
  scattered across the UK) preventing collective resistance.

Interception mechanism:
  Type: court (High Court managed litigation with subpoena power
  for disclosure) + independent technical examiner with reverse-
  engineering capability — load-bearing institutional pair. Statutory
  inquiry as second-stage continuation. The class action is the
  load-bearing legal instrument; the independent expert is the
  load-bearing technical instrument; absent either, the
  interception fails.

  Named institutional actor: Four load-bearing actors, sequenced:

  (1) Alan Bates (former sub-postmaster, Craig-y-Don, Llandudno,
      Wales; suspended by Post Office in 2003 after disputing
      Horizon shortfalls). Bates founded and led the Justice for
      Subpostmasters Alliance (JFSA), beginning in 2009, which
      coordinated the dispersed sub-postmaster community,
      assembled the documentary case across years, secured
      litigation funding (initially via Therium), and survived
      attempts at settlement that would have closed the case
      without the disclosure phase. JFSA's coordination is the
      load-bearing civil-society precondition for the class
      action.

  (2) The High Court of England and Wales (Queen's Bench Division),
      Mr Justice Peter Fraser presiding over the *Bates and Others
      v Post Office Ltd* Group Litigation, 2017–2019. The Group
      Litigation Order (March 2017) consolidated the dispersed
      sub-postmaster claims into one managed action. Fraser J's
      sequence of judgments produced the documentary disclosure
      that the prior prosecutorial process had prevented:
      - *Common Issues* judgment, March 15, 2019, [2019] EWHC 606
        (QB) — found that the sub-postmaster contracts could not
        be construed as making sub-postmasters strictly liable
        for shortfalls of unknown cause.
      - *Horizon Issues* judgment, December 16, 2019, [2019] EWHC
        3408 (QB) — found, in operative language, that the Horizon
        IT system was not "remotely robust" in the relevant
        respects, that bugs, errors, and defects "did have the
        potential to cause apparent or alleged shortfalls in
        branch accounts," and that Fujitsu and the Post Office had
        known of relevant bugs. The judgment ran to 313 pages and
        rested on detailed examination of the KEL (Known Error
        Log) and Fujitsu-disclosed engineering records.

  (3) Jason Coyne (and the wider Second Sight investigation that
      preceded the *Bates* litigation; Coyne acted as the
      claimants' technical expert during the Horizon Issues
      trial). Coyne's reverse-engineering analysis of Horizon
      error logs and his cross-examination of Fujitsu engineers
      (Gareth Jenkins notably) produced the technical foundation
      for Fraser J's findings. Second Sight Support Services had
      previously conducted an independent investigation
      commissioned by the Post Office (2012–15), which the Post
      Office terminated in March 2015 when Second Sight's findings
      began to indict the Post Office; the early Second Sight work
      pre-stages Coyne's later litigation role.

  (4) The Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), in *Hamilton &
      Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 577, April 23,
      2021. The judgment quashed 39 convictions on the principal
      ground that prosecutions based on Horizon evidence had been
      conducted as "an affront to the conscience of the Court"
      where the Post Office had failed to disclose what it knew
      about Horizon's unreliability. Subsequent appellate
      judgments through 2023 quashed further convictions; the
      total of judicially-overturned convictions by the date of
      the 2024 statutory exoneration was approximately 100, with
      hundreds more covered by the statutory mechanism rather
      than appeal.

  Key act of interception (what specifically happened): Fraser J's
  *Horizon Issues* judgment of December 16, 2019, was the
  load-bearing institutional act. The judgment was made possible
  by the *Bates* Group Litigation's disclosure phase, which
  compelled the Post Office and Fujitsu to produce the engineering
  records the prior criminal trials had not seen. Coyne's
  reverse-engineering of Horizon error logs and the
  cross-examination of Fujitsu engineers established the technical
  basis. The *Hamilton & Others* Court of Appeal judgment of April
  23, 2021, converted the civil findings into criminal-conviction
  reversals. The Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry (statutory from
  June 2021) extended the institutional examination beyond the
  litigation-bounded record. The 2024 statutory exoneration (Post
  Office (Horizon System) Offences Act 2024) addressed the
  case-by-case appeal bottleneck.

  Date of interception: December 16, 2019 (Fraser J *Horizon
  Issues* judgment, the load-bearing legal finding). Continuing
  through April 23, 2021 (*Hamilton & Others* quashing) and May
  24, 2024 (statutory exoneration). The interception was begun on
  March 2017 (Group Litigation Order) and on May 21, 2009 (JFSA
  founding).

Amplifiers (mechanisms that supported the interception but were
not load-bearing):
- *Computer Weekly* investigation, beginning May 11, 2009 with
  Rebecca Thomson's "Bankruptcy, prosecution and disrupted lives —
  postmasters tell their story." *Computer Weekly* was the first
  outlet to assemble the dispersed sub-postmaster accounts into
  a recognisable pattern; the publication date coincides with
  JFSA's founding period.
- *Private Eye* sustained coverage, particularly by Richard
  Brooks, from approximately 2011 onward.
- Parliamentary attention: Lord James Arbuthnot of Edrom (Sir
  James Arbuthnot as he then was), MP for North-East Hampshire,
  raised the issue in Commons from 2010 and continued in the
  Lords; APPG on Sub-Postmasters and Horizon led from 2015 by
  Kevan Jones MP.
- Nick Wallis's *The Great Post Office Scandal* (2021, updated
  editions), which documented the case for general audience.
- *Mr Bates vs The Post Office* (ITV, four-part drama, broadcast
  January 1–4, 2024, written by Gwyneth Hughes). The drama is the
  political amplifier that forced the 2024 statutory exoneration
  and the rapid political response; it is NOT load-bearing for
  the institutional interception, which had already been
  accomplished by Fraser J's 2019 judgment and the 2021 *Hamilton*
  appeal. The chapter must say so explicitly — the load-bearing
  interception is judicial; the drama was the amplifier that
  accelerated the political-redress phase.

Cost of escape:
  Time from laundering to interception:
  Twenty years from initial Horizon deployment (1999) to Fraser J's
  *Horizon Issues* judgment (December 16, 2019). Twenty-five years
  to the statutory exoneration (May 24, 2024). The first
  documented sub-postmaster prosecutions began in 1999–2000; the
  pattern was first publicly aggregated by *Computer Weekly* in
  May 2009; the JFSA was founded the same month.

  Reputational, financial, or career cost to the goat-candidates
  during the laundering:
  - At least 700 sub-postmasters prosecuted; approximately 100
    convictions quashed by appellate judgment by 2024, with
    hundreds more covered by statutory exoneration.
  - Prison sentences served by some sub-postmasters; Seema Misra
    was sentenced to 15 months in 2010 while pregnant, a case
    that became central to public attention.
  - Bankruptcies, lost homes, lost livelihoods, family ruptures
    documented across hundreds of accounts compiled in the
    inquiry record.
  - At least four sub-postmaster suicides have been reported as
    connected to Horizon prosecutions; the inquiry has heard
    evidence regarding additional deaths attributed by relatives
    to the prosecutions' effects. The precise count remains part
    of the inquiry's reconstruction.
  - Hundreds of sub-postmasters died before exoneration or
    compensation was completed.

  Cost absorbed by cost-bearers who could not wait:
  - The sub-postmasters who died during the twenty-five-year
    laundering period are the irreducible cost. The Bringing-
    them-Home analogue does not apply here — the redress can
    extend to relatives via the redress schemes, but the
    individual recognition does not.
  - Communities deprived of post office services where branches
    closed after sub-postmaster removal.

  Cost to the intercepting actor:
  - Alan Bates: years of unpaid coordination work; financial cost
    during the JFSA's early period; sustained personal stress
    documented in his testimony to the Inquiry.
  - The *Bates* claimants' litigation funder (Therium) absorbed
    a substantial share of the GBP 57.75 million settlement
    figure (December 2019), reducing the per-claimant
    distribution; this is a recurring limitation of class-action
    interception in the English costs regime.
  - Jason Coyne and the Second Sight team faced sustained
    institutional opposition from the Post Office during the
    2012–15 investigation period; Second Sight's contract was
    terminated in March 2015.
  - Court time, inquiry resources, and the legal-aid budget for
    appellate work were substantial.

Outcome:
  Partial reversal with continuing remedy. (i) Approximately 100
  convictions quashed by appellate judgment 2021–2023; (ii) the
  Post Office (Horizon System) Offences Act 2024 provides
  statutory exoneration without case-by-case appeal for
  prosecutions falling within defined criteria; (iii) the Horizon
  Compensation Schemes (HSS — Horizon Shortfall Scheme; GLO —
  Group Litigation Order scheme; HCRS — Horizon Convictions
  Redress Scheme) are operational, with completion ongoing as of
  May 2026. The reversal is incomplete in documented respects:
  many sub-postmasters did not live to see exoneration;
  compensation distribution has been slow and contested; no
  Post Office or Fujitsu executive has been criminally
  prosecuted as of May 2026, though the Inquiry's final report
  (not yet published as of this case file date) may reshape
  the prosecutorial calculus; and the precise count of affected
  sub-postmasters and deaths is still being reconstructed.

Transferable lesson (one sentence, diagnostic not motivational):
When an institution controls both the diagnostic artifact (the
software, the source code, the error logs) and the record of its
behavior, only a fact-finding venue with subpoena power and an
independent technical examiner who can reverse-engineer the
artifact can pierce the system/object alibi — and the engineering
forensics (Coyne's reverse-engineering of Horizon error logs;
Leveson's reconstruction of the Therac-25 race condition) are the
artifact-level part; the court or inquiry is the institutional
part.

Limits of the lesson (where this counter-case does NOT generalise):
- The lesson generalises to system/object alibi patterns where (a)
  the alibi rests on a software or technical artifact whose
  internal behavior is reverse-engineerable given disclosure,
  (b) a fact-finding venue with subpoena power exists and is
  politically willing to compel disclosure, (c) an independent
  technical examiner of acknowledged competence is available
  and not captured by the deploying institution, (d) a class of
  affected parties can be assembled into a single legal action
  large enough to absorb the cost of disclosure litigation. The
  Horizon case had all four conditions in unusual measure: the
  software was reverse-engineerable from error logs and KEL
  records, the High Court was willing to manage a Group
  Litigation, Jason Coyne / Second Sight had relevant
  expertise, and the JFSA assembled the class. Many system/
  object alibi patterns lack one or more of these conditions.
- The Horizon case is a partial counter-case for Therac-25 in
  the narrow sense the project memo identifies: both involve
  software-driven systems whose alibis ("the machine cannot
  produce that dose"; "the computer says you stole") were
  pierced by independent engineering forensics paired with an
  institutional fact-finder (FDA Class III recall + Leveson's
  IEEE reconstruction for Therac-25; High Court Group Litigation
  + Coyne's reverse-engineering for Horizon). The cases differ
  on key dimensions: Therac-25 had a manufacturer separate from
  the regulator (AECL distinct from FDA), enabling the
  regulator's structural distance; Horizon had Post Office Ltd
  as both deployer and prosecutor, removing structural distance
  and requiring the court to substitute for it. The Therac-25
  cost-bearers were a small number of identifiable patients
  treated within an eighteen-month window; the Horizon
  cost-bearers were hundreds of dispersed sub-postmasters over
  twenty-five years.
- The lesson is narrower than the original Bhopal-pairing
  framing. With Bhopal moved to ch-5, the Horizon counter-case
  no longer carries the "corporate-architectural system alibi
  pierced by sovereign-court reversal" frame. The Therac-25
  pairing narrows the lesson to: software / machine alibi
  pierced by engineering forensics plus institutional
  fact-finding. This narrower lesson is what the chapter installs.
- The drama (*Mr Bates vs The Post Office*) is the most-cited
  amplifier in popular memory but is not the load-bearing
  interception. The chapter must distinguish amplifier from
  load-bearing actor; conflating the two produces a misleading
  lesson ("dramatic storytelling pierces system/object alibi")
  that does not transfer.

Best counterargument:
"Horizon is too disanalogous from Therac-25 to function as its
counter-case. Therac-25 involved a discrete number of patients
exposed to a software fault in a regulated medical-device
environment; Horizon involved hundreds of small-business operators
prosecuted by their own commercial principal in a non-regulated
commercial-IT environment. The interception mechanism in Therac-25
(FDA recall + academic reconstruction) is structurally different
from the Horizon mechanism (Group Litigation + statutory inquiry);
the transferable lesson at the level of "engineering forensics
plus institutional fact-finder pierces system/object alibi" is so
abstract that it loses diagnostic force. A tighter counter-case
for Therac-25 would be the VW Dieselgate ICCT emissions tests
(2014–15), where ICCT's independent testing detected the defeat
device against a manufacturer's alibi about emissions performance —
shared regulatory-environment, shared engineering-fraud structure."
```
