# Nisour Square — The Private Proxy, the Convictions, and the Pardons

Case: Shooting of 17 Iraqi civilians by Blackwater USA contractors at Nisour Square, Baghdad; subsequent US federal prosecution, convictions, partial appellate reversal and retrial, and presidential pardons.
Domain: war / statecraft / private military contracting / occupation
Dates/place: 16 September 2007, Nisour Square traffic circle, Baghdad, Iraq; investigations and prosecutions 2007–2020; pardons 22 December 2020 (Washington, DC).
Case type: partial scapegoat

Classification note: Hybrid per `rules/01-case-taxonomy.md` and
`.claude/skills/taxonomy-classification/SKILL.md`.
  - **Partial scapegoat (primary):** the visible blame container was
    the four named convoy guards on the ground. They were genuinely
    in the chain — they fired the weapons — and four were ultimately
    convicted in US federal court of homicide and weapons charges
    (2014). The laundering operated above them: the US State
    Department's contractual relationship with Blackwater Worldwide
    (then Blackwater USA, later renamed Xe Services and then Academi)
    insulated the corporate principal from criminal liability for
    individual conduct; the contractors' status under Coalition
    Provisional Authority (CPA) Order Number 17 (revised, 27 June
    2004) shielded them from Iraqi criminal jurisdiction. The
    pardons of 22 December 2020 then reversed even the individual-
    level accountability.
  - **System/object alibi (secondary):** the *legal-status shell* —
    "contractor," "diplomatic-protective detail," "CPA Order 17
    immunity" — functioned as a procedural container into which
    responsibility was poured. The contractor category is not a
    criminal-immunity category in international law, but it
    operated as one in the operational moment.
The chapter must address both layers.

```text
Crisis:
  On 16 September 2007, a four-vehicle Blackwater convoy
  designated "Raven 23" entered Nisour Square in central
  Baghdad. The convoy was a State Department protective
  detail for diplomatic personnel. The shooting began at
  approximately 12:08 local time. Within minutes, 17 Iraqi
  civilians were killed and at least 20 more were injured.
  Among the dead were a nine-year-old boy, Ali Kinani, and
  his mother. No US personnel were killed; the convoy
  reported being under attack and called in to State
  Department command. The Iraqi government immediately
  protested; the US State Department initially defended the
  convoy. Within days, FBI investigators were dispatched
  to Baghdad.

Official story (during the operational window):
  Blackwater's contemporaneous position, communicated via
  founder Erik Prince and corporate communications, was
  that the convoy had come under attack from insurgents
  and had returned fire in self-defense. The US State
  Department, which had contracted Blackwater for
  protective-services work under Worldwide Personal
  Protective Services (WPPS), initially supported this
  account. Iraqi government investigations reached a
  contrary conclusion within days; the Iraqi Ministry of
  Interior and the Iraqi Ministry of Defence findings
  characterized the shooting as unprovoked. US
  investigations followed: an FBI team, a State Department
  Diplomatic Security review, the US Army's 21 September
  2007 initial assessment, and the Joint Incident Review
  Board chaired by State Department investigators.

Blame container:
  At the level the case ultimately reached: the four named
  Blackwater contractors in convoy Raven 23 charged in
  federal court — Nicholas Slatten, Paul Slough, Evan
  Liberty, and Dustin Heard. A fifth contractor, Jeremy
  Ridgeway, pleaded guilty to voluntary manslaughter in
  December 2008 and cooperated with prosecutors. Blackwater
  the corporation was not criminally indicted. The State
  Department's contract administration was not criminally
  investigated. Erik Prince, then-CEO and majority owner of
  Blackwater, was not personally indicted. The contractor
  status under CPA Order 17 was the shell that initially
  prevented Iraqi jurisdiction.

Actual responsibility chain (as the record stands):
  1. The convoy members themselves on 16 September 2007:
     four contractors convicted at federal trial in 2014:
     - Nicholas A. Slatten: convicted of first-degree
       murder, US District Court for the District of
       Columbia, 22 October 2014 (initial verdict on the
       lesser charge); subsequent retrial on first-degree
       murder following severance, conviction 19 December
       2018 (after a 2014 mistrial on that count and a
       2017 D.C. Circuit reversal as to him). Sentenced to
       life imprisonment.
     - Paul A. Slough: convicted of voluntary
       manslaughter (14 counts), attempted manslaughter,
       and a firearms charge; 22 October 2014.
     - Evan S. Liberty: convicted of voluntary
       manslaughter (8 counts), attempted manslaughter,
       and a firearms charge; 22 October 2014.
     - Dustin L. Heard: convicted of voluntary
       manslaughter (6 counts), attempted manslaughter,
       and a firearms charge; 22 October 2014.
     - Jeremy P. Ridgeway: pleaded guilty to voluntary
       manslaughter and attempted manslaughter, December
       2008.
     On 4 August 2017, the US Court of Appeals for the
     D.C. Circuit (United States v. Slatten et al., 865
     F.3d 767) affirmed in part, vacated in part, and
     remanded for resentencing the convictions of Slough,
     Liberty, and Heard (on Eighth Amendment "cruel and
     unusual punishment" grounds as to the mandatory
     30-year firearms enhancement sentence for "machinegun"
     use during a crime of violence) and vacated Slatten's
     murder conviction with remand for a new trial (on
     severance grounds). Resentencing of Slough, Liberty,
     and Heard followed; Slatten was retried and convicted
     of first-degree murder on 19 December 2018 and
     sentenced to life.
  2. Blackwater USA / Blackwater Worldwide (later Xe
     Services, then Academi): operational chain — convoy
     dispatch decisions, vehicle configuration, weapons
     load-out, rules-of-engagement training, after-action
     reporting. The corporation paid civil settlements to
     Iraqi families through subsequent litigation. The
     corporation itself was not criminally indicted for
     Nisour Square; its successor Academi reached a
     $42 million settlement with the US State Department
     in 2012 for unrelated export-control and weapons-
     trafficking violations.
  3. US State Department, Bureau of Diplomatic Security:
     contracted Blackwater under WPPS II; supervised the
     protective-detail mission; the Joint Incident Review
     Board reviewed the shooting. Senate Armed Services
     Committee, "Inquiry into the Treatment of Detainees
     in U.S. Custody" (December 2008, the SASC's central
     contractor-related report covers Abu Ghraib;
     contractor-accountability hearings on Nisour Square
     were held by House Oversight, 2 October 2007).
  4. US Department of Defense / Multinational Force-Iraq:
     command relationship to State Department contractors
     in theater was ambiguous and was the subject of
     Congressional inquiry.
  5. Coalition Provisional Authority / US executive: CPA
     Order Number 17 (revised, 27 June 2004), issued by
     the CPA Administrator under US-led occupation
     authority, granted contractors immunity from Iraqi
     legal process for acts performed pursuant to the
     terms of their contracts. Iraqi sovereignty after
     2004 inherited Order 17. Iraq formally repealed CPA
     Order 17's protections for security contractors in
     2008 via the Iraqi cabinet decision following Nisour
     Square, taking effect with the 2008/2009 US-Iraq
     Status of Forces Agreement.
  6. Erik Dean Prince (founder and former CEO of
     Blackwater): named living individual; no personal
     criminal conviction in connection with Nisour
     Square. Testified before the House Committee on
     Oversight and Government Reform on 2 October 2007.

Control:
  Operational control on the ground: the convoy team
  itself; the convoy team leader. Tactical command:
  Blackwater operations supervisor on duty. Mission
  authority: US State Department, Bureau of Diplomatic
  Security. Legal-status authority: CPA Order 17 as
  inherited by post-CPA Iraq pending repeal. Contract
  authority: US State Department contracting officers.

Benefit:
  Blackwater Worldwide benefited from the WPPS contract
  through 2009, when the State Department declined to
  renew it. Erik Prince retained ownership of the
  corporation through and beyond the immediate
  aftermath; the corporation was sold and renamed (Xe
  Services 2009, Academi 2011, merged into Constellis
  2014). The State Department benefited from protective
  services that DSS could not staff in-house.

Knowledge:
  US executive-branch knowledge of contractor-
  accountability gaps was documented by the time of
  Nisour Square: the Government Accountability Office
  (GAO-08-966, July 2008; GAO-09-19, October 2008; and
  earlier reports including GAO-06-865R of June 2006)
  had reported on contractor-oversight deficiencies in
  Iraq. The Department of the Army inspector general's
  prior reports addressed contractor conduct. The CPA
  Order 17 immunity structure was a documented and
  publicly available legal instrument.

Preventability:
  Each step was preventable: contract structures that
  closed the criminal-jurisdiction gap (the 2000 Military
  Extraterritorial Jurisdiction Act, MEJA, had been
  amended in 2004 to cover Department of Defense
  contractors but not unambiguously to cover State
  Department contractors — a gap closed in significant
  part by subsequent legislation); rules-of-engagement
  training audited by the contracting agency; convoy
  composition and behavior reviewed routinely.

Record controller:
  Record is multi-source. US District Court for the
  District of Columbia: trial record (2014, 2018), public.
  D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals: 2017 decision and
  subsequent, public. FBI investigative records:
  partially public. State Department Diplomatic Security
  internal review: partially public. Blackwater corporate
  records: privately held with subpoenaed portions in the
  court record. Iraqi Ministry of Interior investigative
  records: substantially public via Iraqi government
  releases and US trial submissions. Senate Armed
  Services Committee 2008 report and House Oversight
  Committee hearing record, 2 October 2007.

Cost bearer:
  The 17 Iraqi civilians killed and at least 20 wounded
  at Nisour Square, and their families. Named victims
  included Ahmed Al-Rubaie, Mahasin Mohssen Khadum Al-
  Khazali, the nine-year-old Ali Mohammed Hafidh Abdul
  Razzaq Kinani and his family. Civil settlements paid
  by Blackwater/Xe/Academi to Iraqi families through
  US-court-supervised mediation were reported in 2010
  and following years; total figures vary in public
  reporting and are not all matters of court record.

How the alibi hardened:
  - CPA Order 17 immunity from Iraqi jurisdiction —
    structural legal-status shell.
  - Contractor classification — a procedural category
    that placed personnel outside the Uniform Code of
    Military Justice (UCMJ) and ambiguously inside or
    outside MEJA, depending on the contract issuer.
  - Blackwater's contemporaneous "self-defense"
    framing, supported initially by the State
    Department.
  - Brand renaming sequence — Blackwater → Xe → Academi
    → Constellis — distancing the corporate-name
    public memory from the operational chain.
  - The State Department's WPPS contract structure,
    which structured oversight at a level removed from
    operational rules of engagement.

How the alibi weakened:
  - FBI investigative findings (2007–2008) concluded
    that the shooting was unjustified for at least 14 of
    the 17 deaths.
  - Iraqi cabinet repeal of CPA Order 17 protections
    for security contractors (Oct 2007 cabinet decision;
    formal effect with the 2008/2009 US-Iraq Status of
    Forces Agreement entering into force 1 January
    2009).
  - Federal indictment of the five contractors on
    8 December 2008 under MEJA.
  - District Court for the District of Columbia
    initial dismissal on Fifth Amendment due-process
    grounds (31 December 2009) regarding compelled-
    statement use — followed by D.C. Circuit reversal,
    *United States v. Slough*, 641 F.3d 544 (D.C. Cir.
    2011), reinstating the prosecution.
  - Convictions, 22 October 2014, of Slough, Liberty,
    Heard (voluntary manslaughter and related); and
    Slatten (initially on a lesser count, then on
    first-degree murder after the 2017 D.C. Circuit
    severance ruling and the December 2018 retrial).
  - Sentences imposed and (for Slough, Liberty, Heard)
    revised on remand after the August 2017 D.C.
    Circuit decision.
  - Senate Armed Services Committee and House Oversight
    Committee hearings, 2007–2008.
  - GAO reports on contractor-accountability gaps,
    2006–2008.

Best counterargument:
  Strongest defense of the convicted contractors'
  position: the convoy team operated in a high-threat
  environment under unclear rules of engagement; one
  convoy member was killed days earlier in a separate
  incident, raising the convoy's threat perception; the
  D.C. Circuit's 2017 decision found constitutional
  problems with the firearms-enhancement sentence and
  with Slatten's joint trial. The convictions stand
  after retrial and resentencing on remand. Reply: the
  factual finding — that the shooting was unjustified
  for at least 14 of the 17 deaths — survived
  appellate review; the constitutional issues were
  procedural and were resolved on remand by
  resentencing and retrial. The convictions are
  current as of the pardon date and remain on the
  record despite the pardons (a pardon does not vacate
  a conviction; it removes or commutes the sentence
  and may restore civil rights).

  Second counterargument the chapter must address:
  the pardons of 22 December 2020 are a lawful
  executive act under Article II, Section 2, Clause 1
  of the US Constitution. Reply: the chapter does not
  contest the constitutional validity of the pardons.
  The chapter notes that the pardons were issued, that
  the convictions remain on the record, that no Iraqi
  victim received judicial accountability for the
  deaths beyond the convictions that the pardons
  released the defendants from serving, and that the
  pardon is itself a documentable step in the
  responsibility chain — not narrated as "they got
  away with it" but as a procedural-stage event in a
  longer sequence (CPA Order 17 immunity → FBI
  investigation → MEJA indictment → conviction →
  appellate reversal and remand → retrial conviction
  → pardon). Per the chapter brief: narrate the
  chain, not the verdict on the chain.

Book function:
  Canonical case for the **private-proxy** mode of the
  proxy-deniability mechanism in Chapter 4 ("The Proxy
  and the Sponsor"). Demonstrates: (a) the visible
  blame container (the convoy) is real, but the

  (b) US criminal jurisdiction was capable of reaching

  show that the proxy-deniability pattern crosses the
  state/private line.

Intercepting mechanism (counter-case material, beat 9):
  Mixed. The US federal prosecution and convictions
  are an interception — the most institutionally
  substantial in this chapter's three cases. The
  pardons are a partial re-laundering of that
  interception (the conviction remains on the record;
  the sentence does not). The book's chapter must
  represent the interception and the re-laundering as
  two stages in one procedural sequence.
  Interception type: court (US federal court with
  MEJA jurisdiction), amplified by record durability
  (FBI investigative material, Iraqi government
  documentation) and by jurisdictional persistence
  (the 2011 reinstatement after the 2009 dismissal;
  the 2018 retrial after the 2017 reversal).
  Named institutional actor: US District Court for
  the District of Columbia; US Court of Appeals for
  the D.C. Circuit; US Department of Justice public
  integrity / national-security prosecutors.
  Key act of interception: the 22 October 2014
  convictions; the 19 December 2018 Slatten retrial
  conviction.
  Counter-act (re-laundering of the interception):
  the pardons of 22 December 2020 under Article II,
  Section 2, Clause 1 of the US Constitution.
  Cost of escape (for the cost-bearers): 13 years
  from event to retrial conviction; 13 years and 3
  months from event to pardon; the 17 dead Iraqi
  civilians did not receive judicial accountability
  served (the sentences were not completed); civil
  settlements were not adjudications.
  Lesson, diagnostic: a court-level interception of
  a partial-scapegoat laundering is reversible at
  the procedural-superstructure level (pardon, plea
  reduction, jurisdictional change). The cost-
  bearers cannot rely on the conviction stage
  alone; the durability of accountability depends on
  the durability of the sentence as well.
  Status: partial-interception, with documented
  re-laundering. To be developed as the counter-case
  file paired with this case in beat 9 of the
  chapter.
```
