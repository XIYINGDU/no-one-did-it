# Chapter 4 — The Proxy and the Sponsor

> He desired to show that, if any cruelty had been practised, it had not originated with him, but in the natural sternness of the minister.
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *Il Principe*, ch. VII, trans. W. K. Marriott (1908)

On the night of February 26–27, 2014, armed men in unmarked green uniforms entered the Supreme Council of the Autonomous Republic of Crimea and the Council of Ministers building in Simferopol. They wore no insignia. They carried no national flag. They displayed no rank, no unit patch, no name tag. By morning, the parliamentary chamber was under their control.

Through the next twenty-four hours, similar formations appeared at Simferopol International Airport, at the military airfield at Belbek, at telecommunications hubs across the peninsula, and at the perimeter of every Ukrainian military installation outside the Russian Black Sea Fleet's leased Sevastopol garrison. The Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe Special Monitoring Mission — established by Permanent Council Decision No. 1117 on 21 March 2014[<sup>1</sup>](#c4-1) — would be repeatedly denied access at checkpoints staffed by these same uniforms. On 16 March 2014 a "referendum" would be held under armed occupation. On 18 March 2014 the Russian Federation would sign a treaty purporting to incorporate Crimea and Sevastopol[<sup>2</sup>](#c4-2).

The men in unmarked green were the visible actor. They were also, at that moment in the public record, no one in particular.

That arrangement is what follows. It is the relationship between a visible actor whose identification has been engineered out and a sponsor whose control over the act remains intact behind the engineering. The arrangement has a shape. The shape recurs. It recurs across state lines and across procedural categories. It recurs in the seizure of a peninsula by a state that denies any troops were sent. It recurs in the launch of a surface-to-air missile from a vehicle that was supplied by one military and operated by men classified as belonging to another. It recurs in the discharge of weapons by uniformed men who are not soldiers, in a city occupied by an army whose government has agreed in writing that those men are not subject to local courts.

This is the claim. The unmarked uniform in Simferopol, the unmarked Buk-TELAR in Donetsk Oblast, and the contractor protective gear of Raven 23 in Baghdad are three procedural shells covering the same mechanism. Once we can see the shape, we can see how the substitute is built and how the sponsor stays clean.

## Three denials, three vocabularies

Three official stories, told in three vocabularies, in three years, by three governments. They do not sound alike. Listen for shape, not vocabulary.

On 4 March 2014, in a press conference in his Novo-Ogaryovo residence, President Vladimir Putin was asked whether the uniformed personnel operating in Crimea were Russian soldiers. He answered that they were not. He said that anyone could buy such uniforms in a shop[<sup>3</sup>](#c4-3). The framing was repeated across the Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Foreign Ministry's spokeswoman. State media adopted the term "polite people" — *vezhlivye lyudi* — to describe the formations[<sup>4</sup>](#c4-4). The forces were local; they were spontaneous; they were a self-defense response to a government change in Kyiv.

On 21 July 2014, four days after Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 broke up in flight over eastern Donetsk Oblast, the Russian Ministry of Defence held a press conference in Moscow[<sup>5</sup>](#c4-5). Three claims were advanced in sequence. A Ukrainian Su-25 ground-attack aircraft had been observed in the vicinity of MH17. The Buk surface-to-air missile system involved had been supplied to Ukrainian forces from Soviet-era stocks; one was attributed to the Ukrainian 156th Anti-Aircraft Missile Regiment. The Russian Ministry of Defence's spokesman said that no Russian military unit was involved[<sup>6</sup>](#c4-6). The framings would be repeated in June and October 2015 press briefings by the missile manufacturer Almaz-Antey, which advanced its own technical case that the missile came from Ukrainian stocks and that the launch had occurred from territory under Ukrainian government control[<sup>7</sup>](#c4-7).

On 16 September 2007, the United States Department of State's initial response to the shooting at Nisour Square in Baghdad was that the convoy designated Raven 23 had come under attack from insurgents and had returned fire in self-defense[<sup>8</sup>](#c4-8). The convoy was a State Department protective detail operating under the Worldwide Personal Protective Services contract; the contractors were employees of Blackwater USA. The framing was supported by Blackwater's own contemporaneous statements, channeled through founder and chief executive Erik Prince and the corporation's communications office. The Iraqi Ministry of Interior reached the opposite conclusion within days[<sup>9</sup>](#c4-9). The US State Department's initial defense of the convoy held for hours into days, not weeks.

Three vocabularies. Three time zones. One shape. In each case, an event with documentable physical consequences was met by an official story in which a sponsoring institution that supplied the means and that controlled the territory of the act was not the actor that did the thing. The actor was someone else. The someone else was local self-defense, or armed-group fighters, or a private convoy in fear for its life. The shape is a category of denial in which the visible actor and the institutional actor are made to be different actors.

The first reversal here is acoustic, not analytical. Three governments in three years, each saying not us.

## A Buk from the 53rd Brigade

Of the three cases, the documentary record for MH17 is the most complete. The chain runs from a serial-equivalent identification of a missile launcher, to a convoy route across an international border, to a launch site, to a court verdict, to a regional human-rights tribunal, to an aviation regulator. MH17 carries the spine.

The aircraft was a Boeing 777-200ER. The flight had departed Amsterdam Schiphol for Kuala Lumpur. At 16:20 local time — 13:20 UTC — on 17 July 2014, the aircraft broke up in flight over eastern Donetsk Oblast. All 298 people on board were killed: 196 Dutch nationals, 43 Malaysians including the crew of fifteen, 27 Australians, 12 Indonesians, 10 British nationals, four Germans, four Belgians, three Filipinos, one Canadian, one New Zealander. Wreckage fell across sunflower fields near Hrabove and Rozsypne, in territory partially controlled by armed groups designating themselves the Donetsk People's Republic.

The Dutch Safety Board — Onderzoeksraad voor Veiligheid — was given lead responsibility for the air-safety investigation under International Civil Aviation Organization Annex 13 protocols[<sup>10</sup>](#c4-10). A separate Joint Investigation Team led by the Netherlands and including Australia, Belgium, Malaysia, and Ukraine was constituted for the criminal investigation. The two tracks would run in parallel for years.

The Dutch Safety Board issued its final report on 13 October 2015[<sup>11</sup>](#c4-11). The cause was a 9N314M warhead carried by a 9M38-series missile launched by a Buk surface-to-air system. The forensic basis was multi-modal: physical fragment analysis recovered from the wreckage and from the bodies of the crew, blast-pattern reconstruction, and engineering analysis of the cockpit damage. The missile manufacturer Almaz-Antey, in its June and October 2015 press conferences, did not contest that the cause was a Buk; it contested the version of the missile and the location of the launch[<sup>12</sup>](#c4-12).

The Joint Investigation Team's first major public step came on 28 September 2016. The team identified the launch site as a field near the village of Pervomaiskyi, in DPR-controlled territory, and stated that the Buk-TELAR — the Transporter Erector Launcher And Radar vehicle, which combines launcher, radar, and command functions in a single tracked vehicle — had been brought into Ukraine from the Russian Federation on 17 July 2014 and returned to Russia the same night[<sup>13</sup>](#c4-13). The team's evidentiary base included intercepted communications among armed-group fighters, witness statements, photographs and video from local residents, and reconstructions from open-source-intelligence groups, principally Bellingcat.

On 24 May 2018, in a presentation at the Dutch Marechaussee headquarters in Nieuwegein, the Joint Investigation Team announced that the specific Buk-TELAR used to shoot down MH17 had come from the inventory of the Russian 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade, based at Kursk in the Russian Federation's Western Military District[<sup>14</sup>](#c4-14). The identification was by serial-equivalent markings — wear patterns, modifications, and other physical features unique to that vehicle — corroborated against photographs of the Brigade's vehicles taken during prior exercises and during the June–July 2014 convoy. The Brigade itself was not on trial. The point of identification was provenance: this Buk, not a Buk.

On 19 June 2019, the Joint Investigation Team indicted four men[<sup>15</sup>](#c4-15). Igor Vsevolodovich Girkin, also known by his nom de guerre Strelkov, who in 2014 was the self-styled "Minister of Defence of the Donetsk People's Republic." Sergey Nikolayevich Dubinsky, a former officer of the Russian Main Intelligence Directorate and, in 2014, head of intelligence for the armed-group structures in Donetsk. Leonid Volodymyrovych Kharchenko, a field commander operating in the launch area on 17 July 2014. And Oleg Pulatov, Dubinsky's deputy. All four were charged with the murder of 298 persons and with causing the crash of an aircraft resulting in death.

The trial began at the Schiphol Justice Complex in March 2020, conducted by the Hague District Court. Only Pulatov was represented by counsel; the other three were tried in absentia. On 17 November 2022, the court delivered its judgment[<sup>16</sup>](#c4-16). Girkin, Dubinsky, and Kharchenko were convicted in absentia of both counts and sentenced to life imprisonment, with orders to pay compensation to next of kin. Pulatov was acquitted. None of the four was in Dutch custody at the time of the verdict; as of this writing, none has served the sentence imposed.

The chain on the ground had been adjudicated. The chain above the ground — from the Brigade and the Buk to the senior Russian command level that had decided to supply that weapon to forces operating across the border — remained outside any court. On 8 February 2023, the Joint Investigation Team delivered its closing statement. It announced that the criminal investigation was being closed for want of evidence sufficient to bring further charges to court. It also said this: there were strong indications that the decision to supply the Buk-TELAR to the armed forces in eastern Ukraine had been taken at a senior Russian command level, with the team's phrasing reaching what it described as presidential-level awareness[<sup>17</sup>](#c4-17). The statement is a prosecutorial conclusion at the close of investigation. It is not a court verdict. The Joint Investigation Team's finding speaks to the supply of the Buk. It does not speak to ordering the shootdown of an airliner. Hold that distinction. We will need it.

State responsibility, separate from individual criminal responsibility, was the work of a different forum. On 30 November 2022, the European Court of Human Rights, sitting as a Grand Chamber, admitted the joined cases brought by Ukraine and the Netherlands against Russia. The applications were filed under nos. 8019/16, 43800/14, and 28525/20[<sup>18</sup>](#c4-18). The Grand Chamber found, on admissibility, that Russia had exercised Article 1 jurisdiction over the territory from which the Buk was launched. The basis was spatial control, on the standard articulated in *Loizidou v. Turkey*, beginning 11 May 2014. On 9 July 2025, the merits judgment in the joined cases found multiple violations of the Convention, including in respect of MH17[<sup>19</sup>](#c4-19). A separate aviation-regulatory record came from a different body. On 12 May 2025, the Council of the International Civil Aviation Organization found that the Russian Federation had violated its obligations under Article 3 bis of the Chicago Convention in respect of MH17. This was the first such finding by ICAO against a state[<sup>20</sup>](#c4-20).

The taxonomy installed in chapter 2 names four shapes: pure scapegoat, partial scapegoat, system/object alibi, cost-bearing goat. MH17 is a hybrid. In its primary register it is a *partial scapegoat* case. The named individual operators are real participants in the chain. They fired the weapon. Three of them were convicted at a national court that ran adversarial proceedings — Pulatov's acquittal is the strongest evidence that the proceedings ran adversarially. Their personal culpability is on the record. But the chain was meant to stop at them, and not to climb the rung above — to the Brigade, to the supply decision, to the sponsor. In its secondary register, MH17 is a *system/object alibi* case. The "Donetsk People's Republic" itself functions as a non-state legal-political shell that no recognizing state has accepted, designed to be the category into which command for the Buk crew could disappear. Russian alternative-cause framings — the Su-25, the Ukrainian Buk, the 156th Regiment — were efforts to relocate the causal entity from a Russian state system to a Ukrainian state system. We resolve both layers. Neither layer is rhetorical.

What MH17 demonstrates that the Simferopol scene only hints at is the structure of the chain. There is a sponsor. There is a means. There is a territory of the act. There are operators. And there is a procedural shell between the operators and the sponsor, designed to absorb attribution so the chain stops at the operators. This is the architecture we examine in two further forms.

## Nisour Square, the contractor shell

Six years and three months before the Buk fired, an event of a different procedural form took place in Baghdad. The actor was not a state armed force in another country. The actor was not an armed group in unrecognized territory. The actor was a private corporation chartered in the United States, operating in Iraq under contract to the United States Department of State, on a mission to protect American diplomatic personnel. The procedural shell was a contract; the operators were employees.

On 16 September 2007, at approximately 12:08 local time, a four-vehicle Blackwater USA convoy designated Raven 23 entered Nisour Square, a traffic circle in central Baghdad. The convoy was assigned to the Bureau of Diplomatic Security under the Worldwide Personal Protective Services contract. Within minutes, the convoy team fired weapons that killed seventeen Iraqi civilians and wounded at least twenty more. Among the dead were Ahmed Al-Rubaie, Mahasin Mohssen Khadum Al-Khazali, and a nine-year-old boy named Ali Mohammed Hafidh Abdul Razzaq Kinani, killed alongside members of his family. No US personnel were killed at Nisour Square that day. The convoy reported being under attack and called in to State Department command.

The procedural architecture around the convoy on 16 September 2007 was several years in the making. On 27 June 2004, the Coalition Provisional Authority — the US-led occupation authority established by Order Number 1 in May 2003 — had issued the revised version of CPA Order Number 17, "Status of the Coalition Provisional Authority, MNF-Iraq, Certain Missions and Personnel in Iraq." Section 4 granted contractors immunity from Iraqi legal process for acts performed pursuant to the terms of their contracts[<sup>21</sup>](#c4-21). The CPA dissolved twenty-eight days later, on 28 June 2004, transferring sovereignty to an interim Iraqi government. CPA Order 17 was inherited by the new Iraqi state as a matter of occupation-era legal succession. Iraqi prosecution of Raven 23 under Iraqi law was, on 16 September 2007, not available.

US criminal jurisdiction was theoretically available through the Military Extraterritorial Jurisdiction Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 3261–3267 and amended in 2004[<sup>22</sup>](#c4-22). The 2004 amendments extended MEJA's reach to Department of Defense contractors operating overseas. Whether the 2004 amendments unambiguously covered State Department contractors was a contested question of statutory interpretation; the question would be resolved, in the Raven 23 case, by the federal courts.

The contractor classification was the procedural shell. Contractors are not soldiers. They are not subject to the Uniform Code of Military Justice. They are also not Iraqi nationals subject to Iraqi criminal courts when CPA Order 17 attaches. They occupy a category constructed by occupation-era regulation, sustained by inter-governmental agreement, and operated by a private corporation under a State Department contract.

The Federal Bureau of Investigation dispatched a team to Baghdad within days of the shooting. The team operated under the Department of Justice. Its investigation, conducted through late 2007 and into 2008, concluded that the shooting was unjustified for at least fourteen of the seventeen deaths[<sup>23</sup>](#c4-23). The Iraqi Ministry of Interior reached substantially the same conclusion through its own investigative process[<sup>24</sup>](#c4-24). The US Army's initial assessment of 21 September 2007 and the State Department's Diplomatic Security review reached findings critical of the convoy's actions. The House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform held a hearing on 2 October 2007, at which Erik Prince testified under oath[<sup>25</sup>](#c4-25). Prince was the founder and then-chief executive of Blackwater USA. He was not personally indicted in connection with the Nisour Square shooting.

On 8 December 2008, the Department of Justice filed a federal indictment against five members of the convoy team under MEJA: Nicholas A. Slatten, Paul A. Slough, Evan S. Liberty, Dustin L. Heard, and Jeremy P. Ridgeway[<sup>26</sup>](#c4-26). Ridgeway pleaded guilty to voluntary manslaughter and attempted manslaughter in December 2008 and cooperated with prosecutors. The other four stood trial. The Federation Council of the Russian Federal Assembly's resolution authorizing force in Ukraine on 1 March 2014 would be six years away; the Buk would fire seven years away.

The five-year chain to the first verdict was procedurally torturous. The District Court for the District of Columbia dismissed the case on 31 December 2009 on Fifth Amendment due-process grounds, finding that prosecutors had used statements the contractors had been compelled to give to the State Department under threat of job loss — *Kastigar*-protected statements that the prosecutors had failed to wall off from the investigators. The D.C. Circuit reversed in 2011 in *United States v. Slough*, 641 F.3d 544, reinstating the prosecution[<sup>27</sup>](#c4-27). Trial proceeded; on 22 October 2014, a federal jury convicted Slough, Liberty, and Heard of voluntary manslaughter and related counts, and Slatten on a lesser charge[<sup>28</sup>](#c4-28). On 4 August 2017, the D.C. Circuit again intervened. In *United States v. Slatten*, 865 F.3d 767, the appellate court vacated and remanded in part — vacating Slatten's murder conviction on severance grounds and remanding the sentences of Slough, Liberty, and Heard on Eighth Amendment grounds, the panel finding that the mandatory thirty-year firearms-enhancement sentence for use of a machine gun during a crime of violence was constitutionally infirm as applied to these defendants[<sup>29</sup>](#c4-29). Resentencing of the three on remand followed. On 19 December 2018, Slatten was convicted of first-degree murder at retrial in the District Court for the District of Columbia and sentenced to life imprisonment[<sup>30</sup>](#c4-30).

The chain ran for eleven years and three months. The four-defendant cohort had been investigated by the FBI, prosecuted by Department of Justice national-security and public-integrity attorneys, tried twice, sentenced once, resentenced once, retried once, and resentenced again. The factual finding — that the shooting was unjustified for at least fourteen of the seventeen deaths — survived multiple rounds of appellate review.

On 22 December 2020, President Donald J. Trump pardoned Slatten, Slough, Liberty, and Heard[<sup>31</sup>](#c4-31). The pardon power is exercised under Article II, Section 2, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution. The pardons were documented in a statement from the White House Office of the Press Secretary issued that day. A pardon does not vacate a conviction; the convictions remain on the record. The pardons released the defendants from serving their sentences.

We are not making a moral equivalence between an annexed peninsula, a downed airliner, and a corner of Baghdad. We are making a *mechanistic* equivalence. The shell is different in each case. The sponsor is different in each case. The procedural architecture is different in each case. What recurs is the architecture itself: a visible actor whose identification has been engineered into a category designed not to be the sponsoring institution, a means and a territory both controlled by an entity above the operator, and a chain of attribution that the procedural shell is designed to terminate before it reaches the sponsor. Nisour Square shows that the architecture is not particular to authoritarian-state plausible-deniability. It is a *civilizational* method that appears wherever the procedural shell can be built. CPA Order 17 was a US-drafted instrument. The contractor category was a US category. The "self-defense" framing was advanced by a US corporation. The mechanism is portable.

## A resolution, a verdict, an appeal

Three cases. Three procedural shells. Three record-hardening architectures. They produce different durable records by different routes.

For Crimea, the record was fixed by multilateral instruments and by a regional human-rights court. On 27 March 2014, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 68/262, "Territorial integrity of Ukraine," by 100 votes in favor, 11 against, and 58 abstentions[<sup>32</sup>](#c4-32). The resolution affirmed the territorial integrity of Ukraine and the invalidity of the 16 March referendum. Two regional bodies acted in parallel. The European Commission for Democracy through Law — the Venice Commission — had issued Opinion No. 762/2014, CDL-AD(2014)002, on 21 March 2014, finding the referendum incompatible with the Ukrainian Constitution and with European democratic standards[<sup>33</sup>](#c4-33). The Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe followed on 10 April 2014, suspending Russian delegation voting rights in Resolution 1990[<sup>34</sup>](#c4-34). The judicial record came later. On 16 December 2020, the European Court of Human Rights, sitting as a Grand Chamber, issued its admissibility decision in *Ukraine v. Russia (re Crimea)*, applications nos. 20958/14 and 38334/18. The Grand Chamber found that Russia had exercised effective control over Crimea from 27 February 2014[<sup>35</sup>](#c4-35). A separate criminal track opened at The Hague. The Office of the Prosecutor of the International Criminal Court opened a preliminary examination into the situation in Ukraine on 25 April 2014 and an investigation on 2 March 2022[<sup>36</sup>](#c4-36).

For MH17, the record was fixed by a national criminal court, a regional human-rights court, and an aviation regulator. United Nations Security Council Resolution 2166, passed unanimously on 21 July 2014 — including with Russia's vote — demanded international access to the crash site and a full, independent international investigation[<sup>37</sup>](#c4-37). A subsequent UN Security Council draft, S/2015/562, would have established a UNSC-mandated international tribunal for MH17; Russia vetoed it on 29 July 2015[<sup>38</sup>](#c4-38). The Hague District Court at Schiphol's judgment of 17 November 2022 — ECLI:NL:RBDHA:2022:12217 and companion judgments — produced the criminal conviction record[<sup>39</sup>](#c4-39). The European Court of Human Rights Grand Chamber admissibility decision of 30 November 2022 and the merits judgment of 9 July 2025 in the joined cases produced the state-responsibility record[<sup>40</sup>](#c4-40). The ICAO Council finding of 12 May 2025 produced the aviation-regulatory record under Article 3 bis of the Chicago Convention, an article added in 1984 in response to an earlier shootdown of a civilian airliner[<sup>41</sup>](#c4-41).

For Blackwater, the record was fixed by a national federal criminal court and by domestic legislative oversight. The appellate spine is two D.C. Circuit decisions: *United States v. Slough*, 641 F.3d 544 (D.C. Cir. 2011), and *United States v. Slatten*, 865 F.3d 767 (D.C. Cir. 2017)[<sup>42</sup>](#c4-42). The trial-court spine is the District Court records of 2014 and 2018, held under dockets including 1:14-cr-00107-RCL[<sup>43</sup>](#c4-43). The contractor-oversight architecture and its gaps were documented by three Government Accountability Office reports — GAO-06-865R of June 2006, GAO-08-966 of July 2008, and GAO-09-19 of October 2008[<sup>44</sup>](#c4-44). The contemporaneous contractor policy debate sits in two legislative records. The Senate Armed Services Committee issued its report on 11 December 2008. The House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform had held its hearing earlier, on 2 October 2007[<sup>45</sup>](#c4-45). The 22 December 2020 White House Office of the Press Secretary statement documented the pardons; the convictions remain on the record. The CPA Order 17 immunity structure was ended for security contractors going forward by the Status of Forces Agreement signed between the United States and the Republic of Iraq on 17 November 2008, which entered into force on 1 January 2009[<sup>46</sup>](#c4-46).

Three different record-control architectures: a multilateral judicial body, a cross-border criminal Joint Investigation Team backed by a national court, and a domestic federal court with appellate review. Each produced durable record. The institutional infrastructures are not the same. The locking function — the conversion of an event the sponsor would prefer to remain ambiguous into an event whose facts are fixed in instruments the sponsor does not control — is the same.

We have been with the case from the Simferopol opening; we can now name what each instrument did. UN GA Resolution 68/262 did not restore Crimea. It made the invalidity of the seizure a part of international law's documented record. The ECtHR Grand Chamber admissibility decision did not change who controlled the peninsula. It made the fact of Russian effective control a part of the judicial record of the Council of Europe. The Hague District Court judgment did not put three convicted men in custody. It made the murder of 298 persons by a Buk launched from a 53rd-Brigade vehicle a part of the criminal record of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. The Blackwater appellate decisions did not undo the events of 16 September 2007. They made the chain — investigation, indictment, conviction, appellate review, resentencing, retrial, conviction again, pardon — a part of the procedural record of the United States.

A record is not a remedy. We do not pretend that the two are the same. What we will say below is that the record is sometimes the only thing the cost-bearers ever get, and that the durability of the record is itself an outcome.

## Three counterarguments at full strength

We have been quick to name the architecture. We have been slower to listen to the strongest version of the case against doing so. Before the next move, three counterarguments deserve to be heard at full strength. We respond to each. We do not pretend they are weaker than they are.

The first counterargument concerns Crimea. The most defensible version of the Russian official position is this. The Crimean population's preference for closer ties with Russia was documented in pre-2014 polling and is not in dispute. The 1997 Partition Treaty placed Russian naval forces lawfully in Sevastopol; their presence was not novel. The 2010 Kharkiv Pact extended that arrangement. The 21 February 2014 change of government in Kyiv was, from the perspective of a substantial portion of Crimea's Russophone population, a constitutional rupture that they did not accept. The 16 March 2014 referendum, however imperfect, captured a measurable sentiment. President Putin's later televised admissions can be read as political assertion in front of a domestic audience rather than as legal admissions in a binding sense. The case for restraint, on this reading, is that we are reading a domestic political process — local preference, lawful basing, constitutional dispute — as if it were a foreign aggression.

We do not contest the existence of pre-2014 sentiment. We do not contest the lawful 1997 basing. We do not adjudicate the constitutional question of the February 2014 government change in Kyiv. Our claim is narrower. A forcible seizure of parliamentary buildings and military installations by unmarked forces of a state that denied their presence is not authorized by local sentiment or by naval-basing rights. The 1994 Budapest Memorandum, signed by the Russian Federation, the United Kingdom, and the United States and acceded to by France and China, committed the signatories to respect the independence, sovereignty, and existing borders of Ukraine[<sup>47</sup>](#c4-47). UN Charter Article 2(4) binds Member States regardless of local political preferences inside the target state[<sup>48</sup>](#c4-48). As for the "boasting" framing of Putin's televised admissions, the International Law Commission's Articles on State Responsibility provide pathways for attribution: Article 4 attributes the conduct of state organs to the state, and Article 11 attributes private conduct that a state subsequently acknowledges and adopts as its own[<sup>49</sup>](#c4-49). The 17 April 2014 *Direct Line* and 15 March 2015 *Crimea: The Way Home* statements function as Article 4 evidence of organ direction in the Crimea operation, without regard to whether the acknowledgement was solicited or volunteered. The 17 April 2014 *Direct Line* and the 15 March 2015 documentary *Crimea: The Way Home* are quoted with attribution[<sup>50</sup>](#c4-50). They are not adopted as our framing of legitimacy. They are quoted as on-record statements of state direction by the person in command — and that is what we quote them for.

The second counterargument concerns MH17. The Joint Investigation Team is led by the Netherlands, a country whose citizens died in the largest single number. That structure raises a question about investigatory neutrality. The Joint Investigation Team's 8 February 2023 closing statement on presidential-level Russian decision-making is a prosecutorial conclusion at the close of investigation; it is not a court verdict. The Hague convictions of November 2022 were rendered in absentia, without cross-examination of the defendants. The only defendant represented by counsel at trial — Pulatov — was acquitted, which on this reading raises a question about the strength of the evidence against the others.

We accept the procedural distinction and preserve it throughout. State-responsibility framing rests on the ECtHR — the 30 November 2022 admissibility decision and the 9 July 2025 merits judgment — and on the ICAO Council finding of 12 May 2025. It does not rest on the Joint Investigation Team's closing statement. Individual-responsibility framing rests on the Hague convictions in absentia, with the in-absentia qualifier stated each time and with Pulatov's acquittal honored. The Joint Investigation Team's 8 February 2023 statement is used only with the prosecutorial-finding qualifier; we do not say "Putin ordered the shootdown of an airliner," because no court has found that and the Joint Investigation Team's finding speaks to supply, not to ordering. As for the investigatory-neutrality challenge, the evidentiary base was multi-modal — intercepted communications, satellite imagery, photographic and video material from local residents and open-source investigators, witness statements, physical fragment analysis, and the missile manufacturer's own admissions of missile type — and was tested adversarially in a national court that *differentiated the evidence per defendant*. The Pulatov acquittal is, on our reading, the strongest evidence that the proceedings ran adversarially. A court that convicted everyone in front of it would invite the criticism the counterargument advances. A court that acquitted one and convicted three did not behave that way. A residual point survives. The JIT, the OSCE SMM, and the open-source-intelligence base on which the Buk-TELAR identification rested all sit inside a Western evidentiary ecosystem. We do not claim that ecosystem is neutral; we claim the evidence was multi-modal and that the Pulatov acquittal is the strongest available evidence that the proceedings were adversarial. The chain rests on what survives that qualification.

The third counterargument concerns Blackwater. The convoy operated in a high-threat environment under rules of engagement that were the subject of ongoing congressional inquiry, in a city where convoys were attacked routinely and in which one Blackwater contractor had been killed days earlier in an unrelated incident. The 2017 D.C. Circuit decision identified constitutional problems with the firearms-enhancement sentence and with Slatten's joint trial; the panel's analysis was substantive, and the remand was substantive. The pardons of 22 December 2020 are a lawful exercise of Article II, Section 2, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution. Framing the pardons as "getting away with it" would mistake a constitutional remedy inside the system for one outside it.

We do not contest the constitutional validity of the pardons. We do not say "they got away with it." We narrate the chain in procedural-stage sequence: CPA Order 17 immunity, FBI investigation, MEJA indictment, *Kastigar* dismissal, D.C. Circuit reinstatement, 2014 convictions, 2017 D.C. Circuit reversal-in-part and remand, 2018 Slatten retrial and resentencing of the others, 22 December 2020 pardons. The pardon is a documented step in that chain. It is not a verdict on the chain. The constitutional issues identified by the 2017 D.C. Circuit decision were procedural; they were addressed on remand by resentencing and retrial. The underlying factual finding — that the shooting was unjustified for at least fourteen of the seventeen deaths — survived appellate review. Our claim is durability: the chain ran for thirteen years from FBI investigation in 2007 to the 22 December 2020 pardons, and produced convictions that the President released the defendants from serving; the convictions remain on the record. That is a fact about the architecture. Whatever inference the chain supports is for us to draw, not to be drawn for us.

Three counterarguments. Three responses. The architecture survives the responses; we survive the architecture.

## The sponsor narrates the order

The image we opened with — the uniform without identification — returns now in three forms, and we watch each one collapse.

In Simferopol, the unmarked uniform was the seizure formation itself. The collapse was not external. It was the sponsor's own televised acknowledgement, delivered first in fragmentary form and then in full. On 17 April 2014, during the annual *Direct Line with Vladimir Putin* televised question-and-answer programme broadcast on the major Russian state channels, Putin stated that Russian servicemen had backed the Crimean self-defense forces[<sup>51</sup>](#c4-51). The earlier denial — *anyone can buy such uniforms in a shop*, delivered six weeks earlier — was thereby retired. Almost a year later, on 15 March 2015, in the documentary *Crimea: The Way Home*, directed by Andrey Kondrashov and broadcast on Rossiya-1, Putin stated that he had personally ordered the operation, including movement of special-operations forces, Main Intelligence Directorate personnel, and Black Sea Fleet units[<sup>52</sup>](#c4-52). The uniform engineered to belong to no one belonged, on the sponsor's own account, to him. The 1 March 2014 Federation Council Decision No. 48-SF — "On the use of the Armed Forces of the Russian Federation on the territory of Ukraine" — was on the public record from the day it was issued and contradicted the 4 March 2014 denial three days later[<sup>53</sup>](#c4-53); but the Federation Council resolution alone did not pierce the public framing. The pierce came when the sponsoring head of state, on state television, narrated the order. The collapse was performed, not extracted.

In Donetsk Oblast, the unmarked or repainted Buk-TELAR moved across an international border on 17 July 2014. The collapse came at three points and was extracted, not performed. The first point was the Dutch Safety Board's 13 October 2015 report identifying the missile system. The second was the Joint Investigation Team's 28 September 2016 presentation locating the launch site and tracking the convoy across the border. The third — the moment the alibi finally broke — was the 24 May 2018 presentation matching the Buk-TELAR by serial-equivalent markings to the Russian 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade at Kursk. The match was made because the Brigade had taken photographs of its vehicles during exercises, because the open-source-intelligence community had documented the convoy, and because the Joint Investigation Team had the patience to put the two records next to each other. The collapse was made of evidence that the Brigade itself had created.

In Baghdad, the contractor protective-detail kit — the body armor without rank, the unmarked or differently-marked vehicles, the equipment of a category neither soldier nor civilian — was the operational image. The collapse came when the FBI investigation concluded that the shooting was unjustified for at least fourteen of the seventeen deaths, when the Department of Justice indicted under MEJA, when the District Court tried the case, and when the convoy team's own behavior in the square was reconstructed in trial exhibits. The collapse extended for years. Each procedural step extracted more record from the contractor classification's shell.

Three alibi-collapse moments. The sponsor's own broadcast pierced one. A multinational investigative team pierced the second. A federal court system pierced the third. The mechanism that produced the three uniformed images is the same. The institutions that pierced them are not.

The proxy-deniability arrangement is a single method. The institutions facing it are also a method — or, more accurately, several different methods, each suited to a different shell. The pierce comes when the chosen institution has the right tools for the shell. It does not come otherwise.

## Three questions for the proxy

Chapter 3 installed eight questions. We install three more here. The three are the eight at work on a proxy case.

Faced with a visible actor whose identification has been engineered out — an unmarked uniform, an unmarked vehicle, a contractor classification, a non-state armed-group designation, an independent reviewer hired by the entity being reviewed, an arm's-length affiliate that handles the part of the business that would embarrass the parent — ask three questions of the proxy:

> 1. **Who supplied the means?**
> 2. **Who controls the territory of the act?**
> 3. **Who would lose access if the proxy were prosecuted?**

When the answers converge on a single entity, that entity is the sponsor. The proxy is the shell. The shell is real, and someone built it.

Run them on MH17 as the worked example. The means was a Buk-TELAR from the 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade at Kursk. The territory of the launch was, per the ECtHR's effective-control finding, under Russian jurisdiction since 11 May 2014. The entity that would lose access from a state-level prosecution was the Russian state, which retained operational reach into eastern Ukraine through the supply chain the Joint Investigation Team mapped. The three answers converge on one entity. Now run the same three questions against the Crimea seizure and against Nisour Square. Every fact needed is already laid down above. The answers converge in both cases too — but the recognition only sticks if we do the work.

The three questions specialize the eight. They are not a replacement. They are how the eight land when the case in front of us is a proxy case. The eight find the responsibility chain in any case; the three find the sponsor in this kind of case. Where the answers diverge, we are not looking at a proxy arrangement. Where they converge, we are.

Hold the rule. Use it on every case we have not yet seen.

## Seven channels of interception

We have been alternating between two registers — the architecture of the laundering, and the architecture of its interception. The two are not separate. Our load-bearing observation is that the second architecture is as multi-channel as the first. Crimea, MH17, and Blackwater each show the proxy mechanism running through a different shell. They also show interception running through different channels — and they show interception being undone, or being incomplete, in different ways.

Lay them side by side. Read the matrix one channel at a time.

> *Audio-form rendering of the matrix that follows. Print and screen readers may skip to the table.*
>
> Seven channels of interception, three cases. The first channel is independent technical verification. For Crimea, OSCE monitors were denied access; satellite imagery and convoy footage carried the load. Partial. For MH17, the Dutch Safety Board, Bellingcat's open-source work, and the Joint Investigation Team's convoy reconstruction all succeeded. For Blackwater, the FBI's on-scene investigation and the Iraqi Ministry of Interior both succeeded. The second channel is multilateral body or regulator. For Crimea, the UN General Assembly resolution, the Venice Commission opinion, and the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe each fixed the record. For MH17, the UN Security Council passed Resolution 2166 unanimously, then Russia vetoed the tribunal draft a year later; the ICAO Council finding of 2025 was the first against a state. For Blackwater, the Senate Armed Services Committee, the House Oversight hearing, and the GAO reports fixed the record. The third channel is a state-responsibility venue. For Crimea, the European Court of Human Rights Grand Chamber found Russian effective control from February 2014. For MH17, the same court admitted in 2022 and ruled on the merits in 2025; the ICAO finding stands alongside. For Blackwater, no state-responsibility proceeding ran; CPA Order 17's immunity was repealed prospectively by the US–Iraq Status of Forces Agreement. The fourth channel is criminal charges against named individuals. For Crimea, none — no Russian individual has been charged for the 2014 seizure. For MH17, Girkin, Dubinsky, and Kharchenko were convicted in absentia in 2022; Pulatov was acquitted. For Blackwater, Slatten was convicted of first-degree murder at retrial; Slough, Liberty, and Heard of voluntary manslaughter; Ridgeway pleaded. The fifth channel is criminal charges at the sponsor or executive layer. For Crimea, none. For MH17, the Joint Investigation Team's 2023 closing statement reached "strong indications" of senior-level Russian decision-making — a prosecutorial finding, not a court verdict, and no indictment followed. For Blackwater, no charge against Erik Prince or named State Department officials. The sixth channel is sentence served. For Crimea, not applicable. For MH17, none of the three convicted are in Dutch custody. For Blackwater, sentences were imposed on remand and then the President pardoned the four on 22 December 2020. The seventh channel is cost-bearer status. For Crimea, the Tatar Mejlis was banned in April 2016, citizenship was forced, conscription began in 2015, and detentions are documented by the UN human-rights office; the territory has not been restored. For MH17, 298 dead, families carried a decade of litigation cost, no extradition or sentence service as of this writing. For Blackwater, seventeen Iraqi civilians dead, civil settlements paid through US-court-mediated process, no judicial accountability served and pardons released the defendants from serving.

![Three proxy wars across seven channels of accountability — where each channel succeeded, stalled, or was never attempted, from a state-direct case to a state-via-shell to a private contractor.](book/evidence/diagrams/proofs/ch04-interception-matrix.png)

Three proxy forms, seven channels apiece: the interception is real, and so is the re-laundering, and both belong on the same diagnostic.

When you read that a case has been closed, or resolved, or addressed — look at every channel. Look at which channels closed it. Look at which channels reversed the closing. The record is the durable artifact. The sentence is not.

The proxy-deniability arrangement is one method, and the institutions that can intercept it are several methods. The contest between them runs across many years and many forums. Following only one channel — only the criminal trial, only the regulator's finding, only the human-rights court's judgment — will sometimes mislead us into concluding that interception succeeded when sentencing failed, or that interception failed when record-fixing succeeded. With the matrix in front of us, we can see what each channel did. We can see what the *next* channel did. We can see what *no* channel did.

The first move, in chapter 3, was to install the diagnostic. The second move, here, is to see the diagnostic running across a multi-channel architecture of interception and its undoing.

The proxy is a method. The pierce is a method. Knowing the proxy is half of the recognition. The other half is knowing what each channel can and cannot do — and what the next channel will do to what the prior channel did.

Recognise it first. The proxy-deniability pattern shows up in non-headline life every time a more powerful entity uses a less powerful, less visible, or differently-categorized intermediary to do something the more powerful entity wants done but would not survive doing in its own name. A corporate parent running labor practices through a temp agency or staffing firm whose contract terms shield the parent from employment-law exposure. A platform-economy company structuring drivers, sellers, raters, or moderators as "independent contractors" to keep liability and benefits at the contractor level. A human-resources function routing a difficult termination through an outside investigator whose recommendation the company then "must" follow. A procurement organization placing a supplier with a long-known compliance problem one layer down via a subcontractor. A political figure operating through a "civic foundation" or "non-profit affiliate" that carries a message without the candidate's name on it. The recognition trigger is the pairing of a *legal-person category* with a procedural shell. The visible actor is structurally constructed *not* to be the entity that would otherwise be the obvious one.

Diagnose it next. Three observable markers borrowed directly from the three cases above. First, the visible actor has been engineered to be unidentifiable in the relevant dimension. A uniform without insignia. A shell-corporation structure. A contractor classification. An "independent investigation found" with a payment from the entity being investigated. Second, the means came from the sponsor. The Buk came from the 53rd Brigade; the contract came from the State Department; the funding came from the parent; the training came from the principal. The chain of supply is recoverable even when the chain of command is denied. Third, when the visible actor faces accountability, the sponsor's access does not change. The territory remains. The contract continues. The parent retains the customer relationship. The political figure keeps the office. When all three markers are present, we are looking at a proxy-deniability arrangement, not an arm's-length relationship.

Act on it. The three-question rule is the action.

> - **Who supplied the means?**
> - **Who controls the territory of the act?**
> - **Who would lose access if the proxy were prosecuted?**

Three different roles run the rule differently. For those of us named as the responsible actor for something whose means came from above, whose territory was controlled above, and whose prosecution would cost someone else access — get those three answers in writing before accepting the framing. The Blackwater convoy team's federal trial shows how high the procedural cost of un-laundering can run; the cost is lower when the chain is documented contemporaneously rather than reconstructed eleven years later. For those of us in the bystander seat, reading a story about a "non-state armed group," a "contractor," a "subsidiary," or a "rogue committee" — ask which sponsor supplies, which sponsor controls the territory, and which sponsor loses access. If the reporting cannot answer those three, the reporting is part of the alibi. For those of us with some institutional power — the manager, the board member, the procurement officer, the regulator, the journalist, the voter — refuse to accept a chain that stops at the visible actor when the means and the territory and the access all sit upstream. The institutional question is structural: what record would the institution need to maintain to make the chain visible? Then maintain it.

Avoid becoming the proxy. We are being positioned as the proxy when we are being asked to be the visible actor for something whose means, training, instructions, or political cover came from above — and when our visibility is *structured* to be the only visibility. Warning signs from the three cases above. We are being issued equipment without our employer's insignia. We are being asked to sign in our personal capacity for an institutional act. Our classification — contractor, subcontractor, volunteer, "independent expert" — was chosen by the upstream party, not by us. The explanation our employer offered the public names us and not them. The Blackwater contractors were inside a legal-status shell that the State Department relied on and that CPA Order 17 maintained. The shell was real; their exposure to prosecution was also real. The shell did not protect them when interception came. The first person to put their name on the unmarked vehicle carries the prosecution.

The unmarked uniform in Simferopol, the unmarked Buk-TELAR moving across the border at Donetsk Oblast, the contractor convoy in Nisour Square — these were three rungs of the same ladder. The ladder is the institution. Once we can see the ladder, we can see where the next rung will be built. Part II's remaining work runs this same recognition on patterns the proxy form has tried to hide.
