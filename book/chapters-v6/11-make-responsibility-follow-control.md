# Chapter 11 — Make Responsibility Follow Control

> In those scriptures it has been laid down that kings should stand with the rod of chastisement uplifted in their hands.
>
> — *The Mahabharata*, Book 12, Śānti Parva, Rājadharmānuśāsana Parva, Section CXXI, trans. Kisari Mohan Ganguli (1883–1896)

Three documents, all from the last ten years, all signed by officials acting within their authority. None contains an unlawful sentence. Each leaves a hole the diagnostic of chapter 3 walks into.

The first is the Boeing Deferred Prosecution Agreement of 7 January 2021. The Statement of Facts admits that two named Boeing 737 Technical Pilots made misleading statements to the Federal Aviation Administration's Aircraft Evaluation Group about the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System[<sup>1</sup>](#c11-1). The corporation paid approximately $2.5 billion. The agreement filed no indictment against any officer above the Technical Pilot layer. As ch-2 installed, and as ch-5 walked at full depth in the anchor case, the chain that produced the Lion Air and Ethiopian crashes did not climb to the engineering executives who approved MCAS's 2016 authority expansion, did not climb to the Type Rating commercial-differentiator decision, did not climb to the Organization Designation Authorization framework under which Boeing employees signed for the FAA. The agreement closed at the rank the agreement chose to close at. Nothing in its text said which person, by name, bore the consequence if the certification questions failed.

The second document is the 1 August 2002 Office of Legal Counsel memorandum on the meaning of "torture" under 18 U.S.C. § 2340A, signed by Assistant Attorney General Jay S. Bybee and principally drafted by John C. Yoo[<sup>2</sup>](#c11-2). The memorandum was withdrawn by Acting OLC head Daniel Levin on 30 December 2004[<sup>3</sup>](#c11-3). As ch-2 installed at the partial-scapegoat layer and as ch-9 returned to at the executive-legal-opinion layer, the OLC's role inside the executive branch is to give the official legal interpretation by which the executive will act. The opinion was authored. The opinion was signed. The opinion was retracted. No person in the OLC chain — author, signatory, supervising Attorney General — was the subject of an SEC-style attestation requirement, a regulator's named-function allocation, or a statutory inquiry with compulsory-evidence power over the drafting process. The opinion is a document. The document had a signature line. The signature line was not, by design, the seam at which any subsequent inquiry could fix individual accountability.

The third document is Executive Order 13328 of 6 February 2004, which chartered the Commission on the Intelligence Capabilities of the United States Regarding Weapons of Mass Destruction — the body we earlier called Robb-Silberman[<sup>4</sup>](#c11-4). Chapter 10 installed the order's load-bearing feature: by its own terms it scoped the Commission's mandate to the intelligence community's product and did not extend it to how policymakers used that product. The order's Section 2 mandate charged the Commission to assess the Intelligence Community's capacity to collect, analyse, and disseminate; it named no authority to examine how policymakers used the resulting product. The order is two pages. It contains no concealment. It does what it says. The scope is the design — by what the order grants, not by what it excludes.

Three designs. Each is a sentence in a corporate plea, a Justice Department memorandum, or an executive order. Each, on its face, contains nothing the diagnostic of chapter 3 names as laundering. The hidden architecture is somewhere else. The hidden architecture is what the document does not contain — a named officer who bears the consequence of the control question failing at the layer the document touches. Each was lawful, candid, and self-explaining; the single thing each was scrupulous enough to leave out was a person.

The fair clue: in each case, the opposite design choice exists in another jurisdiction, or in another statute on the same country's books, or in a different historical moment of the same administration. The variable is not the intent. The variable is the design. What follows is how to read for that variable, and what to do when reading is not enough.

## Three documents, no name

The institutional defence of each of the three design absences is on the public record. The defences are not weak. Each has its scholarly literature, its court precedent, its public-policy advocates. We take them at full strength before turning the diagnostic on them.

The defence of the Boeing DPA structure runs through Department of Justice prosecution policy. Corporate criminal liability under *New York Central R.R. v. United States*, 212 U.S. 481 (1909), reaches the corporation through the acts of its agents. The Department's policy under the Principles of Federal Prosecution of Business Organizations conditions corporate plea negotiations on the corporation's cooperation, including the production of evidence against individual wrongdoers[<sup>5</sup>](#c11-5). The defence holds that individual prosecution and corporate plea operate as a single bargaining instrument; conditioning the corporate plea on individual prosecution would, on the defence's account, chill the cooperation by which both are reached. The corporation is the party at the table. The corporation can be punished. The individual prosecution is a downstream question that the cooperation may or may not produce. The fact that no Boeing officer above the Technical Pilot layer was indicted is not, on this account, a design absence. It is the operation of the design.

The defence of the OLC opinion process runs through the constitutional separation of powers. Office of Legal Counsel opinions are internal executive-branch guidance binding on the executive in the absence of judicial review. The OLC's role is to give the executive its best legal advice; the lawyer's role within the executive is the lawyer's role anywhere, namely to advise the client. Attorney–client privilege adheres in part. Imposing an SEC-style attestation requirement on OLC opinions would, on this defence, convert legal advice into a public certification regime that the constitutional design did not contemplate and that would, in effect, give the legal opinion the force of law independent of its policy use. The opinion is a document. The remedy for a bad opinion, on this account, is withdrawal, public criticism, and the appointment of better lawyers. Statutory attestation is the wrong instrument.

The defence of the Robb-Silberman charter scope runs through inquiry-design prudence. An inquiry empowered to examine how policymakers used intelligence would, on this defence, become a political instrument by which one administration adjudicates the previous administration's policy choices. The fenced charter is, on this account, the institutional discipline that distinguishes a technical inquiry from a partisan one. The Commission's mandate, framed by Section 2 of the order around the intelligence community's capacity to collect, analyse, and disseminate, was, in this view, the condition under which any honest examination of the WMD failure could proceed in 2004.

And underlying all three defences sits a fourth, broader defence of the design status quo. It is the libertarian / regulatory-capture line associated with Henry Manne, Frank Easterbrook, and Gary Becker in different forms, and it is the dominant steelman to come: design moves that impose individual responsibility on senior officers risk producing defensive compliance rather than actual accountability, criminalising operational judgment, and deterring qualified individuals from senior roles. We hold this argument open and engage it at full strength below.

Each defence is internally consistent. Each is the public account given by a serious institution of why the design is the design. The diagnostic does not refute any of them at the level on which they operate. The diagnostic asks a different question.

## The diagnostic turned forward

The diagnostic of chapter 3 — its eight questions of blame, control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, cost, and what responsibility would look like if it followed control instead of visibility — has done its work after the harm across the cases we have installed: Bhopal at the chairman layer in chapter 5, family separation through the misdemeanor statute in chapter 6, the Horizon prosecutions through the five-role conflation of the Post Office in chapter 7, the second-term proclamation arc in chapter 8, the AI training-data and deployment grammar in chapter 9, the Iraq WMD case through the National Intelligence Estimate in chapter 10. It walks backward from the visible blamed party. It reaches the named decision-maker who controlled the layer at which the harm could have been stopped.

Chapter 11 turns the same eight questions forward. Run as a design instrument rather than a forensic instrument, each question becomes a specification. *Who would be publicly blamed?* That is the design's exposed surface — the Technical Pilot named in the Boeing DPA's Statement of Facts, before any question of who above him was indicted. *Who would have control?* That is the design's named locus — the Acme Markets president who, by reason of his position, had the authority to direct sanitation at the Baltimore warehouse. *Who would know?* That is the design's attestation point — the chief financial officer signing the § 302 certification under penalty of § 906. *Who could prevent?* That is the design's intervention window — the senior manager allocated, by written Statement of Responsibilities, the function in which the contravention occurred. *Who would control the record?* That is the design's audit boundary — the inquiry chair's section 21 notice, by which Cabinet papers and JIC assessments are compelled into the inquiry's record. Who would bear the cost if the layer fails? That is the design's accountability seam — the named individual whose signature, allocation, or compelled testimony fixes the consequence to a person rather than to a procedural shell.

Four statutes and doctrines, all currently on the books, demonstrate the inversion at four different layers of institutional life.

The first is the Park doctrine of responsible-corporate-officer liability under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The doctrine takes its name from *United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658 (1975), in which the Supreme Court affirmed the misdemeanor conviction of Acme Markets, Inc. president John R. Park for adulterated-food violations at company warehouses, holding that the FDCA reaches an executive who, by reason of his position in the corporation, has "the responsibility and authority either to prevent in the first instance, or promptly to correct, the violation complained of"[<sup>6</sup>](#c11-6). The doctrine is conventionally described, in corporate-law casebooks, as strict liability under the FDCA's misdemeanor structure, with an "objective impossibility" defense framed around the officer's responsible relation and authority to prevent or correct. The opportunity-to-prevent reading we rely on — the design-rule reading that treats Park as conditioned on the executive's position in the responsibility chain and the executive's opportunity to prevent the violation — is one defensible reading of the Court's text and is associated with the Sepinwall line of scholarship; the casebook line treats Park as strict liability. We use the design-rule reading because it maps Park onto the named-attribution specification we are arguing for. The named officer is the certifying officer of the corporation's compliance at the relevant operating layer.

The second is Sarbanes-Oxley §§ 302 and 906, enacted in the wake of Enron, WorldCom, and the 2002 audit-failure cluster. Section 302, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 7241 (as implemented by SEC Rules 13a-14 and 15d-14), requires the chief executive officer and chief financial officer of each issuer to certify in each periodic report that they have reviewed the report, that based on their knowledge it does not contain material misstatements, that based on their knowledge the financial statements fairly present the issuer's condition, and that they have designed and evaluated the issuer's internal controls[<sup>7</sup>](#c11-7). Section 906, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1350, attaches a criminal penalty to knowingly false certifications: up to ten years' imprisonment for a knowing violation, twenty for a wilful violation[<sup>8</sup>](#c11-8). The named officers are the CEO and the CFO. The named act is a personal signature attached to a periodic filing. The named state of mind for the criminal penalty is knowing falsity.

The third is the United Kingdom's Senior Managers and Certification Regime. The pattern, in one sentence: every senior function inside a regulated firm must be allocated, in writing, to a single named individual who can be held to it by the regulator. The statutory plumbing follows.

The regime was inserted into the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 by the Financial Services (Banking Reform) Act 2013. The inserted sections — 59ZA, 59A, and 59B — govern the approval and statements of responsibilities for senior managers. The regime is operationalised through the Financial Conduct Authority's Senior Management Arrangements, Systems and Controls sourcebook. SYSC 4.7 governs the allocation of senior management responsibilities. SYSC 24 governs the allocation of prescribed responsibilities under the regime. SYSC 27 governs the certification-employees layer[<sup>9</sup>](#c11-9).

The regime has three pillars that the design-rule argument must distinguish, because they operate at different layers of the firm. (1) The Senior Managers Regime: an individual exercising a Senior Management Function must be pre-approved by the regulator, must have a written Statement of Responsibilities, and bears the statutory duty under FSMA s.66A(5) to take such steps as a person in their position could reasonably be expected to take to avoid contraventions in their area of responsibility. (2) The Certification Regime, under FSMA ss.63E–63F: the firm itself certifies, annually, that staff in specified non-approved functions are fit and proper; certification is firm-issued, not regulator-approved. (3) The Conduct Rules (FCA COCON): individual-conduct standards applicable to a broader population of staff. The design-rule argument operates specifically at the Senior Managers Regime layer — the SMF + Statement of Responsibilities + s.66A(5) duty — not at the regime as a whole. One further point on burden: the Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016 removed the regime's original reverse burden of proof. The regulator must now prove that the senior manager failed to take reasonable steps. Under the regime, every Senior Management Function within an authorised firm must be allocated, by a written Statement of Responsibilities, to a named individual. The named officer is whichever individual the firm has allocated the function to. The named record is the Statement of Responsibilities and the firm's responsibilities map.

The fourth is the United Kingdom's Inquiries Act 2005, and within it the compulsory-evidence power granted at section 21. The Act authorises a Minister to establish a statutory public inquiry chaired by a person appointed under section 4[<sup>10</sup>](#c11-10). Section 21 empowers the chair, by notice in writing, to require a person to attend, to give evidence, or to produce documents, with criminal penalty for non-compliance under section 35[<sup>11</sup>](#c11-11). The named officer is the inquiry chair. The named record is whatever the chair compels into the inquiry's record. The named instrument by which the chair extracts cabinet papers, internal correspondence, classified material under appropriate restriction, is a section 21 notice.

Each of these is, in our vocabulary, a *named-attribution design*. Each translates one or more of the diagnostic's eight questions into a specification that operates before the harm rather than after it. The matrix below shows what each design forces an answer to, and what each leaves unmapped.

## The president of Acme Markets

The Park doctrine is older than nearly every modern laundering case we have treated. Its statutory anchor — the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 — was enacted thirty-six years before the Bhopal warehouse audit and forty-six years before the Bhopal disaster we installed earlier at the Anderson layer.

The case the doctrine takes its name from is unremarkable on its facts. Acme Markets, Inc. was a national retail food chain. Its president, John R. Park, was charged on a five-count misdemeanor information under the FDCA after the Food and Drug Administration found, in 1971 and 1972, that warehouses operated by Acme were contaminated with rodent infestation that had reached food held for sale. The contamination was real. The FDA's prior warnings to Acme management — including a letter informing Park that the Baltimore warehouse showed evidence of rodent infestation — were on the record. Park's defence at trial was that the company had a written compliance program, that operational control of warehouse sanitation had been delegated to subordinate employees, and that he had no personal involvement in the specific contamination found.

The Supreme Court, in a 6-3 decision authored by Chief Justice Warren E. Burger, affirmed Park's conviction. The relevant passage stands at 421 U.S. 671 to 674. The FDCA, the Court held, imposes "not only a positive duty to seek out and remedy violations when they occur but also, and primarily, a duty to implement measures that will insure that violations will not occur"; it reaches "all persons who, in respect of an act in violation of the Act, share responsibility in the business process out of which the act resulted"; and the requirement that the responsible-relation standard fall within "the range of the agent's authority" preserves the distinction between an officer who could have prevented the violation and one whose role was unrelated to it[<sup>12</sup>](#c11-12).

We read the Court's standard as one of opportunity-to-prevent: a defendant convicted under Park has, by virtue of the position he occupied, the authority and the responsibility to prevent the violation in the first instance or promptly to correct it. (As flagged earlier, the casebook line characterises Park as FDCA strict liability with an objective-impossibility defense; the opportunity-to-prevent framing is our design-rule reading, not the only available one.) The opportunity must be a real opportunity. *Park* itself, on the trial record affirmed by the Supreme Court, turned on the FDA's prior written warning to Park, which the record documents he received[<sup>13</sup>](#c11-13). A subordinate-only structure with no notice to the named officer would not on its face satisfy the standard.

The doctrine has been affirmed and tested at the senior-officer layer in subsequent decades. The *DeCoster* line is the modern test case. In *United States v. DeCoster*, 828 F.3d 626 (8th Cir. 2016), cert. denied, 137 S. Ct. 2160 (2017), the Eighth Circuit affirmed three-month prison sentences imposed on father-and-son Quality Egg executives Austin "Jack" DeCoster and Peter DeCoster under the responsible-corporate-officer doctrine, after the executives pleaded guilty to FDCA misdemeanor counts in connection with the 2010 salmonella outbreak. The panel divided on Eighth-Amendment and due-process objections to incarcerating an RCO defendant who had pleaded guilty without admission of personal knowledge of the violation[<sup>14</sup>](#c11-14). *DeCoster* did not narrow Park. *DeCoster* extended Park to actual prison time, against the strongest contemporary constitutional objections, at the senior-officer layer the doctrine was originally designed for. The doctrine exists; the doctrine has been tested; the doctrine has produced prison sentences against named senior officers in the half-century since *Park*; the constitutional objections to incarceration absent personal knowledge are live in the dissents but did not carry.

Park is not, on our reading, a finished design. Park is the *named design move* — codified in statute and judicially expounded — from which the later moves we now walk descend. SOX §§ 302 and 906 are recognisable Park-shaped responses to a different statutory domain. The Senior Managers and Certification Regime in the United Kingdom is recognisable as Park's most extended form, expanded into a written-allocation regime for every senior function in a regulated firm. Each was built knowing the prior move existed. The design tradition has half a century of operating record.

## Two signatures, every quarter

The 2002 record-discipline design is the United States' clearest post-*Park* extension to a domain — financial reporting — that *Park* itself did not reach. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 was the legislative response to the Enron failure of late 2001 and the WorldCom failure of mid-2002. Among its many provisions, two carry the design-rule argument. They are both responsibility-following-control design moves, and they are both currently in force.

The pattern, in one sentence: two named officers must personally sign each periodic financial filing, and a knowingly false signature carries prison time. The statute does the rest.

Section 302, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 7241, instructs the Securities and Exchange Commission to require, by rule, that the chief executive officer and the chief financial officer of each issuer personally certify each periodic report. The certifying officers must affirm that they have reviewed the report. They must affirm that, based on their knowledge, it does not contain material misstatements or omissions. They must affirm that, based on their knowledge, the financial statements fairly present the issuer's financial condition. They must affirm that they have designed, evaluated, and disclosed the effectiveness of the issuer's internal controls over financial reporting[<sup>15</sup>](#c11-15). The SEC's implementing rules — principally Rules 13a-14 and 15d-14 under the Securities Exchange Act — operationalise the certification in the form attached to every Form 10-K and Form 10-Q.

Section 906, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1350, attaches criminal penalty to knowing or wilful false certification: a knowing violation is punishable by fine and up to ten years' imprisonment; a wilful violation, up to twenty years[<sup>16</sup>](#c11-16). The named individual is the certifying officer. The named act is signing. The named state of mind is knowing or wilful falsity.

The criminal-enforcement record under § 906 is small. We do not claim § 906 has produced a wave of CEO prosecutions. We claim something narrower, and the statutory text alone carries the claim. SOX §§ 302 and 906 named the individual required to attest, attached the attestation to a periodic public filing with criminal exposure, and removed the "we delegated to the auditor" alibi from the cleanest layer of corporate reporting.

The narrowness matters because the alternative was not zero accountability. The alternative was diffuse accountability — to the audit committee, to the external auditor, to the corporation in the abstract. Each of those parties existed before SOX. None could be put in the same room as a single named signature on a periodic filing. The certification did not produce a new offence in the criminal-procedure sense; it produced a new *certifying individual* at the seam where the prior diffusion had obscured every individual.

The contrast with the corporate-prosecution design installed in ch-5 sits inside the same country and the same period. The Boeing case under ch-5's anchor was prosecuted by the Department of Justice as a corporate-respondent matter. The 2021 DPA Statement of Facts named the Technical Pilots; the corporate plea did not name an indicted officer above them. The Sarbanes-Oxley regime sits in the same year-range as the design choices that produced the DPA framework, and operates in the same federal architecture. Two designs; same country; opposite specification at the named-officer layer. SOX names the CEO and the CFO and attaches criminal exposure to their signature. The DPA framework names the corporation and attaches financial penalty to its plea. The named individual sits inside one of these designs and does not sit inside the other. The diagnostic predicts where laundering can still operate.

The Park doctrine and the Sarbanes-Oxley §§ 302 and 906 regime together demonstrate that responsibility-following-control design has more than fifty years of operating record at the corporate-officer layer in U.S. statute and federal-court practice. The design is not novel. The design exists. The question we are asking is whether the design will be extended to the layers it has not yet been extended to — and whether the layers at which it operates today are the layers at which the laundering, having displaced, now occurs.

## The defensive-compliance objection

The strongest counter to the design-rule argument is not partisan. It does not come from the left or the right. It comes from a body of corporate-law and economic scholarship that has, for a generation, argued that responsibility-following-control statutes of the Park-SOX-SMCR kind produce defensive compliance, not actual accountability.

The argument runs roughly as follows. Imposing personal certification or named-function allocation on senior officers, beyond record-documented knowing misconduct, exposes those officers to liability that they cannot, as a practical matter, reach down through. A CEO of a Fortune 500 company cannot personally inspect every operational layer for which the company's CFO will sign a § 302 certification. A senior manager at a multinational bank cannot, by the architecture of the firm, personally verify every transaction her Statement of Responsibilities makes her the named owner of. The named-attribution design, on this account, therefore does one of three things. It chills the recruitment of senior officers willing to bear the exposure. It generates a permanent compliance overhead — outside counsel reviews, internal certification chains, audit committee theatre — that captures resources without producing accountability. Or it produces a checklist culture in which the auditor signs because the box exists, not because the auditor has verified the underlying conduct. The result is the appearance of responsibility, distributed across senior officers whose practical control of the certified activity is itself a fiction. The argument was advanced in different forms by Henry Manne in commentary on Park, by Frank Easterbrook and Daniel Fischel on responsible-officer liability and the structure of the firm, and by Gary Becker in the broader economic analysis of criminal-deterrence design.

The strong form of this counter adds an empirical observation we must engage. Across the cases we have installed, the most prominent prosecutions of senior individuals — Volkswagen's Oliver Schmidt and James Liang in the United States, Rupert Stadler in Germany — were reached under existing fraud and conspiracy statutes, not under Park-style or SOX-style certification regimes. The Volkswagen executives, on the counter's account, were prosecuted with the criminal-law instruments the U.S. and German systems already possessed; no new responsibility-following-control statute was required. The OLC opinion process that produced the 1 August 2002 Bybee memo was, separately, internal to the executive branch and would not have been altered by a SOX-equivalent attestation regime because internal executive-branch legal advice is not periodic public filing. The 2003-04 Robb-Silberman charter scope was set by executive order, not by inquiry statute; if the political will to set a broader charter had existed, it could have been set without amending statute. The counter therefore concludes: the design rule we argue for would have changed nothing in the cases where it should have worked, and would have produced compliance overhead in the cases where the laundering never approached prosecution.

This is a real argument. It has scholarly weight. It is our dominant steelman.

The response is design-specific and does not contest the counter at the level the counter operates on. It has three parts.

First, the four design moves walked above are not strict-liability moves. Park's standard is opportunity-to-prevent, conditioned on the officer's position and notice. SOX § 906 is conditioned on knowing or wilful falsity. SMCR's named-officer regulatory duty requires the manager to take such steps as a person in the position could reasonably be expected to take. The Inquiries Act § 21 compels evidence; it does not by itself impose individual liability for the conduct revealed. Each design names the conduct that triggers exposure. None operates as universal personal liability for every harm.

Second, the design rule is not "punish the certifier for every operational failure." It is name the certifier as the decision-maker who must choose which layer to inspect, who must record the sub-delegation, and who bears the consequence of failing to inspect the layer at which the harm occurred. Park itself runs on this distinction. The doctrine is recognisable in the slip-opinion text as a structural one: the responsible relation is the relation in which the officer had the authority to designate which subordinate would do the inspecting and to verify that the inspecting had been done. Design happens at the named-attribution layer, not at the strict-liability layer.

Third, the four design moves would not have prevented the cases this book has installed. The narrower and more honest point is that the design moves change the cell of the diagnostic matrix where the laundering must occur. The Volkswagen prosecutions reached the engineering layer; they did not reach the executive layer in the United States, and reached it only partly in Germany. The Bybee memo's authorship survived the OLC's internal withdrawal mechanism because no statutory attestation operated at that layer. The Robb-Silberman charter survived because no statute compelled a broader inquiry charter. Whether the laundering displaced, and to which cell it displaced, is the question the matrix above frames. The libertarian counter is partly correct on whether named-attribution design eliminates laundering. It does not. The question is not regulation vs. no regulation. The question is laundering vs. no laundering. Where the named-attribution layer is filled, the laundering must displace; where the layer is unfilled by design, the laundering does not need to displace at all.

## Same country, opposite specification

The defensive-compliance counter is most testable through asymmetry. Four design moves where responsibility-following-control was embedded; four moves where responsibility was fenced. Side by side, one diagnostic question at a time.

It is read horizontally — the design move the laundered system makes, then the design move the responsibility-following-control system makes — at each of four layers we have already named.

#### At the corporate-officer layer.

The DPA-without-individual-indictment move negotiates a corporate plea, names a financial penalty, attaches a compliance monitor, and closes the prosecution without filing an indictment against an executive officer above the operational layer at which the cooperation was reached. The opposite move is the Park doctrine. The FDCA names the responsible officer at the operating layer of the FDA's enforcement domain. The misdemeanor information runs against the named individual whose position carried the authority and the opportunity to prevent the violation. Same general statutory family — both federal regulatory regimes operating against corporations — and opposite specification at the named-officer layer. The DPA closes the chain at the corporate plea. Park names the individual at the seam where the corporate plea would otherwise close.

#### At the financial-reporting layer.

The pre-2002 diffuse-attestation move treated the periodic filing as the product of the company's accounting and audit functions, signed off by the audit committee, certified in form by the external auditor, defended in the abstract by the corporation as the certifying party. The opposite move is SOX § 302. The certification is personal. The signature is by name. The criminal exposure under § 906 attaches to the named officer for knowing or wilful falsity. The corporation continues to exist as a party to the filing. The corporation does not displace the individual. The same form continues to be filed; the named individuals on its certification page are the difference.

#### At the regulated-financial-firm layer.

The pre-SMCR allocation of responsibility within a UK bank or insurer ran through committees, internal control functions, board sub-committees, and the corporate-form abstraction. When the post-2008 enforcement environment asked who had been responsible for what had failed, the answer routinely returned a body, not a person. The opposite move is SMCR. Each senior management function is allocated, by Statement of Responsibilities, to a named individual. The individual bears a regulatory duty to take such steps as the position permits to avoid contraventions in their area. The FCA's enforcement record under the regime — Final Notices issued against named senior managers in retail banks for failures in their allocated function — is the operating record. The corporate form has not vanished; the named function-holder is now visible inside it.

#### At the inquiry-statute layer.

The Robb-Silberman move scopes the inquiry's charter, by executive order, to a sub-component of the question the inquiry purports to examine. The Commission's Section 2 mandate ran to the intelligence community's capacity to collect, analyse, and disseminate; the order named no authority to examine how policymakers used the resulting product. The scope was set by what the order granted, not by what it excluded. The opposite move is the Inquiries Act 2005 § 21. The inquiry's chair can compel the production of documents and the attendance of witnesses, with criminal penalty for non-compliance. The Iraq Inquiry — Chilcot — used the power to obtain Cabinet papers, JIC assessments, and the Blair–Bush correspondence, including the 28 July 2002 minute in which Blair wrote "I will be with you, whatever"[<sup>17</sup>](#c11-17). Chapter 10 installed the contrast at the wartime case level. We read the contrast there as the difference between a charter that fenced the alibi and a charter that did not. Read at the design-rule level, the contrast is between an executive-order instrument whose scope is set by the executive being examined and a statutory instrument whose compulsion power is set by Parliament under §21, even where the terms of reference are set by the establishing Minister under §5. Both are executive scope-setting; only the UK regime hard-wires the §21 evidence-compulsion power regardless of the Minister's terms.

Four rows. Each holds the same diagnostic question against the laundered design and the responsibility-following-control design. The right column does not eliminate laundering. The right column changes the cell of the matrix in which the laundering must occur. The Boeing case displaced into the layer above the Technical Pilots. SOX displaced fraud into structures the certification did not reach. SMCR displaced misconduct into the layers below senior-manager allocation. The Iraq Inquiry's compulsion power produced findings the Robb-Silberman charter could not produce, and produced no prosecutions of senior officials it documented. Displacement is the prediction, not the rebuttal. The right-column moves were not built to eliminate laundering. They were built to name the cells in which the laundering could continue to occur. Chapter 3 named the questions as a forensic instrument, run backward from a harm; the same eight questions, run forward, are these design specifications. The questions did not change. The direction did.

## Name the person before the design ships

Return to the three documents we opened on. None contained an unlawful sentence; each left a hole where a named officer should have borne the consequence of the control question failing. The design tradition just walked is what fills those holes. The Boeing Deferred Prosecution Agreement closed at the Technical Pilot layer; a Park-shaped specification would have named, on the face of the agreement, the officer whose position carried the authority to prevent the certification failure above that layer. The Bybee–Yoo opinion was authored, signed, and withdrawn with no attestation requirement operating over its drafting; a SOX-shaped specification would have attached a named, prosecutable signature to the layer at which the opinion's legal conclusion was certified as sound. Executive Order 13328 scoped Robb-Silberman away from policymakers' use of the product; an Inquiries-Act-shaped specification would have hard-wired the chair's compulsion power regardless of the order's terms. The holes were not unfillable. They were unfilled by design.

The rule that follows from the matrix and the asymmetry table is portable across statute, charter, contract, and policy document. We state it in protocol form.

> When you propose, sign, or advocate for an institutional design — a statute, a corporate plea structure, an inquiry charter, an oversight body's terms of reference, a contract clause, an internal policy, an AI deployment authorisation — ask whether a named person bears the consequence of the control question failing at each layer the design touches. If no named person bears it, the design is laundering by construction. Add the name to the design before the design is built.

The rule is a test, not a sentiment. It is applied document by document and clause by clause. It is testable against any of the four cells the matrix above identifies: who is named as control-holder, who attests to knowledge, who exercises the prevention authority, who bears the consequence of the layer failing. The rule does not require new statute. The rule requires that the institutional design name the responsibility-following-control specification on the face of its operative text — the way Park names the responsible officer, the way SOX names the CEO and the CFO, the way SMCR names the function-holder, the way the Inquiries Act names the inquiry's chair as the compelling officer.

Our role under the rule is design participant. We sign contracts, vote on referenda, serve on committees, draft charters, allocate roles inside organisations we run or work in. Each of those acts is a design moment. The rule applies at each.

This is our one use of the term *responsibility laundering* here. Our argument is that the term names a recurring institutional method by which power keeps control while moving blame onto a weaker bearer, a procedural shell, or a system without a name. The design rule above is our portable counter-method. It is not the only counter-method we install. It is the first.

## Four designs in operation

Four institutional designs in operation. Not hypothetical. Each names the moment of individual accountability the design has produced, the year, the named accountability mechanism's text, and the cost of the escape — the constraint the design places on the powerful actor it binds.

The four, in one line each: **Park** (US, FDCA, 1975 — the responsible officer); **SOX §§ 302/906** (US, 2002 — the CEO and CFO personally certifying, under criminal exposure); **UK SMCR** (2016 — a written Statement of Responsibilities allocating a senior management function to a named individual); and a **section 21 inquiry** under the UK Inquiries Act 2005 (compulsory-evidence power, an inquiry chair compelling the record). Take each in turn.

#### 1. Park doctrine.

*United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658 (1975), affirmed by a 6–3 Supreme Court[<sup>18</sup>](#c11-18), holds that an officer in a position of "responsibility and authority" to prevent or correct a Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act violation bears criminal liability under the Act, regardless of personal knowledge — at 421 U.S. 671 to 674. The FDA has continued to use the doctrine across the half-century since *Park*. *United States v. DeCoster*, 828 F.3d 626 (8th Cir. 2016)[<sup>19</sup>](#c11-19), tested it at the senior-officer layer: the Eighth Circuit affirmed three-month prison sentences against father-and-son Quality Egg executives Austin "Jack" DeCoster and Peter DeCoster; the Supreme Court denied certiorari the following year. The constraint is what the named executive cannot do — route operational compliance entirely through subordinates and remain criminally untouchable when FDA notice has reached the executive's position.

#### 2. SOX §§ 302 and 906.

Sarbanes-Oxley, enacted 30 July 2002[<sup>20</sup>](#c11-20), requires the CEO and CFO personally to certify the issuer's internal controls (§ 302), under criminal exposure for knowing falsity (§ 906)[<sup>21</sup>](#c11-21). Every Form 10-K and 10-Q filed by every SEC-reporting issuer since 14 August 2002 bears two named individual signatures with that exposure attached. The criminal-enforcement record under § 906 is modest in volume; the structural effect is broader than the prosecutorial record alone. The argument is that this is, in substantial part, what the named-signature requirement was for — not to produce prosecutions, but to produce signatures that could be prosecuted, attached to the layer at which the prior diffusion had obscured every individual.

#### 3. UK SMCR.

The Senior Managers and Certification Regime, installed under FSMA 2000 ss. 59ZA, 59A, and 59B as inserted by the Banking Reform Act 2013, with FCA Handbook SYSC 4.7, 24, and 27 carrying the operative provisions[<sup>22</sup>](#c11-22). It came into force for banks, building societies, and PRA-designated investment firms in 2016 and was extended in 2018–19. The named mechanism is the Statement of Responsibilities — a written allocation of a senior management function to a named individual — together with that manager's regulatory duty to take such steps as a person in that position could reasonably be expected to take to avoid contraventions in the allocated area. The FCA's Final Notices register records enforcement against named senior managers for failures within their function. The regime has not eliminated misconduct in UK financial services. The constraint: under the regime, the named manager cannot point to a committee.

#### 4. Inquiries Act 2005 § 21, contrasted with Executive Order 13328.

The Inquiries Act came into force on 7 June 2005[<sup>23</sup>](#c11-23); section 21's compulsory-evidence power is the regime's load-bearing tool. The Iraq Inquiry, chaired by Sir John Chilcot, used the section to obtain Cabinet papers, JIC assessments, and the Blair–Bush correspondence; the report of 6 July 2016 found that the September 2002 dossier had presented JIC assessments with "a degree of certainty that was not justified" and that the legal basis for war had been "far from satisfactory"[<sup>24</sup>](#c11-24). The contrasting design is Executive Order 13328 of 6 February 2004, which chartered the Robb-Silberman Commission to examine the intelligence community's product on Iraq WMD but, by its own terms, scoped the Commission to intelligence-community capacity and named no authority to examine policymaker use of the intelligence[<sup>25</sup>](#c11-25). Two designs, two countries, parallel intelligence failure on the same case: the compelling design produced the Cabinet-paper record; the fencing design produced an intelligence-community audit whose conclusions did not extend to the policy use. The constraint under § 21 is the political distance the design imposes on the executive of the day — the chair sets the evidentiary scope, and the executive accepts the compulsion or accepts the public record of its refusal.

Each panel is honest about what the design produced and what the design did not produce. None of the four designs eliminated the laundering this book has installed. Each named the cell of the diagnostic matrix in which the laundering, having displaced, would now be visible. That is the design's named work. The design is not a remedy. The design is the named-attribution specification on which subsequent remedy depends.

The matrix on which we have been operating leaves Q3 — who benefited — across all four moves under-mapped. That cell is what comes next. The design rule names the responsible party. The record discipline is what makes the rule operate.

The design rule reaches our own reading and working life wherever a written document — a board charter, a contract clause, an inquiry's terms of reference, an internal policy — names a function but no person. The recognition trigger is exactly that gap: the scope clause that says "the Committee shall review the firm's internal-control system" with no individual whose written allocation makes the review theirs; the indemnification clause that protects "the Corporation" without naming the officer whose decision triggers it; the "designated person" left as a placeholder. Each is a Park-shaped hole, and the question that surfaces it is one question: at each layer this design touches, who is the named individual whose written allocation makes them the certifying officer for the control question that layer is responsible for? If the answer is a function without a person, a person without a function, or a committee without an allocation, the design absence is the seam. The signature is the seam. Watch the seam.

We are most likely to be positioned as the goat at the layer where our role intersects the design absence. Chapter 6 named the signature as the seam in administrative-law pretext cases. Chapter 11 adds the design-side correlate: do not sign the named-signer line of a function whose written allocation you did not write, whose sub-delegations you were not given, and whose escalation paths route back to the layer that produced the prior failure. Where the written allocation does not exist, ask for it in writing before signing. The written request is itself the design move. The absence of the allocation is the disqualifying condition.

Next we take up the record discipline that makes the design rule operate at the cells of the matrix we have left unmapped.
