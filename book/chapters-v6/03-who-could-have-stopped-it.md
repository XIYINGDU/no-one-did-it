# Chapter 3 — Who Could Have Stopped It?

> he who does not prevent or oppose wrong, if he can, is just as guilty of wrong as if he deserted his parents or his friends or his country.
>
> — Cicero, *De Officiis* I.23, trans. Walter Miller (Loeb Classical Library, 1913)

In May 2014, a Volkswagen Jetta TDI and a Volkswagen Passat TDI were driven along the western highways of the United States with portable emissions equipment bolted to their tailpipes. A BMW X5 diesel was driven the same routes for comparison. The three cars were rented and instrumented by engineers from the Center for Alternative Fuels, Engines and Emissions — CAFEE — at West Virginia University, under a contract from a small Washington nonprofit called the International Council on Clean Transportation.

The work was uncontroversial in design. CAFEE had built a reputation in heavy-duty diesel testing. The portable system, called PEMS, measured what came out of the exhaust pipe while the car was moving. The hypothesis driving the project was friendly to the manufacturers. The ICCT had set out to document that modern US light-duty diesels were achieving in-use emissions performance their European counterparts could be expected to match.

The results came back the other way. A measurement commissioned to vindicate the manufacturers became the instrument that exposed them; the favour the industry had been expecting was the one no one asked for. On the road, the Jetta emitted oxides of nitrogen at fifteen to thirty-five times the federal limit. The Passat ran roughly five to twenty times the limit. The BMW X5 stayed within the standard. The CAFEE report — Thompson, Carder, Besch, Thiruvengadam, and Kappanna, "In-Use Emissions Testing of Light-Duty Diesel Vehicles in the United States," published May 15, 2014[<sup>1</sup>](#c3-1) — laid the numbers out and left the interpretation to others.

Then a year and a half went by. Through the spring and summer of 2014, into 2015, Volkswagen explained the discrepancy to the California Air Resources Board and to the United States Environmental Protection Agency as a technical problem — calibration issues, after-treatment subtleties, things that would be addressed by a software update. In December 2014, Volkswagen issued a voluntary recall, internally tagged service action 23U1. CARB re-tested. The road-versus-lab gap remained. CARB warned, in the summer of 2015, that without an adequate explanation it would refuse to certify the company's 2016 model-year diesels for sale in California.

On September 3, 2015, in a meeting room at CARB's El Monte facility, Volkswagen confessed. The cars contained a piece of software that recognized when they were being tested and altered the engine's behavior accordingly. On September 18, 2015, the EPA issued a Notice of Violation under the Clean Air Act[<sup>2</sup>](#c3-2). Within days, the company's chief executive resigned. Within sixteen months, the corporation had pleaded guilty in federal court in Detroit to a felony conspiracy to defraud the United States[<sup>3</sup>](#c3-3).

The lie is not what concerns us here. The lie is well-documented. The gap is what concerns us — the sixteen months between the publication of the CAFEE numbers and the day Volkswagen admitted what those numbers meant. What the company said inside that gap, who said it, who shaped what was said next when the prior version failed, and which actors paid the cost while the explanation cycled through its versions.

Above all, a question and the eight smaller questions that compose it: *Who could have stopped it?*

## Three stories, in sequence

Volkswagen did not arrive at "we cheated" in one move. It arrived there in three.

The first official story ran from May 2014 through September 2015. The discrepancy was a *technical issue*. After-treatment chemistry, perhaps. Calibration drift. The company would investigate, file an explanation with CARB, and address what could be addressed through a software recall. The December 2014 voluntary action was the operational form of this story — a recall framed as ordinary remediation. The story bought sixteen months. During that period the company continued selling 2015-model-year affected vehicles in the United States and Europe.

The second official story ran from September 2015 into 2016. A *small group of engineers had acted alone*. The framing entered Volkswagen's public statements in the week after the EPA's Notice of Violation, sat behind the early statements of the outgoing chief executive, and shaped the design of the corporation's internal investigation. The company retained the US law firm Jones Day in September 2015 to investigate. The narrative had operational weight. When in September 2016 the US Department of Justice obtained a guilty plea from a Volkswagen engineer named James Liang[<sup>4</sup>](#c3-4), and again when in January 2017 federal agents arrested a compliance manager named Oliver Schmidt at Miami International Airport[<sup>5</sup>](#c3-5), the criminal docket moved against engineers and a mid-level executive before it moved against the company's top leadership. The "rogue engineers" story acquired faces.

The third official story is harder to date because it never had a public unveiling. It is what remained after the first two stories failed: *the defeat-device software is the cause*. In the consumer-settlement era of 2016 and 2017, in trade-press analyses, in the public-facing language of the corporation, the software became the agent. The Volkswagen Jetta did not cheat; *the device* in the Jetta cheated. The "clean diesel" advertising campaign had not deceived consumers; *the device* had. The chief executive had not been part of any fraud; *the device* had been built by people whose connection to him had to be proved separately, case by case.

Three stories, in sequence. Each was pierced before the next entered the public account. Whether the sequencing was strategic choreography or a series of successive retreats under pressure, the operational effect was the same: each story ceded as little ground as the prior story's collapse forced, and each protected what the prior story had not yet been required to surrender. The diagnostic does not depend on choreography; it depends on the sequence being detectable in the record.

Paying attention to the sequence teaches us something paying attention to any single story cannot. The sequence is the laundering. The diagnostic now to be introduced is the instrument that detects the sequence.

## Eight questions, in order

A question has been circling. We now ask it, and then ask the seven smaller questions that compose it.

> When something has gone wrong and someone is being blamed, ask these eight questions. Ask them in this order. Ask them all.
>
> 1. **Who or what was publicly blamed?** Not who was finally adjudicated. Who is the named container — the engineer, the algorithm, the contractor, the procedure, the deceased commander, the broken machine.
> 2. **Who had control?** Who held the design authority, the operational authority, the approval signature, the budget line. Control may sit several layers above the named container.
> 3. **Who benefited?** Whose unit sales, whose stock price, whose quarterly bonus, whose career trajectory, whose strategic positioning. Benefit is the cheapest evidence to find and the fastest to confirm.
> 4. **Who knew or should have known?** What was documented in pre-production testing, internal memoranda, supplier warnings, audit reports, incident logs. Knowledge is what the record shows — not what someone could plausibly have inferred.
> 5. **Who could have prevented recurrence?** At what point did the chain still have time to take a different decision? Who held authority at that point? The preventability question separates the unlucky from the responsible.
> 6. **Who controlled the record?** Which entity held the documents, decided what was discoverable, set the protective orders, ran the internal investigation, hired the law firm whose report was never released. Record control is upstream of every other answer.
> 7. **Who bore the cost?** Death, injury, displacement, prosecution, reputational ruin, economic loss, surveillance burden. Cost bearing is rarely the same population as control or benefit; when it is, no laundering occurred. When it isn't, the gap names the laundering.
> 8. **What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility?** This is the meta-question. Re-run the case with control as the ordering principle. Where does the chain now stop? That is the chapter's answer to who could have stopped it.

The questions form a diagnostic. They do not by themselves convict anyone. They expose the gap between named blame and held responsibility.

Run them on Volkswagen.

Volkswagen Dieselgate is what this book calls a hybrid case. The taxonomy installed in the previous chapter named four shapes: pure scapegoat, partial scapegoat, system/object alibi, and cost-bearing goat. Volkswagen is, in its primary register, a system/object alibi — the defeat-device software and the federal test procedure became the reified causes that absorbed the human decision chain. It is, in its secondary register, a partial scapegoat — engineers and one compliance manager bore criminal consequences while the executives and the supervisory board faced markedly weaker accountability under US procedure. And it carries a deliberate-fraud overlay, because individual actors knowingly designed the deception. The diagnostic is not embarrassed by the hybrid. The diagnostic is built for the hybrid. The eight questions pierce the hybrid into its layers and show where each layer routed responsibility.

*Question one: who or what was publicly blamed?* In sequence, four containers: the after-treatment system; a small group of engineers; James Liang and Oliver Schmidt by name; and finally the defeat-device software itself, as a thing apart from the people who built it.

*Question two: who had control?* Volkswagen AG in Wolfsburg held the engineering authority for the EA189 diesel family and for the V6 TDI platforms that carried the device into Audi and Porsche vehicles. The Statement of Facts filed with Volkswagen's January 11, 2017 plea agreement in the Eastern District of Michigan[<sup>6</sup>](#c3-6) identifies named Volkswagen engineering managers as the approvers of the original defeat-device strategy in 2006, when Volkswagen concluded it could not meet the US Tier 2 Bin 5 nitrogen oxides limit with the hardware architecture it had budgeted. Robert Bosch GmbH wrote the underlying engine-control software framework. Volkswagen Group of America in Herndon, Virginia held US-market compliance authority and filed the certificates of conformity with the EPA — the documents that affirmed each vehicle's compliance with the Clean Air Act.

*Question three: who benefited?* Volkswagen AG, through the US-market diesel sales the company could not have achieved at compliant emissions levels with the cost-acceptable hardware it had selected. Audi AG and Porsche AG, through the V6 TDI program. Executives at multiple layers, through compensation tied to US-market growth. The "clean diesel" advertising campaign, which positioned Volkswagen diesels as the environmentally responsible alternative to gasoline and hybrid vehicles, was a strategic market position for the company in the United States across roughly 2008 to 2015. The defeat device made it possible.

*Question four: who knew or should have known?* The record is documented across more than a decade.

In 2006, the defeat-device strategy was adopted as the engineering response to a hardware-and-budget constraint, per the Statement of Facts[<sup>7</sup>](#c3-7). In 2007 and 2008, Robert Bosch warned Volkswagen in writing that the requested software behavior could be an illegal defeat device under US law, according to DOJ filings and Bosch's own US settlement. The supplier sought, and per the DOJ Bosch settlement received, a letter of indemnification from Volkswagen[<sup>8</sup>](#c3-8). The supplier flagged the legal risk; Volkswagen continued.

In May 2014 the CAFEE report surfaced the road-versus-lab gap[<sup>9</sup>](#c3-9). From May 2014 through September 2015, internal discussions across Volkswagen engineering and management addressed possible explanations. German management-board and supervisory-board briefings occurred during this period; their precise content is among the matters being adjudicated in Germany. On September 3, 2015, Volkswagen confessed to CARB and EPA staff.

The sixteen-month gap between independent detection and confession is the cleanest evidence of Volkswagen's documented internal knowledge — the timing of that knowledge, and what was said in the interim.

*Question five: who could have prevented recurrence?* At least five points in the chain still held that option. In 2006, the engineering decision could have been made differently. In 2007 and 2008, the Bosch warning could have been heeded. At any model-year refresh between 2009 and 2015, the after-treatment architecture could have been redesigned. The CAFEE testing showed the BMW X5 meeting the same limit on the same roads — compliant hardware existed. In May 2014, Volkswagen could have answered CARB and the EPA truthfully. And across that decade, internal compliance controls capable of detecting an eleven-million-vehicle fraud could have been installed. The Jones Day investigation, commissioned by Volkswagen in September 2015, was a retrospective acknowledgment of the absence of those controls.

*Question six: who controlled the record?* The internal engineering and management archives in Wolfsburg remained, for years, under corporate control. Volkswagen commissioned its own outside investigation by Jones Day. The full Jones Day report has not been publicly released; redacted portions and witness accounts have entered the US and German court records under judicial protective order. The corporation has resisted further disclosure. Against that record-control move, the cleaner counter-record is the United States criminal docket. The Statement of Facts attached to the Volkswagen AG plea[<sup>10</sup>](#c3-10), the indictments of Liang and Schmidt[<sup>11</sup>](#c3-11), and the May 2018 indictment of additional executives[<sup>12</sup>](#c3-12) are public primary documents. The SEC's March 14, 2019 civil complaint against Volkswagen AG and the company's then-former chief executive entered another public record[<sup>13</sup>](#c3-13). Where the corporation controlled the internal-investigation record, the US criminal and civil systems controlled the public one.

*Question seven: who bore the cost?* Three populations. First and most quietly, the people who breathed the excess nitrogen oxides emitted across the affected vehicles' on-road operation from model year 2009 through 2015. Peer-reviewed estimates published in *Environmental Research Letters* by Barrett, Speth, and colleagues attribute on the order of sixty premature US deaths to that fleet's excess emissions, with several hundred additional cases of chronic bronchitis and respiratory hospitalization[<sup>14</sup>](#c3-14); methodologies vary, and we carry the figure as a peer-reviewed estimate rather than a settled count. Second, approximately 600,000 US consumers whose affected vehicles received buyback or fix offers under the $14.7 billion 2016 settlement — these cost bearers were compensated. Third, Volkswagen workers and dealers, who bore reputational and business cost; Wolfsburg-area production schedules were disrupted; some dealers reported sales declines through 2017.

Question eight: what would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility? Responsibility would attach to the engineering managers who authorized the 2006 defeat-device strategy. To the executives who were briefed across 2014 and 2015 and chose the "technical issues" response. To the supervisory-board members who received those briefings and authorized multi-year continuation. To the Bosch personnel who drafted the 2007–2008 warning memoranda and continued the contract. To the Volkswagen Group of America compliance staff who filed false certificates of conformity. It would attach less heavily to James Liang, the engineer who pleaded guilty in September 2016 and served forty months[<sup>15</sup>](#c3-15). Liang is in the chain — he wrote code that did what it did — but the chain runs upward through the people who told him what the engine had to do and accepted what they were told it did.

The diagnostic does not declare anyone guilty. It declares where the chain runs, and where the public attribution stops short of where the chain runs.

> *The chain map as a table; the eight-question walk above is its audio rendering, and listeners may skip the table.*

The story blamed individual engineers and "the software" — Volkswagen first described "a few" engineers, and James Liang and Oliver Schmidt were convicted in the United States. The eight questions point upward.

## A subcompact, an eleven-dollar fix

Now run the same eight questions on a different car company, in a different decade, under a different regulatory architecture. If the diagnostic is portable, the questions answer just as cleanly when the technology is sheet metal instead of software and the year is 1973 instead of 2014.

The Ford Pinto entered production for the 1971 model year. It was a subcompact developed on a compressed schedule and a tight cost target — the program is widely reported in trial testimony and contemporaneous trade reporting to have been developed in approximately twenty-five months, against an industry norm of forty-three. The fuel tank sat aft of the rear axle and forward of the rear bumper, in a crush zone that, on rear impact at moderate closing speeds, could be penetrated by exposed bolt heads on the differential housing or by the rear bumper's deformation into the tank itself. Rear-impact crash tests inside Ford in approximately 1970 and 1971 documented the rupture. The California Court of Appeal's published opinion in *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 757, 174 Cal.Rptr. 348, at pages 777–778, recites that "crash tests revealed that the Pinto's fuel system as designed could not meet the 20-mile-per-hour proposed standard," in the testimony of a former Ford engineer named Harley Copp[<sup>16</sup>](#c3-16).

Circa 1973, Ford produced an internal memorandum titled "Fatalities Associated with Crash-Induced Fuel Leakage and Fires." The document monetized projected fatalities, projected serious burn injuries, and projected vehicle burn losses against the per-vehicle cost of a fuel-system fix. A precision must travel with this memorandum from the moment it is named, because the popular reading and the peer-reviewed reading diverge: the document is not, as the popular shorthand has it, a secret Pinto-only calculation of whether to fix a deadly car; the law-review re-examination by Gary T. Schwartz documents that its fatality figures were drawn from regulatory cost-benefit numbers prepared in the context of fleet-wide fuel-system rulemaking[<sup>17</sup>](#c3-17). What the document is, on both readings, is a cost-benefit-against-fatalities calculation that Ford had in its files — Ford's own arithmetic, in a regulatory frame, not ours. Carry that distinction through what follows; the figures below are reported as Ford's regulatory-context monetization, not as a standalone fix-or-not memo. Run the diagnostic.

*Who was publicly blamed?* In sequence, the striking driver — the operator of the rear-impacting vehicle, framed by Ford's litigation posture across the 1970s as the proximate cause of harm — and then the vehicle itself. "The exploding Pinto" became the named cause in popular language, a reified product that absorbed the design and approval choices into a defect framing. Eventually Ford the corporation became the third container, absorbing the civil judgment in *Grimshaw* and the federal recall.

Who had control? Ford Motor Company held design authority for the Pinto. The fuel-tank placement was a Ford engineering and packaging decision, driven by program styling, trunk-space, and weight targets. Lee Iacocca, as Ford president from 1970 through 1978, held ultimate program-oversight authority during the Pinto's design and during the period when the cost-benefit memorandum was prepared and circulated. The trial record names additional Ford engineers and program managers in the design-approval chain; we do not put names on the cost-benefit decision that the *Grimshaw* trial record does not put there. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration held delegated regulatory authority but had Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 301 — the fuel-system-integrity standard — in rulemaking through the early 1970s; the 30-mile-per-hour rear-impact provision was finalized in 1976 with phased compliance through model year 1977.

Who benefited? Ford Motor Company, through Pinto unit sales across approximately three million vehicles produced during the program. The cost-benefit memorandum's eleven-dollar-per-vehicle fix figure, applied across the production volume, is the unit-economics figure behind its widely cited $137 million side totals. Ford's revenue from the affected vehicles ran several multiples of the avoided fix cost. Executives whose compensation tracked program performance benefited. After Iacocca left Ford in July 1978, he joined Chrysler in November 1978 and became one of the most prominent US automotive executives of the subsequent decade.

Who knew or should have known? Ford-internal crash testing in approximately 1970 and 1971 had documented the fuel-tank rupture, per Copp's testimony at *Grimshaw* and the appellate opinion[<sup>18</sup>](#c3-18). The 1973 memorandum gave the calculation a documentary form. Internal awareness of the vulnerability across multiple model-year refreshes from 1971 through 1976 is established by trial testimony. The eleven-dollar fix was technically modest; Ford engineers had identified it.

The two readings flagged at the memorandum's first mention have named sources worth setting on the record. The popular reading was foregrounded by Mark Dowie's September/October 1977 *Mother Jones* piece "Pinto Madness"[<sup>19</sup>](#c3-19) and developed in the academic monograph *Corporate Crime Under Attack* by Cullen, Maakestad, and Cavender (1987)[<sup>20</sup>](#c3-20); it treats the memorandum as Ford's secret Pinto-only calculation of whether to fix a deadly product. The peer-reviewed re-examination by Gary T. Schwartz, "The Myth of the Ford Pinto Case," 43 *Rutgers L. Rev.* 1013 (1991)[<sup>21</sup>](#c3-21), argues that the memorandum's fatality figures were drawn from NHTSA-influenced regulatory cost-benefit numbers prepared in the context of fleet-wide rollover and fuel-system rulemaking, not a Pinto-only secret. Both readings are partly correct. The document *is* a cost-benefit-against-fatalities calculation that Ford had in its files. The document was *not* simply a fix-or-not memo for one car. We record both readings and take neither as decoration.

Who could have prevented recurrence? At Pinto design freeze in approximately 1969 and 1970, Ford could have specified a fuel-tank location forward of the rear axle. At pre-production testing in 1970 and 1971, Ford could have redesigned the fuel-system protection in response to the rupture results. Across each model-year refresh from 1972 through 1976, the eleven-dollar fix could have been incorporated. In May 1978, after NHTSA's preliminary determination of defect, Ford could have announced the recall promptly rather than negotiating into June.

Who controlled the record? Ford's internal engineering and management archives in Dearborn held the crash-test records, the design-review minutes, and the cost-benefit memorandum. Those documents entered the public record — and were locked there — through *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* discovery in 1978, the trial verdict on February 6, 1978, and the California Court of Appeal's published opinion of May 29, 1981[<sup>22</sup>](#c3-22). The trial record at Orange County Superior Court, Case No. 197761, before Judge Leonard Ginsburg, is the venue at which Ford lost record control over its own design history. The California Supreme Court denied review on September 16, 1981. The published appellate opinion is the load-bearing record-control document. Where Volkswagen would later commission Jones Day to investigate and then keep the report, Ford's record passed into the courts.

Who bore the cost? Two populations, with the harm range itself a contested figure. The closing materials of NHTSA Recall Campaign 78V-138 attribute twenty-seven deaths to Pinto rear-impact fuel-system fires across the model years covered by the recall — 1971 through 1976 Pintos and 1975 and 1976 Mercury Bobcats[<sup>23</sup>](#c3-23). The Dowie *Mother Jones* piece and subsequent advocacy estimates put the figure as high as 500 to 900 across the production run[<sup>24</sup>](#c3-24). Schwartz's 1991 re-examination argues that the Pinto's fire-fatality rate was not statistically extraordinary against comparable subcompacts and supports the lower NHTSA range as the defensible attribution[<sup>25</sup>](#c3-25). We record the range with methodologies cited: NHTSA-attributed 27; advocacy 500–900; the peer-reviewed re-examination supports the lower NHTSA figure. The disfiguring burn-injury count is documented at levels several multiples of the fatality figure. The named *Grimshaw* plaintiff, Richard Grimshaw, was thirteen years old at the time of his May 28, 1972 accident; he suffered burns over ninety per cent of his body and lost his nose, his left ear, and most of his fingers. Lily Gray, the driver of the Pinto in that accident, died of her burns[<sup>26</sup>](#c3-26).

What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility? It would attach to the Ford design-program leaders who approved the fuel-tank location at the 1969–1970 design freeze. To the engineering managers who reviewed the 1970–1971 crash-test results and approved production nonetheless. To the executives who approved the cost-benefit framework and the not-fix decision across the 1971–1976 model-year refreshes. To the regulatory-strategy team that crafted the "we comply with the standards in effect" framing across the decade. To the program-oversight executives — including Iacocca, who held program-oversight authority as Ford president during the design and post-production review periods. It would attach less heavily to the striking drivers whose ordinary collisions were converted into fatal fires by the design choice.

The corporation is correctly in the chain. The *Grimshaw* punitive verdict landed there. But the corporation served as a sufficiency container, and the individuals whose roles had carried design and program-oversight authority faced no personal civil or criminal liability for the choices we have documented.

The diagnostic survives the forty-year jump.

## What civil discovery pried loose

There is a recurring problem in any responsibility case: the record. Who keeps the documents, who has subpoena power over them, who decides what is producible, who narrates the documents back to the public after they emerge. The diagnostic's sixth question — who controlled the record? — is not a question one answers once. The answer changes as the case moves through procedural venues. Sometimes the record never hardens at all.

In Dieselgate, the record-control fight was won, in operational terms, in two stages. The first stage was the empirical anomaly itself: the CAFEE PEMS data of May 2014 was a measurement that did not require the corporation's cooperation[<sup>27</sup>](#c3-27). It was a number, attached to a car, attached to a road. CARB's enforcement persistence across the following sixteen months converted the measurement into a regulator-actionable case. The second stage was the EPA Notice of Violation of September 18, 2015[<sup>28</sup>](#c3-28), and then the DOJ Statement of Facts filed January 11, 2017 with the Volkswagen AG plea agreement in the Eastern District of Michigan, with the plea entered March 10, 2017 before Judge Sean F. Cox[<sup>29</sup>](#c3-29). From that day forward, the chain of internal decisions was a public document in a court of the United States. The corporation could no longer narrate it alone.

What did not harden, in the same proceedings, was the Jones Day investigation. Volkswagen commissioned the law firm in September 2015. The full report has not been publicly released. Portions have entered US and German court records under protective order; witness accounts have surfaced through subsequent proceedings; the corporation has continued to resist disclosure of the full work product. This is the diagnostic's sixth question in operation. Who controlled the record? In the case of the DOJ Statement of Facts: a federal court. In the case of the Jones Day report: the corporation that commissioned it, and the law firm bound to it.

In Pinto, the record hardened in a different venue under a different mechanism. Ford had built the Pinto, run the crash tests, prepared the memorandum, and kept the documents. *Grimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.* was a civil-discovery proceeding in California state court. Discovery in the United States gives the plaintiff a tool that no consumer letter, no journalism inquiry, no internal whistleblower request matches: it compels the defendant to produce documents the defendant would prefer to keep. Ford's in-house crash-test results entered the trial record in February 1978 because civil discovery compelled their production. The cost-benefit memorandum entered the trial record because civil discovery compelled its production. The Court of Appeal's published opinion of May 29, 1981, at 119 Cal.App.3d 757, then locked the documents into the appellate record — citable, retrievable, and read in product-liability courses across the country for the next forty years[<sup>30</sup>](#c3-30).

Two venues, two record-hardening mechanisms, separated by four decades. A regulator notice in 2015. A court opinion in 1981. The diagnostic's instruction is not to favor one venue over the other. The instruction is to ask, at the moment the case appears to close: which document, in which court, with which retention rule, has become the case's permanent record? The answer locates where responsibility's first reading was set.

Some records did not harden at all. Bosch's 2007–2008 warning memoranda to Volkswagen, referenced in the DOJ filings and in Bosch's January 2017 US settlement, have not been publicly released in their underlying form. The internal Ford engineering documents prior to the Pinto crash-test program — design-review minutes that might have shown the earlier moment at which the fuel-tank location was selected — remained within Ford's archives. Record control is partial. Even in cases where civil and criminal interception succeed, fragments of the chain stay private. Where the record never hardens at all — where no civil discovery is available, no regulator has subpoena power, no criminal docket opens — the diagnostic's later questions return unknowns. We work cases of that kind later. Here we have worked the cases where the record was pried loose.

## The Schwartz objection, taken seriously

Both cases face serious objections. We do not deflect them. We take them at their strongest.

The strongest objection to the Pinto framing comes from Gary T. Schwartz, in the law-review article cited above[<sup>31</sup>](#c3-31). Schwartz argued, in 1991, that the popular narrative of the Pinto case — the Dowie *Mother Jones* piece, the Cullen monograph, the popular shorthand of "the exploding Pinto" — overstates the case in several ways. The fatality count, on the documented NHTSA figure, is twenty-seven across the recall model years, not the 500 to 900 figure that circulated in advocacy literature[<sup>32</sup>](#c3-32). The cost-benefit memorandum, Schwartz argued, was not a Pinto-only secret calculation but a regulatory-context cost-benefit document prepared as part of NHTSA's rollover and fuel-system rulemaking. Civil liability succeeded at *Grimshaw*. The recall happened. FMVSS 301 was strengthened[<sup>33</sup>](#c3-33). The case is taught in every product-liability course in US law schools. Calling this "partial scapegoat," Schwartz's reading would suggest, overstates the laundering: accountability did climb to the corporation, the design choices were named in court, and the regulatory architecture was reformed in response.

The objection is at its strongest at the *Grimshaw* appellate stage and at the FMVSS 301 reform stage. We record both. The fatality range is preserved as a range with methodologies cited. The memorandum's framing is preserved per Schwartz: a regulatory cost-benefit document, not a secret. Schwartz's sustaining of the core finding — that Ford's design and approval choices could and should have prevented foreseeable harm — is our anchor on the substance.

But the objection is weakest, and our reading strongest, on the named-individual layer. The diagnostic question is not "did the corporation pay?" The corporation paid. The diagnostic question is *whether responsibility followed control, benefit, knowledge, and preventability*. The chain of design authority for the Pinto ran through named Ford engineers, named program managers, and named executives including Iacocca, who as Ford president oversaw the program. None of those named individuals faced personal civil or criminal liability for the design or for the not-fix decision. The corporation absorbed the civil punitive damages and the recall; the corporation was acquitted on the criminal charge in Indiana. The cost-benefit memorandum, in its origin a regulatory document, became the artifact that named the not-fix decision but did not name the deciders.

The strongest objection to the Volkswagen framing runs the other way. *This is deliberate fraud, not laundering.* James Liang is in prison[<sup>34</sup>](#c3-34). Oliver Schmidt served seven years[<sup>35</sup>](#c3-35). Rupert Stadler, the former Audi chief executive, was convicted in Munich in June 2023 of fraud by negligence; he received a suspended twenty-one-month sentence and a €1.1 million fine[<sup>36</sup>](#c3-36). Volkswagen AG pleaded guilty in Detroit[<sup>37</sup>](#c3-37).

The corporation paid nearly $30 billion across penalties and settlements. The criminal penalty came to $2.8 billion. The civil penalty added $1.5 billion. The US consumer settlement reached $14.7 billion. Subsequent global settlements supplied the remainder. Robert Bosch, separately, paid $327.5 million[<sup>38</sup>](#c3-38).

This is what working accountability looks like; the laundering framing obscures rather than illuminates what happened here.

The objection is correct that the deliberate-fraud overlay is real. We do not deny it. Our point is taxonomic and procedural, and it is narrower than the objection assumes. *Inside* the fraud, Volkswagen ran a multi-stage laundering sequence — three sub-alibis, deployed in order, each pierced before the next was deployed. The "technical issues" framing protected the corporation for sixteen months. The "rogue engineers" framing protected Volkswagen executives for at least the first six months of the criminal docket. The "defeat-device software" framing protected the design and approval chain across the consumer-settlement period. Each sub-alibi was eventually pierced. That is the point. The diagnostic detects the staging.

And the diagnostic detects, in Volkswagen, where the chain stopped. Liang and Schmidt are in the chain — and the US criminal system reached them. The May 2018 US indictment of the former chief executive of Volkswagen AG, Martin Winterkorn, alongside four other executives, is in the chain — and the US criminal system did not reach him; he has not been extradited. The German Braunschweig fraud charges against the same individual opened on September 16, 2021 — and were severed for medical reasons; subsequent proceedings remain pending as of May 2026, and he continues to deny involvement. The supervisory-board members briefed across 2014 and 2015 are in the chain — and they face no individual adjudication. The Jones Day report is in the chain — and it remains substantially private.

"What working accountability looks like" is the right frame for Liang and Schmidt. It is not the right frame for what happened, or did not happen, above them. Both objections survive in part. Both are answered in part. Neither dissolves the diagnostic — both demonstrate it.

There are two moments at which the public story can no longer be sustained. The diagnostic does not produce them. The diagnostic detects them once they happen.

In Dieselgate, the moment was the CARB threat to withhold 2016-model-year certification, which forced the September 3, 2015 confession at El Monte, and then the September 18, 2015 EPA Notice of Violation that converted the confession into a record. Inside seven months, Volkswagen had pleaded guilty in federal court. The "technical issues" framing did not survive the EPA's notice. The "rogue engineers" framing did not survive the May 2018 indictment of five executives. The "defeat-device software" framing survives in trade-press shorthand but did not survive the Statement of Facts, which names Volkswagen AG as the entity that "by and through its employees" designed and concealed the device.

In Pinto, the moment was the *Grimshaw* jury's verdict on February 6, 1978: approximately $2.5 million in compensatory damages to the Grimshaw and Gray plaintiffs combined ($2,516,000 to Richard Grimshaw and $559,680 to the Gray estate) and $125 million in punitive damages — the largest punitive award in US product-liability history at the time. The trial court reduced the punitive damages to $3.5 million on Ford's post-trial motion. The May 29, 1981 published appellate opinion sustained both compensatory and reduced punitive damages and articulated the "conscious disregard" standard for punitive liability in design-defect cases. The "we comply with the standards in effect" framing survived the trial verdict; it did not survive the appellate opinion's recitation of the in-house crash-test results and the cost-benefit memorandum.

In each case, the alibi did not collapse on its own. It collapsed under documentary weight. In Dieselgate, the document was an enforcement notice citing a peer-reviewed measurement and a corporate confession; in Pinto, the document was a published appellate opinion citing the manufacturer's own crash tests and its own internal memorandum. The diagnostic's instruction is not to wait for the document to arrive; the diagnostic's instruction is to identify which document, if produced, would force the alibi to weaken — and then to ask why that document has not yet been produced.

## Run the eight questions, slowly

Our rule is the diagnostic itself. Run it. Run it in order. Run it on every case where someone is publicly blamed. The full eight questions are collected in the field guide at the back, in the form a reader can carry away.

When the public chain and the responsibility chain match, no laundering occurred. When they diverge, the gap names the laundering — and the eight questions name where, in the chain, the gap opened.

The questions form a diagnostic, not a verdict. They do not by themselves convict anyone. They do not assign moral weight. They do not tell us how angry to be. They tell us where to look. They tell us, when an explanation has been offered, which parts of the chain that explanation has accounted for and which parts it has stepped past.

Run them slowly. Each one in turn. The diagnostic does its work in the order: the public attribution is named first because that is the version we are presented with; control is named second because that is the version we are being kept from; benefit, knowledge, preventability, and record-control then run the chain upward to the layer the public attribution did not reach. Cost-bearing is named seventh because cost-bearing is the dimension the laundering relies on us to ignore. The eighth question is the test. Re-run the case with control as the ordering principle. Where the chain stops this time, when compared with where the public chain stopped, gives the measure of the laundering. The order matters less than the completion: a case run through all eight questions, in any order, will produce the same chain map. The order recommended here is the order in which the public typically encounters the case.

## Five channels, two cases

Both cases ran into interception. Neither ran into clean interception. The asymmetry between them — between which interception channels worked and which did not — is the load-bearing teaching point.

Set the channels side by side. Five channels carry the comparison: independent technical verification, regulator finding, civil action, criminal action against named individuals below the executive layer, and criminal action at the executive layer.

::: {.figure-embed}
![Volkswagen Dieselgate against Ford Pinto across five accountability channels — what climbed and what stalled in two famous corporate cases, four decades apart.](book/evidence/diagrams/proofs/ch03-vw-vs-pinto-channels.png)
:::

The first three channels — independent verification, regulator finding, civil action — worked in both cases. The interception that worked in Pinto's technical layer surfaced through civil discovery; Dieselgate's equivalent surfaced through independent third-party measurement. In each case the alibi weakened under documentary weight and a published primary record emerged.

The bottom two channels carry the asymmetry. The criminal channel in Dieselgate reached two named individuals at the engineering and compliance-executive layers; it did not reach the chief executive in the United States and remains contested in Germany. The criminal channel in Pinto reached the corporation as defendant and acquitted it; it never reached an executive. Civil action in Pinto landed on the corporation and stopped there. Iacocca walked from Ford to Chrysler four months after the *Grimshaw* verdict and never faced personal liability for the Pinto. Winterkorn was indicted in two jurisdictions; he has not been adjudicated in either, as of May 2026.

The asymmetry is the lesson. Two cases, forty years apart. The same diagnostic instrument. Different escape channels left open. Watch only the civil channel — only *Grimshaw*, only the Volkswagen consumer settlement — and we see a story of corporate accountability and stop there. Watch only the criminal channel — only Liang and Schmidt, only the Indiana acquittal — and we see either modern justice working or a forty-five-year-old failure of justice, depending on which case. Neither view is wrong; both are incomplete. The diagnostic forces us to track every channel and to record which channel locked the public attribution and which channel walked past the chain it was supposed to reach.

The cost of escape, in each case, was real. Volkswagen paid roughly $30 billion across penalties and settlements. Two named individuals served prison time. The corporation pleaded guilty to felony counts in a US federal court. Bosch paid $327.5 million. The European in-use compliance regime under Real Driving Emissions, effective 2017, is a direct regulatory response. None of this is nothing. And none of it reached the supervisory board, or the people in the briefings between May 2014 and September 2015, who had sixteen months to choose differently.

Ford paid civil damages in *Grimshaw* and in numerous subsequent product-liability cases; recalled 1.5 million vehicles; bore reputational cost that contributed to the Pinto's discontinuation after model year 1980. None of this is nothing. And none of it reached the Ford executives who approved the design and the framing.

The teaching point is portable. When a case is described as "closed" or "resolved," ask which channel closed it. Ask which channels did not. The diagnostic's eight questions are the test for whether the channels that closed the case were the channels that needed to. When the channel that closed the case names a corporation and the responsibility chain runs upward to individuals who held control, the chain has been intercepted at the corporate layer and walked past at the individual layer. That is not failure; that is partial interception. We leave here prepared to recognise partial interception when it is presented as closure.

The diagnostic is portable to our lives. Not as a rhetorical move; as an instrument. We will not be regulators. We will not depose anyone. But the eight questions are usable in the smaller versions of the cases walked above, and the smaller versions are everywhere. They show up the way Volkswagen and Ford did: the explanation moves twice within a finite window. A workplace investigation that begins as edge-case behavior, hardens into a vendor-quality issue, and closes on the most junior person who touched the file. A loan denial, a content takedown, a hiring filter, where the account shifts from policy to operator to model. The warning sign is the three-stage shift — three official stories in sequence, each naming a lower or more disposable cause than the last — and the single sharpest move against it is to ask, in writing, who knew or should have known. That question carries the longest paper trail: Bosch's 2007–2008 written warnings to Volkswagen and Ford's 1970–1971 in-house crash-test memoranda became load-bearing precisely because they were on paper, and they were asked for.

The eight questions are the instrument. We ran them twice above. We can run them on any case we read about for the rest of our lives. What follows will run them, too — on cases that are messier than these two, on cases where the chain runs through algorithms and committees and procedural shells, on cases where the document that would lock the record has not yet been produced and may never be. The diagnostic is what survives those cases.

It is not a verdict. It is not a remedy. It is a way of looking at a story long enough to see the gap.

When we see the gap, we have found the laundering.
