# Chapter 7 — The Record Is the Battlefield

> The *isnad* is a matter of religion; and were it not for the *isnad* any one could say what he pleased.
>
> — Alfred Guillaume, *The Traditions of Islam: An Introduction to the Study of the Hadith Literature* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1924), ch. IV, p. 84

On 11 November 2010, in Court 2 of Guildford Crown Court, Seema Misra was sentenced to fifteen months in prison. She was thirty-five years old and eight weeks pregnant. She had operated a Post Office branch on Old Woking Road in West Byfleet, Surrey, since June 2005. The charge was theft of £74,609 from her own branch. The principal evidence was the accounting record produced by a piece of software called Horizon, supplied to the Post Office by Fujitsu Services Ltd.

The prosecution was brought by the Post Office itself. Under English law the Post Office could prosecute privately, without referral to the Crown Prosecution Service. The party bringing the case, the party investigating, the party producing the evidence, and the party trying the case were all the same institution.

A Fujitsu engineer, Gareth Jenkins, gave expert evidence at the trial. The substance of that evidence, on Horizon's remote-access architecture and on the reliability of the branch-level accounts, was treated as authoritative by the jury. The defence had not been given the underlying engineering record. The defence did not have it because the Post Office had not disclosed it. The Post Office had not disclosed it because the engineering record sat inside Fujitsu's contracted systems, and Fujitsu's contract was with the Post Office.

Misra was told by Post Office investigators, before the trial, that no other sub-postmaster had reported similar problems with Horizon. She was told that Horizon was reliable. She was told that no one could remotely alter her branch accounts without her knowing. Each representation was offered as a fact. Each was central to her decision to contest the charge.

Her son was born while she was on bail awaiting sentence. After the verdict she was taken straight from the dock to HM Prison Bronzefield. Her conviction was not quashed until 23 April 2021 — more than ten years later — by the Court of Appeal in *Hamilton & Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 577.

The fair clue is in the sentence above the verdict. *No other sub-postmaster has reported similar problems.* We do not yet know what was happening at the same moment in other Post Office branches across the United Kingdom. We will return to that sentence.

## Three representations, one system

The Post Office's account of Horizon, sustained from the system's rollout in 1999 through to the disclosure phase of the *Bates* Group Litigation in 2017–2019, rested on three layered representations. Each was load-bearing for the prosecution model. Each was found false, in the operative sense, by Mr Justice Peter Fraser's *Horizon Issues* judgment of 16 December 2019.

The first representation was that Horizon was reliable. The Post Office told sub-postmasters, told courts, and told Parliament that the system was robust and that any shortfall it displayed at a branch represented a genuine discrepancy attributable to the sub-postmaster. The representation rested for evidentiary purposes on a common-law presumption that the output of a properly used computer is presumed reliable. The presumption was captured in cases such as *Castle v Cross* [1984] 1 WLR 1372. It was restored as the operative rule by the 1999 repeal of section 69 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984. The burden of rebutting it falls on the party who challenges the output. In practice, the sub-postmaster in the dock bore the burden of proving that the machine had erred. The engineering record that would have allowed any such proof sat in Fujitsu's hands. Fraser J, in [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB), found that the Horizon system was not "remotely robust" in the relevant respects. He further found that bugs, errors, and defects had had the potential to cause apparent or alleged shortfalls in branch accounts.

The second representation was that no other sub-postmaster had reported similar problems. Post Office investigators told individual sub-postmasters under investigation that their case was isolated. The representation was false from no later than May 2009. On 11 May 2009, *Computer Weekly* journalist Rebecca Thomson aggregated the dispersed accounts in a single article. The Justice for Subpostmasters Alliance (JFSA) was founded by Alan Bates in the same month. The Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry, statutory under the Inquiries Act 2005 since 1 June 2021, contains documentary evidence on its transcript record that the representation continued to be made in individual investigations after that date.

The third representation was that remote access to a branch's accounts was not possible without the sub-postmaster's knowledge. Fujitsu engineers in fact had the capability to make remote edits to branch accounts through processes the Inquiry record names — operational change requests, remediation actions, balancing transactions. The capability was denied or minimised in expert evidence given by Gareth Jenkins in multiple Post Office prosecutions, including the Misra trial of 2010. The denial was later established as untrue per the *Horizon Issues* judgment and the subsequent Inquiry disclosure.

We can hold each of those three representations in mind separately. The point is that they were not separate. They were a system. The reliability claim placed the evidentiary burden on the defence. The isolation claim foreclosed any joint defence. The remote-access claim closed off the only forensic route by which a sub-postmaster could have shown that the Horizon record had been altered by someone other than herself. Each representation depended on the engineering record that would have falsified it being held by the institution making the claim. Record-control laundering is not single-shot misinformation. It is the structural inaccessibility of the evidence that would falsify the official story.

## Five roles, one institution

Our diagnostic asks eight questions — blame, control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, cost, and what responsibility would look like if it followed control instead of visibility.

In every prior chapter the diagnostic has worked. Asked of the Boeing 737 MAX certification, asked of the proxy-shootdown of MH17, asked of the Stanford prison file or the wartime court-martial chain, the eight questions have walked the responsibility chain upward from the visible scapegoat to the actor whose control, benefit, knowledge, and preventability mark them. Now we close Part II by exposing the diagnostic's structural limit.

The Post Office held five roles simultaneously across the period of the laundering. It was the **deployer** of the Horizon system — the institution that bought the software from Fujitsu and ran it on every branch terminal. It was, through its contract with Fujitsu, the institutional **holder of the audit trail** of Horizon's behaviour. That audit trail included the Known Error Log (KEL), the source code, the remote-access logs, and the engineering records the *Bates* litigation would later force into disclosure. It was the **complainant** when a Horizon shortfall appeared — the party identifying the sub-postmaster as having stolen or falsely accounted for the missing cash. It was the **prosecutor**: the Post Office brought criminal prosecutions of sub-postmasters directly under English law, without Crown Prosecution Service charging review. The Inquiry's Volume 1 Final Report records, at paragraph 3.24, that "approximately 1,000 persons were prosecuted and convicted throughout the United Kingdom" during the period the Inquiry covers, based on Horizon evidence; 236 received prison sentences per contemporaneous reporting and earlier Inquiry interim work. And it was the **disclosure controller** — the party responsible for disclosing to the defence any material that might undermine the prosecution case or assist the defence. That responsibility ran under the Criminal Procedure and Investigations Act 1996, including the initial duty under section 3 and the continuing duty under section 7A added by the Criminal Justice Act 2003.

Five roles. One institution. Walk the eight questions through that architecture and watch each one collide with it.

Fujitsu Services Ltd held the engineering record and the source code; against the five roles stood the lone sub-postmaster in the dock, one per branch.

*Who was publicly blamed?* The sub-postmaster at whose branch the Horizon shortfall appeared.

*Who had control?* Post Office Ltd held control of the prosecutorial process, the investigative function, the contractual relationship with Fujitsu, and the disclosure process. Fujitsu Services Ltd held control of the engineering record and the remote-access capability. No outside party held meaningful control of any element of the chain.

*Who benefited?* Post Office Ltd avoided the cost of investigating and remedying Horizon's underlying defects by routing branch shortfalls into personal-liability recoveries from sub-postmasters under their contracts. Fujitsu Services Ltd retained the Horizon contract and continued to bid for and win further UK Government IT contracts across the period.

*Who knew or should have known?* Per Fraser J's *Horizon Issues* findings of 16 December 2019[<sup>1</sup>](#c7-1), Post Office Ltd and Fujitsu Services Ltd both had institutional notice of relevant Horizon bugs across the period. The KEL recorded bugs that had the potential to cause apparent or alleged shortfalls in branch accounts. One of them, listed in the *Horizon Issues* judgment's schedule of bugs as the Dalmellington bug, struck a branch transferring cash to a sub-branch: the screen froze, and each press of the key silently confirmed a further dispatch of the same sum, so one transfer registered as several and the system showed a shortfall that was never real[<sup>1</sup>](#c7-1). The sub-postmaster was then held liable for it. On 15 July 2013, in written advice to the Post Office subsequently disclosed to the Inquiry, the barrister Simon Clarke of Cartwright King advised that prosecutions which had relied on Jenkins's expert evidence had serious problems[<sup>2</sup>](#c7-2). The Inquiry transcript record is reconstructing chain-of-command knowledge inside the Post Office in detail that the *Bates* litigation alone could not reach.

*Who could have prevented recurrence?* Anyone with disclosure authority over the engineering record. CPIA-compliant disclosure to the defence in any one of the prosecutions would have placed the KEL into the hands of a sub-postmaster's lawyer. None of the prior prosecutions saw the KEL. The CPIA disclosure failure is the load-bearing prosecutorial finding in the Court of Appeal's April 2021 *Hamilton & Others* judgment[<sup>3</sup>](#c7-3): the Court held that the Post Office's failure to disclose what it knew about Horizon's unreliability was "an affront to the conscience of the Court."

*Who controlled the record?* The institution making the accusation.

*Who bore the cost?* The sub-postmaster in the dock.

What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility? It would point at the architecture itself. The architecture is the answer.

Six of the eight questions return the same institution. Two return its contractor. None of the eight reaches a finding without disclosure of the engineering record. And disclosure of the engineering record was, for twenty years, controlled by the institution being diagnosed.

Where one institution holds all five roles — deployer, audit-trail controller, complainant, prosecutor, and disclosure controller — the record stops being evidence and becomes a weapon. The eight questions can be asked; they cannot be answered.

Two case-type labels apply, and we decline to choose between them. The hybrid is explicit. Horizon is, at the institutional layer, a **system/object alibi**: the software's output is treated as the diagnostic authority, the sub-postmaster who disputes the output is treated as disputing reality, and the corporate architecture that produced the output disappears into the machine's name. Horizon is, at the cost-distribution layer, a **cost-bearing goat** pattern: the sub-postmasters bore prosecution, bankruptcy, imprisonment, and in documented cases suicide while the architecture that produced their convictions remained in place. Both classifications are load-bearing. The system/object alibi names the mechanism. The cost-bearing goat names the harm.

## Therac-25, Dreyfus, MH17

The five-role conflation is the institutional form of a mechanism that operates at every scale at which records can be made and held. We now take two callbacks — one beneath the institutional layer, one above it — to show the same shape at the artifact layer and the state layer. The walk through the eight questions does not need to be repeated. Chapters 2 and 4 installed those cases at full depth. The point here is the comparison.

In Chapter 2 we met the Therac-25, the radiation therapy machine built by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited, which produced fatal overdoses at six US and Canadian treatment centres between June 1985 and January 1987. The system/object alibi at the artifact layer rested, as Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner reconstructed in their 1993 *IEEE Computer* analysis, on a race condition in the machine's software and on a treatment terminal whose audit trail was a single memory-mapped error-code counter. Operators saw "Malfunction 54" and "TREATMENT PAUSE" on a green screen with no logged history of the scan parameters that had preceded the fault. When the FDA's Edward Miller and the rest of the investigation team pieced the failure mode together across 1986 and 1987, the load-bearing forensic move was reverse-engineering the machine's behaviour from the *absence* of a record. The audit trail had not been hidden. It had not been kept. At the artifact layer, record-control laundering is sometimes the omission of a record by design — the audit trail that would have caught the fault was never built into the machine.

A briefer historical anchor. In 1894 the Statistical Section of the French Army General Staff convicted Alfred Dreyfus on a document — the bordereau — whose comparison material the prosecution refused to disclose. The Cour de Cassation's eventual quashing in 1906 rested on the survival of the Statistical Section's own archive. Where the record exists despite the institution's interest in suppressing it, the responsibility chain can climb. Where the record has been suppressed, falsified, or never made, it cannot.

At the state layer, the same logic appears. On 17 July 2014, a Buk surface-to-air missile, fired from territory in eastern Donetsk Oblast under the partial control of armed groups designating themselves the "Donetsk People's Republic," brought down a Malaysia Airlines Boeing 777 — Malaysia Airlines Flight 17 — en route from Amsterdam to Kuala Lumpur. All 298 people on board were killed. The Russian Federation's official accounts — a Russian Ministry of Defence press conference of 21 July 2014 advancing a Ukrainian Su-25 alternative cause, manufacturer Almaz-Antey press briefings in June and October 2015 attempting to relocate the missile's origin onto Ukrainian-held stocks — depended on the same record-control logic the Post Office relied on. The state controlling the territory of the launch site and the source of the launcher controlled the record of who had moved what across its border.

What broke the alibi at the state layer was a record assembled outside that state's control. The Joint Investigation Team, led by the Dutch Public Prosecution Service and including Australia, Belgium, Malaysia, and Ukraine, pieced the chain of custody of the Buk-TELAR launcher together from intercepted telephone communications shared by Ukraine, satellite imagery from US and EU sources, photographs and video posted to social media by Russian and Ukrainian civilians along the convoy route, and Bellingcat's open-source-intelligence reconstruction of the convoy's geolocated path. The JIT's identification of the launcher on 24 May 2018 as belonging to the Russian 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade rested on a record assembled in the open, across sovereign jurisdictions, in a way the Russian Federation could not retroactively close. The Hague District Court at Schiphol convicted Igor Girkin, Sergey Dubinsky, and Leonid Kharchenko in absentia on 17 November 2022 of the murder of all 298 persons on board; Oleg Pulatov was acquitted. None of the four has served the sentences. The JIT's closing statement of 8 February 2023 identified strong indications that the decision to supply the Buk was taken at senior Russian command level — a prosecutorial finding short of indictment, not a court verdict. The Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights, in admissibility decisions of 30 November 2022 and a merits judgment of 9 July 2025 in *Ukraine and the Netherlands v. Russia*, found multiple violations of the Convention, including in respect of MH17.

Three scales. One mechanism. At the artifact layer (Therac-25), the audit trail is absent by design. At the institutional layer (Horizon), the audit trail exists but is locked inside the institution that benefits from its inaccessibility. At the state layer (MH17), the audit trail can be assembled in the open from civic infrastructure that the institution cannot recall after the fact. The escape paths differ by scale. The architecture of the laundering is the same.

## The layered lawful-step defense

The Post Office did not need to suppress the engineering record by some single act. The architecture suppressed it by default. A stack of layers, each lawful in itself, converged to make the Horizon output structurally unfalsifiable from any position a sub-postmaster could occupy.

The first layer was the sub-postmaster's contract. The standard sub-postmaster contract made the sub-postmaster personally liable for branch shortfalls regardless of cause. The contract converted any Horizon-displayed shortfall into a recoverable debt against the individual. That was true whether the shortfall represented genuine cash missing, a software error in Horizon, a remote-access transaction the sub-postmaster had not made, or a bug in a balancing transaction the Fujitsu help desk had instructed the sub-postmaster to enter. Fraser J's *Common Issues* judgment of 15 March 2019, [2019] EWHC 606 (QB), found that the contract could not be read as making sub-postmasters strictly liable for shortfalls of unknown cause. That finding was twenty years after the contract terms had begun operating as if they did.

The second layer was the common-law presumption of computer reliability. English law, from *Castle v Cross* onward, treated the output of a properly used computer as presumptively reliable. The defence carried the burden of rebutting the presumption. Without disclosure of the engineering record, no individual sub-postmaster could rebut it in practice. The Law Commission opened a post-2019 consultation on whether the presumption survives the Horizon case. As of May 2026, that consultation is ongoing. The rule itself has not been statutorily reformed.

The third layer was private prosecution. English law permitted the Post Office to bring criminal prosecutions directly, without Crown Prosecution Service charging review. The structural distance that public prosecution provides — the separation of complainant from prosecutor that the CPS's two-stage charging test was designed to enforce — was absent. The Post Office investigated its own complaints, framed its own charges, controlled its own disclosure, and pressed its own cases at trial. The same body whose accounting system had produced the alleged shortfall stood at the prosecution table.

The fourth layer was the dispersion of victims. Sub-postmasters operated as ostensibly self-employed agents, one per branch, scattered across the United Kingdom from Llandudno on the north Welsh coast to West Byfleet in Surrey. No aggregating institution existed before Alan Bates founded the JFSA in May 2009. Without coordination, the individual sub-postmaster under investigation had no way to know that her case was not isolated. The Post Office's representation that no other sub-postmaster had reported similar problems exploited the architecture of the network the Post Office had built.

The fifth layer was the plea-rate architecture. The Inquiry's Volume 1 Final Report records, at paragraph 3.24, that approximately 1,000 sub-postmasters were prosecuted and convicted across the United Kingdom based on Horizon evidence; contemporaneous reporting and the Inquiry's earlier interim work put the imprisonment count at 236. The Final Report was published 8 July 2025 under Sir Wyn Williams's chairmanship. The Court of Appeal, in its April 2021 *Hamilton* judgment, concluded that "all the guilty pleas of the successful appellants in the Horizon cases were founded upon Post Office Ltd's failures of investigation and disclosure" and that many sub-postmasters "may have felt they had no real alternative but to plead guilty"[<sup>4</sup>](#c7-4). Many sub-postmasters pleaded to false accounting — a lesser charge than theft. They did so on legal advice that the alternative was a contested trial against the Post Office's control of the engineering record, with prison consequences attached to a theft conviction. The high plea rate then reinforced the institutional narrative that the sub-postmasters had stolen. The architecture produced its own confirmatory data.

None of these layers, taken alone, is unusual in English commercial or criminal practice. Personal-liability contract terms exist across many agency relationships. The common-law presumption of computer reliability protects every electronic record offered as evidence. Private prosecution is a long-standing feature of English procedure. Geographically dispersed agency networks are common. Plea-based dispositions are the statistical majority of criminal outcomes in England and Wales. The observation here is that record-control hardening is a layered architecture. No one layer made the laundering possible. Their convergence did.

This is the observation that exports. Record-hardening is not a single decision by a single actor. It is the cumulative effect of contractual, common-law, and procedural defaults each of which is unremarkable in isolation. Each layer has to be attacked separately. Reform of any one layer alone is not enough.

## A decade against the architecture

Resistance to record-control laundering is expensive and slow. We name both costs.

The resistance began at Craig-y-Don, Llandudno. Alan Bates, sub-postmaster of a branch on the north coast of Wales, found Horizon shortfalls he could not reconcile and refused to cover them from his own pocket. He was suspended by the Post Office in 2003. He did not stop. Across the following six years he continued to assemble what he could from his own records, his own correspondence with the Post Office helpdesk, and what other sub-postmasters were prepared to tell him.

The first public aggregation of the dispersed accounts was Rebecca Thomson's *Computer Weekly* article of 11 May 2009 — "Bankruptcy, prosecution and disrupted lives — postmasters tell their story." For the first time a recognisable pattern existed in the public record. JFSA was founded the same month. Karl Flinders's continuing investigative work at *Computer Weekly* from 2009 onward became the longest-running journalistic archive of the scandal, an open-source documentary base on which later litigation would draw.

In 2012, under sustained pressure from MPs including James Arbuthnot, then MP for North East Hampshire, the Post Office commissioned Second Sight Support Services to conduct an independent investigation. As Second Sight's findings began to indict the Post Office's account, the Post Office terminated the Second Sight contract. The termination was March 2015. The Post Office's first independent technical examination of Horizon was ended by the institution that had commissioned it, before the examination's conclusions could enter the prosecutorial record.

In 2013 the barrister Simon Clarke provided advice to the Post Office that prosecutions which had relied on Gareth Jenkins's expert evidence had serious problems, given Jenkins's failure to disclose Horizon issues. The Clarke advice is a document on the Inquiry record. The Post Office did not act on it to systematically review or stop affected prosecutions.

A decade of resistance against an institution that had every advantage. The mathematics of the resistance are worth stating. One civil-society aggregating institution (JFSA), founded by a former sub-postmaster doing the work unpaid. One specialist investigative outlet (*Computer Weekly*) sustaining coverage for more than a decade against the cost of doing so without commercial reward. One commissioned independent investigation (Second Sight) terminated by the institution that had commissioned it. One piece of legal advice (Clarke) acknowledging the problem inside the Post Office's own counsel function, and not acted upon. Across all of this the prosecutions continued. The architecture survived the resistance because the architecture controlled the record on which any individual prosecution had to be defended.

The strongest counterargument to the frame we have built is here, and we take it at full strength. Horizon was a documented miscarriage of justice that the institutions eventually acknowledged. Fraser J's judgments, the Hamilton appeal, the statutory inquiry, the statutory exoneration of 2024, the apology and the returned CBE, the ongoing investigation by Operation Olympos — all of it demonstrates accountability did climb. To call this 'record-control laundering' is to under-count the institutional repair work.

The institutional repair work happened. Every element named in the counterargument is on the public record. Our response is not that the repair work did not happen but that it required *every one* of six load-bearing preconditions. Sustained civil-society aggregation against active institutional resistance (JFSA). Third-party litigation funding (Therium) without which no individual sub-postmaster could have afforded the disclosure phase. A High Court willing to manage a Group Litigation Order through years of disclosure. An independent technical examiner (Jason Coyne) capable of reverse-engineering the Horizon error logs once disclosed. A judge (Fraser J) willing to write a 313-page judgment naming the system as not remotely robust. A Criminal Cases Review Commission willing to refer the principal block of cases to the Court of Appeal. Subtract any one of those preconditions and the alibi sustains. Subtract any two and it sustains comfortably.

The architecture that made the interception necessary remains in place. That is the harder claim. We return to it in the counter-case ahead.

The breakthrough came in stages, and each stage required an instrument the prior twenty years had lacked.

In March 2017 the High Court of Justice granted a Group Litigation Order in *Bates and Others v Post Office Ltd*. The GLO is a procedural innovation in English civil practice that consolidates dispersed claims into one managed action under the supervision of a judge with case-management authority. Funded by Therium, the litigation entered its disclosure phase. For the first time, court order compelled the Post Office and Fujitsu to produce the engineering records the prior criminal prosecutions had not seen — the KEL, the source-code-level engineering documentation, the remote-access logs.

On 15 March 2019, Mr Justice Peter Fraser handed down the *Common Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 606 (QB). The judgment found that the sub-postmaster contracts could not be construed as making sub-postmasters strictly liable for shortfalls of unknown cause. The first of the record-hardening layers had cracked.

On 16 December 2019, Fraser J handed down the *Horizon Issues* judgment, [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB). The judgment ran to 313 pages. It found that the Horizon IT system was not "remotely robust" in the relevant respects, that bugs, errors, and defects had had the potential to cause apparent or alleged shortfalls in branch accounts, and that Fujitsu and the Post Office had known of relevant bugs across the period of the laundering. The judgment rested on detailed examination of the KEL and the Fujitsu engineering records — material the Post Office and Fujitsu had not disclosed in the prior twenty years of criminal prosecutions.

The institutional preconditions of that judgment are not abstract. Fraser J could write what he wrote because the High Court compelled the disclosure that put the engineering record on his desk. Fraser J could read what was on his desk because Jason Coyne, the claimants' technical expert, had reverse-engineered the Horizon error logs and could reconstruct, in adversarial expert evidence, what the Horizon system had actually done across the relevant period — not what the Post Office's witnesses said it had done. Court with subpoena and disclosure power. Independent technical examiner with reverse-engineering capability. Both required. Neither alone is sufficient. A court without disclosure power cannot compel the record. A court with disclosure power but no independent examiner cannot interpret the disclosed record. The pairing is the load-bearing institutional fact of the entire counter-laundering process.

On 23 April 2021, the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) handed down *Hamilton & Others v Post Office Ltd* [2021] EWCA Crim 577. The judgment quashed thirty-nine convictions of sub-postmasters on the principal Horizon ground. The Court of Appeal held that the Post Office's failure to disclose what it knew about Horizon's unreliability was "an affront to the conscience of the Court"[<sup>5</sup>](#c7-5). Subsequent appellate judgments through 2023 quashed further convictions, bringing the judicially overturned total to approximately one hundred. The Post Office (Horizon System) Offences Act 2024, receiving Royal Assent on 24 May 2024, then provided statutory exoneration without case-by-case appeal for convictions falling within defined criteria — a political response to the bottleneck that case-by-case Criminal Cases Review Commission referral had produced.

The Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry, statutory under the Inquiries Act 2005 since 1 June 2021, with Sir Wyn Williams as chair, continues. Paula Vennells, who served as Chief Executive of Post Office Ltd from 2012 to 2019, gave evidence to the Inquiry across three days on 22, 23, and 24 May 2024 under oath. She had returned her CBE in January 2024 following public petition pressure after the broadcast of the ITV drama *Mr Bates vs The Post Office* on 1–4 January 2024. The drama did not produce the institutional reversal — that had been accomplished by Fraser J four and a half years earlier — but it produced the political acceleration that the 2024 Act required. As of May 2026, no criminal charge has been brought against any Post Office or Fujitsu personnel in connection with the laundering. Operation Olympos, the Metropolitan Police investigation into possible offences, remains ongoing. The Inquiry's final report is not yet published.

Two further elements of the record-hardening architecture deserve naming. Gareth Jenkins, the Fujitsu engineer whose expert evidence had been relied upon in multiple Post Office prosecutions including R v Misra (2010), is named in the Inquiry record. He has not been criminally prosecuted as of May 2026. The Inquiry's transcript record contains his evidence under oath. The criminal-status characterisation we apply to Vennells applies equally to Jenkins.

## The first device is the record

#### The first anti-laundering device is the record.

The rule is ordinal — first, not *primary among many* — because of a structural relationship, not a frequency claim. The eight-question diagnostic requires a record to operate on. Where the diagnosed institution controls the record, the diagnostic returns the institution's preferred answer. Restoring record-access is therefore prior to running the diagnostic, in the same sense that opening one's eyes is prior to reading. Having walked the five-role conflation, watched the eight questions collide with it, and followed the six preconditions of the interception, we now have the architecture to read the rule as a structural diagnostic rather than a slogan.

The operational form of the rule is the five-role distribution principle. No single institution should hold all five record-control roles — deployment, audit-trail, complaint, prosecution, disclosure — simultaneously. Where it does, an outside fact-finder with subpoena power and an independent technical examiner with reverse-engineering capability are the necessary instruments to break the asymmetry. Both required. Neither alone is sufficient.

This is what we export to Part III. The five-role conflation is not a description of a peculiar feature of late-twentieth-century Post Office governance. It is the typical architecture of an algorithmic decision system deployed by a single institution against a population of decision-subjects. The vendor builds the model. The deploying institution runs the model and holds the audit log. The deploying institution flags the decision-subject. The deploying institution adjudicates the appeal. The deploying institution decides what to disclose in the appeal. Five roles. One institution. The diagnostic we have installed will apply directly.

## Eight channels, asymmetric reach

The interception of the Horizon laundering proceeded across eight channels. Six succeeded. One is in progress. One has, to date, not delivered. The picture is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is the beat-9 teaching point.

![Eight channels of accountability against the Horizon scandal — which succeeded, which stalled, which has not been delivered.](book/evidence/diagrams/proofs/ch07-horizon-channels.png)

The record-control architecture remains intact in English law. The common-law presumption of computer reliability has not been statutorily reformed. The Post Office's private prosecution authority remains. The sub-postmaster contract's personal-liability term has been narrowed by *Common Issues* but the underlying contract architecture persists. The five record-control roles can still be held by a single institution in domains the law has not yet addressed.

The cost of the escape is the necessary companion to the table above. We do not soften it.

The interception took twenty years from the rollout of Horizon in 1999 to Fraser J's *Horizon Issues* judgment in 2019. Approximately seven hundred sub-postmasters were prosecuted across that period, with approximately four thousand more pursued through civil recovery. At least four documented suicides have been connected to Horizon prosecutions in public reporting, and the Inquiry has heard evidence regarding additional deaths attributed by relatives. Hundreds of sub-postmasters died before exoneration or compensation was completed. Seema Misra was sentenced in November 2010 while pregnant; her conviction was not quashed until April 2021, more than a decade later. Hundreds of sub-postmasters were bankrupted, lost their homes, lost their livelihoods. Communities lost post office services where branches closed after sub-postmaster removal.

The interception worked because the High Court compelled the record and Jason Coyne could read it. The interception took twenty years. Sub-postmasters died waiting. The architecture that made the interception necessary is still in place.

The teaching point follows directly. When a case of record-control laundering is described as resolved, ask which interception channel resolved it. Ask which channels did not. Ask whether the structural architecture remains in place. Six channels delivering remedy do not equal one channel delivering recurrence prevention.

Record-control laundering does not announce itself. It operates wherever the institution deciding against us also holds the tool that produced the decision, the audit trail of how the tool behaved, the dispute process, and the disclosure. The list is long and ordinary: algorithmic decisions on loans, benefits, child-welfare flags, predictive-policing scores, résumé screening, content takedowns; employment arbitration run on the employer's own HR record; consumer-credit disputes adjudicated on the reporting agency's own file; electronic health records in a malpractice claim; body-cam footage held by the department being challenged. One question surfaces it: who holds the record that could falsify the claim being made against me, and are they the same institution making the claim? When the answer is the same institution twice, the conflation is live — and three Horizon markers confirm it: the decider holds the tool, the appeal routes back to the decider, and the reliability claims (*it is robust; no one else reported this; remote alteration is impossible*) cannot be checked without the engineering record the cost-bearer cannot reach.

From there the move is a single move at every position: reach the record, or document that it was refused. Demand the engineering record in writing — audit log, change log, error history, access log, disclosure file — and treat a denial as the finding itself, not a dead end. Pleading or settling on a record one has not seen forecloses the route the *Bates* litigants kept open.

The trap closes on whoever is named accountable for a tool's output they cannot inspect. Horizon's sub-postmasters signed daily accounts on a display they could not audit; the Therac-25 operators pressed "P" past Malfunction 54 messages that logged nothing. The same shape, at artifact scale — and the warning is concrete: a request to certify or own a decision whose audit trail sits in another's hands, asked by the institution that also runs the dispute and controls the disclosure. *If you cannot inspect the record, you cannot legitimately sign for the output.* The signature is where the unauditable record finds its name.

Part III turns to algorithmic decision systems built around the same five-role conflation. The diagnostic we installed here applies directly to those systems. We leave Part II with the eight-question diagnostic and with the prior condition for the diagnostic to operate: the record. The first anti-laundering device is the record. Everything Part III will say about machine alibis builds on the architecture we installed here.
