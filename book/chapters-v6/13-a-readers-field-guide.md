# Chapter 13 — A Reader's Field Guide

> Is virtue a thing remote? I wish to be virtuous, and lo! virtue is at hand.
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* 7.29, trans. James Legge

Twelve chapters ago we opened on two goats brought before a priest at the entrance of a tent in the wilderness. One was killed. The other had the iniquities of the community confessed over its head and was sent into the desert with a designated man, never to return. The community watched. The priest spoke. The substitution was named.

What the modern case lost, we argued there, was the naming. The altar moved. The goats kept arriving — a Dutch council communist in a burning Reichstag, an oil-field worker on a Therac-25 treatment couch, two Italian immigrants on a streetcar in Brockton, a flight crew over Bishoftu, a sub-postmaster in a Surrey courtroom, a family separated under a misdemeanor statute, the named cause that was "the data," "the model," "the process," "the procedure." The substitution went underground into the morning headline, the press release, the docket number, the corporate apology, the version-number readme. The community no longer watched a public rite. The community read the official statement and moved on.

We have reached this point after twelve chapters in the position the ancient witness occupied: watching the rite. The next move, in the ancient rite, was the priest's. There is no priest here. There is a field guide.

The instrument is ours.

## Three reasons to leave it on the shelf

Three reasons are routinely given for why a diagnostic of this kind should live inside a volume — read once, admired, shelved — rather than walk out into working life. Each has a strong form. We set each at its strongest, then set the artefact next to it.

The first is that a serious analytic instrument requires expertise a general audience does not have. The eight questions are not a quiz; the four categories are not a Buzzfeed test; the responsibility chain is law-school work in disguise. Put crudely: leave it to the analysts. The diagnostic in untrained hands will be mechanical, miscalibrated, and confident in the wrong places.

The strong form of the objection is real and we do not deny it. The second-week law student who reaches for the term "war crime" before the procedural stage supports it produces exactly the kind of mis-attribution the diagnostic was built to interrupt. The diagnostic in untrained hands can produce its own laundering — a public verdict bolted onto a partial reading.

Our answer is structural and is built into the artefacts themselves. The six artefacts that follow are not a checklist. Each carries, on its face, a chapter pointer to the case at which the artefact was installed. When we find a cell unclear, a category contested, or a layer unfamiliar, we return to the worked example. The field guide is the portable reminder; the worked example is the discipline. The two travel together.

The second reason is that the diagnostic is for institutional actors — judges, inspectors general, parliamentary staffers, oversight-committee members — and translates poorly to the citizen reading the morning headline. The strong form: the eight questions name the analytic task of a public inquiry. The citizen is not running a public inquiry.

Our answer is that the citizen, the juror, the journalist, the employee, the manager, the contractor, the professional, and the voter each meet institutional events at a specific surface. The crosswalk — Artefact 5 — names that surface. The eight questions do not change at each surface; the action does. What the citizen does at the seam of an algorithmic denial is not what the voter does at the seam of an inquiry-statute amendment, and the crosswalk names the difference rather than collapsing it.

The third reason is that the diagnostic is its argument — that lifting the diagnostic out of its installing chapters strips it of the narrative architecture that made it intelligible. The strong form: anyone who reads only the field guide will arrive at the four categories without the cases that taught the categories, and will reach for the labels without the discipline of the chain. The diagnostic in checklist form becomes a slogan; the slogan becomes a substitute for the work.

This is our load-bearing counterargument, and it returns at full strength below. The structural answer is the same as before, scaled up: the artefacts do not travel alone. A by-chapter index — Artefact 6 — is the tether. Every artefact points back to its installing chapter. When we find the artefact insufficient for a specific case, we return to the worked example. The field guide is not a substitute for the installing chapters. The field guide is the diagnostic at the moment it leaves our hands and enters our life.

## Six artefacts, to be carried

The heaviest slot, and the deliverable. Six artefacts follow, each designed to be photocopied, folded, and carried.

### Artefact 1 — The eight questions

The diagnostic is verbatim from our project file. Read them in order. Write down the answers. Return to the question whose answer is "unknown."

> For every case ask:
>
> 1. Who or what was publicly blamed?
> 2. Who had control?
> 3. Who benefited?
> 4. Who knew or should have known?
> 5. Who could have prevented recurrence?
> 6. Who controlled the record?
> 7. Who bore the cost?
> 8. What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility?

How to use them. Take a single institutional event — a court filing, a news report, a corporate apology, an inquiry charter, an AI incident report, a contract clause. Walk the eight questions in order. Where the public account names a non-human verb-subject — the model, the system, the process, the institution — the answer to question 1 is the alibi container, and the answer to question 8 names the human chain the container displaced. The mismatch between the answers to question 1 and question 8 is the laundering's location. The mismatch is what the next five artefacts unpack.

The eight questions were installed in chapter 3 against the Volkswagen Dieselgate case and the Ford Pinto case. Both were partial scapegoats in which engineers were named while the executive chain that approved the design choice remained outside criminal exposure. We return to that worked example in chapter 3 when the questions do not yield a clean climb.

### Artefact 2 — The four-category taxonomy

The four categories are verbatim from rule `01-case-taxonomy.md` of our project file. They are mutually exclusive; the only allowed mixing is explicit declaration.

> **Pure scapegoat:** mostly innocent actor is blamed to close the case.
>
> **Partial scapegoat:** actor is involved or guilty at one level, but blame stops too low and protects a wider chain.
>
> **System/object alibi:** A tool, machine, algorithm, benchmark, corporation, legal entity, committee, market, or procedure becomes the named cause.
>
> **Cost-bearing goat:** victim or worker is not accused as guilty but absorbs harm while responsibility is avoided.

How to use them. After the eight-question walk, ask which category the laundering fits. The most diagnostic question per category is different. For pure scapegoat, the diagnostic is question 4 (who knew, and what did the documentary record show they knew about the named actor's non-involvement). For partial scapegoat, it is question 2 (who had control above the named actor). For system/object alibi, it is question 1 itself (the named cause is a thing, not a person). For cost-bearing goat, it is question 7 (the cost-bearer is named in the public account; the controller is not).

Hybrid cases exist. Chapter 9's AI training-data litigation cluster is named as system/object alibi (primary) plus cost-bearing goat (secondary): the laundering grammar renames human creative work as substance, and the rights-holders absorbing the cost are not visible at the narrative centre. The hybrid is declared rather than fudged. The taxonomy survives because every claim of "complicated" routes through an explicit second category.

The four categories were installed in chapter 2 against Therac-25 and Boeing 737 MAX (system/object alibi), Sacco and Vanzetti (pure scapegoat), Abu Ghraib military police (partial scapegoat), and Ukrainian children transferred from occupied territory (cost-bearing goat). We return to those worked examples in chapter 2 when the category is contested.

### Artefact 3 — The AI-stack three-layer diagnostic

Chapter 9 installed the AI-stack model alibi at three structurally distinct layers. The artefact lays them out in a single column.

> **Input layer.** The named verb-subject is "data," "the corpus," "the shadow library," "the market." The displaced human decisions are the named acquisition decisions: which corpora, on what dates, from what sources, under what licensing documentation. The diagnostic question is the acquisition record. The chapter-9 worked example is the *Bartz v. Anthropic* summary judgment of 23 June 2025, in which Judge Alsup distinguished lawfully acquired books from shadow-library copies on the basis of the acquisition record.
>
> **Deployment layer.** The named verb-subject is "the model," "the reward signal," "the release process." The displaced human decisions are the version designation, the post-training reward-shaping log, and the named individuals who approved the default-model swap. The diagnostic question is the post-training change record. The chapter-9 worked example is OpenAI's GPT-4o sycophancy post-mortem of 29 April 2025, which named the optimisation framework rather than any individual.
>
> **Evaluation layer.** The named verb-subject is "the variant," "the policy," "the benchmark." The displaced human decisions are the submission decision and the disclosure-policy interpretation. The diagnostic question is the benchmarked-versus-shipped variant gap. The chapter-9 worked example is the Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental submission to LMArena in April 2025 and LMArena's subsequent policy revision.

How to use them. Run the eight questions three times per AI system — once at each layer. The named human decision-maker is different at each layer. The displaced decision is different. The interception instrument is different. Subtract any layer and the alibi sustains at the unexamined floor.

### Artefact 4 — The five-layer war stack

Chapter 10 installed the war-laundering stack at five layers. The artefact lays them out in a single column.

> **Chain-of-command secrecy layer.** A wartime incident is publicly blamed on enlisted personnel or a low-rank unit while doctrine memos, theatre-commander authorisations, and interrogation-technique approvals remain classified during the operative window. The chapter-10 worked example is the Abu Ghraib chain: night-shift military police were court-martialled while the OLC memoranda of 1 August 2002 and the Sanchez authorisation of 14 September 2003 were declassified only after the operative window had closed.
>
> **Proxy-deniability layer.** Operations are conducted by entities with deniable legal status — armed groups, people's republics, local forces, irregulars — while sovereign-state command of supply, training, and recovery is publicly denied. The chapter-10 worked example is MH17: the Buk-TELAR belonged to the Russian 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade; the public framing attributed the shootdown to local armed groups or to Ukrainian forces.
>
> **Contractor legal-status-shell layer.** Private military, private surveillance, private interrogation, and private logistics operate under contractual arrangements that exclude criminal jurisdiction and are described as administrative convenience rather than as accountability architecture. The chapter-10 worked example is Blackwater / Nisour Square: Coalition Provisional Authority Order 17 excluded Iraqi criminal jurisdiction; the State Department contract insulated the corporate principal.
>
> **Civilian-invisibility-through-euphemism layer.** Cost-bearers — civilians, refugees, transferred children, displaced populations — are described in care-language ("evacuation," "guardianship," "humanitarian relocation," "rescue from a war zone") while the underlying conduct continues. The chapter-10 worked example is the Russian transfer of Ukrainian children under Presidential Decrees No. 330 and No. 585 of 2022.
>
> **Strategic-justification layer.** A national institutional product — a national intelligence estimate, an expert assessment, a model output — is presented in public with hedges stripped, and the body that audits the product is barred by charter from examining its policy use. The chapter-10 worked example is the Iraq WMD National Intelligence Estimate of October 2002, presented as certainty at the UN Security Council, and the Robb-Silberman Commission's Executive Order 13328 charter that excluded examination of policy use.

How to use them. Ask which layers are firing simultaneously. Where three or more of the five are present, the case is structurally laundering-shaped, and the diagnostic applies. Treat any claim that responsibility in war is irrecoverable as the laundering mechanism itself.

### Artefact 5 — The role-by-domain crosswalk

The crosswalk is our largest artefact. Rows are roles. Columns are six anti-laundering moves we have installed across chapters 2 through 12. Each cell names one specific move the named role can execute in the named domain. Where the same move applies across multiple cells, the cell points across to the shared move rather than restating it.

The six anti-laundering moves (columns), in installation order:

> **Move 1 — Name the controller.** When a procedure or institution is cited as cause, ask in writing which named person made the decision the procedure produced. (Chapters 2, 3, 5, 6.)
>
> **Move 2 — Demand the acquisition or decision record.** Ask for the document — the manifest, the email chain, the approvals log, the Statement of Responsibilities — that shows who decided what, on what date, under what alternative available to them. (Chapters 7, 10, 12.)
>
> **Move 3 — Refuse the signature without the chain.** Refuse the named-signer position for a function whose written allocation, sub-delegations, and escalation paths have not been documented. (Chapters 6, 7, 11.)
>
> **Move 4 — Make the destruction or classification visible.** Where records are destroyed, classified, sealed, or deleted, demand the destruction log, the classification basis, or the retention exception in writing. (Chapters 9, 10, 12.)
>
> **Move 5 — Apply the design test before signing the design.** When proposing, signing, or advocating an institutional design, ask whether a named individual bears the consequence of the control question failing at each layer the design touches. (Chapter 11.)
>
> **Move 6 — Vote and advocate for the compelling architecture.** Where the design choice is structural — inquiry statutes, oversight charters, AI disclosure regimes, certification regimes, FOIA reforms — choose the architecture that grants compulsory-evidence access over the architecture that fences it. (Chapters 8, 11, 12.)

The Inquiries Act 2005 §21 model is one available shape; the Robb-Silberman Executive Order 13328 model is another. We do not need a verdict on which shape is better in every jurisdiction. We need to know which shape is on the ballot.

## The chord behind the instrument

The older echo here is the work behind us. The twelve installments this field guide returns to are the chord on which the field guide rests, and we name the chord here so anyone who picks up the field guide first can locate the worked examples.

Chapter 1 named the rite. Chapter 2 named the four shapes. Chapter 3 named the eight questions. Part II — chapters 4, 5, 6, 7 — stress-tested the diagnostic against the proxy, the partial scapegoat at the corporate parent, the administrative-law pretext, and the institution that controls the record. Part III — chapters 8, 9, 10 — stress-tested the diagnostic against the three domains that fight it hardest: war, partisan inversion, and the AI stack. Part IV — chapters 11 and 12 — turned the diagnostic forward into a design rule and a record-discipline triplet.

The field guide is not a thirteenth chapter that adds new mechanism. It is the consolidation pass. Anyone who has not read the twelve chapters reads the field guide and decides which chapter to open first. Anyone who has read them carries the field guide as a portable reminder.

The chord belongs to the twelve chapters, not to the consolidation. The field guide is the chord's printed form.

The field guide introduces no new citation. Where the artefact summarises a case, the originating chapter's case file and source ledger are the authoritative record.

The instrument's trustworthiness is the discipline of the twelve installments it consolidates.

The reversal: the field guide is not a flattening of the diagnostic. The field guide is the diagnostic at the moment it leaves our hands.

## The risk of the checklist

The strongest objection to a field guide of this shape comes from anyone who has finished the twelve chapters and is now being asked to carry six artefacts out into Monday morning.

The objection runs as follows. Field guides flatten the diagnostic into bullet points and lose the structural argument that gave the diagnostic its force. The eight questions on one artefact separated from their cases become a checklist; the four categories on one artefact separated from their installing chapter become labels; anyone who reads only the field guide will apply the diagnostic mechanically and reach wrong answers. Chapter 3 itself flagged this risk inside its own walk — the diagnostic-as-checklist risk. The risk is real at chapter scale. Across twelve chapters it is greater. A field guide that strips the cases from the artefacts converts a diagnostic into a slogan, and the slogan becomes the substitute for the work the twelve chapters asked us to do.

The strong form of the objection has historical evidence on its side. Diagnostic taxonomies that travel without their cases have been weaponised in every direction. "Scapegoat" is now a one-word counter-accusation that does no analytic work and licenses the user to dismiss any contested attribution. "Witch hunt" is its mirror form. The DSM categories travel without their case histories and produce mechanical clinical judgements. Logical fallacies as memes travel without their reasoning context and produce a generation of internet debate that names the fallacy and stops. Each is a diagnostic instrument that became a checklist and then a label. We are right to worry that the field guide on the next photocopy could become the next instance.

We do not deny the risk. We answer structurally. The six artefacts above are not a checklist; each is tethered, by typography and by explicit pointer, to the worked example that installed it. The by-chapter index is the tether. When we find an artefact insufficient for a specific case, we return through the index to the worked example. The artefact is a portable reminder; the worked example is the discipline. The two travel together. Whoever carries only the artefacts will produce shallower diagnostic work than whoever carries both. Whoever carries neither will produce no diagnostic work at all. The field guide raises the floor without raising the ceiling.

The second answer is harder. The diagnostic was never the property of the analyst. We have been arguing for twelve chapters that the laundering depends on the asymmetry between the institutional actor with control and us without it; the diagnostic was assembled, in part, to redistribute that asymmetry. A diagnostic that stays inside the twelve chapters is a diagnostic that has accepted the asymmetry. The risk that the diagnostic in untrained hands will be applied mechanically is real and is smaller than the risk that the diagnostic in trained hands will be applied to the cases the trained hands already control. The field guide accepts the first risk in order to lower the second.

The third answer is the exit move, which we take up below.

## The letter that names the model

For twelve chapters we have occupied the seat of the observer. The earlier chapters walked documented intercepts of laundering attempts: the *Cour de Cassation*'s 1906 quash-without-remand of the Dreyfus conviction; the Knapp Commission's subpoena-powered hearings on the NYPD pad system; Mr Justice Fraser's *Horizon Issues* judgment; the Joint Investigation Team's serial-equivalent identification of the Buk-TELAR; Sabraw J's class action ordering reunification of separated families; the SCOTUS APA review of the census citizenship question; Chilcot's compelled access to UK Cabinet papers; the SCOTUS per curiams of April 2025 in *Trump v. J.G.G.* and *Noem v. Abrego Garcia*; the Pentagon Papers publication; Walsh's reconstruction of the destroyed Poindexter notes; Judge Alsup's distinction between lawfully acquired and shadow-library training corpora. Each intercept required an instrument the earlier chapters named. None of the intercepts arrived by itself.

In every named intercept, the intercepting actor was at first a person who noticed something. Picquart noticed that the bordereau handwriting matched Esterhazy's, not Dreyfus's. Serpico noticed that the precinct pad ran on the assumption that he would take. Alan Bates noticed that no other sub-postmaster could possibly have been doing what the Post Office's prosecutors said only he was doing. Hans Blix noticed that UNMOVIC's site visits were finding nothing. The intercepts started before the inquiries existed.

The diagnostic we now hold is the formalisation of what those persons noticed. Our next intercept will not appear in a future edition. It will appear in our working life, in whatever role we hold.

What follows walks one specific shape of next intercept, in the second person, because the shape is most likely to find us in the working life of 2026 and the years after. The shape is the algorithmic denial.

Imagine you receive a letter. The letter cites a model — your bank's credit-risk model; your employer's screening tool; your insurer's pricing engine; a platform's content-moderation classifier; a government agency's eligibility filter. The letter does not name the model's version. The letter does not name the training corpora. The letter does not name the post-training reward-shaping changes that occurred since the last release. The letter names you and an outcome adverse to you. The verb of the letter is *the model decided*. The subject of the verb is the thing.

The eight questions, in order. Who was publicly blamed? You — the addressee of the letter, the bearer of the denial. Who had control? The institution that deployed the model. Who benefited? The institution that deployed the model, in the form of an automated decision at scale. Who knew or should have known? The named human decision-makers at the deployer — the product manager, the risk-committee chair, the model-deployment approver. Who could have prevented? The same. Who controlled the record? The deployer, at all three of chapter 9's layers — the training-corpus manifest at the input layer; the version designation and reward-shaping log at the deployment layer; the procurement-document benchmark gap at the evaluation layer. Who bore the cost? You. What would responsibility look like if it followed control instead of visibility? The chain from the field office that sent the letter, through the operations director who runs the model in production, to the executive who approved the deployment.

You write back. You ask the three-record demand from chapter 9: input-layer (which corpora, on what dates, under what licensing), deployment-layer (which model version, what post-training changes, what appeal path that does not route back to the same model), evaluation-layer (whether the deployed model matches the model whose benchmark scores were cited in any marketing or procurement document supporting the deployment).

The institution may answer. The institution may not. The crosswalk tells you what to do next according to the role you hold. As cost-bearer, you document the denial and the response; the documentation is the record on which a future intercept can rest. As employee — if the model is your employer's — you raise the Statement of Responsibilities question through whatever channel does not route back to the same model. As contractor — if you contract with the deployer — you propose, in writing, the named-attribution clauses the contract did not contain. As professional, you refuse the certification, opinion, or attestation that depends on the institution's product whose record you cannot inspect. As citizen and voter, you submit comment on the AI-disclosure rulemaking the European AI Act and California's SB-942 have begun and the analogous regimes in your jurisdiction have not yet.

The walk above is a shape, not a case. It is hypothetical here by design. The discipline forbids fabricating the specifics of an event that did not happen to a real person. The shape, however, is documented in every direction — the *Bartz* acquisition record at the input layer; the GPT-4o sycophancy post-mortem at the deployment layer; the Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental submission at the evaluation layer; chapter 7's Horizon five-role conflation that the AI stack reproduces; chapter 11's design rule that the deployer's design did not satisfy; chapter 12's record-discipline triplet the deployer's record did not survive. Whoever reaches the moment the letter arrives meets the earlier chapters at the same time. We carry the field guide into the moment.

The alibi-collapse beat is the moment we recognise the diagnostic operating in our own domain. The diagnostic does not collapse. The alibi the institution sent in the letter collapses. The collapse is partial; the collapse is costly; the collapse is documented. The collapse is real.

## Walk the diagnostic before the verdict

The portable rule, stated as protocol:

> Carry the eight questions. Carry the four categories. Carry the three AI-stack layers and the five war-stack layers. Carry the role-by-domain crosswalk. Carry the by-chapter index. When you meet an institutional event that calls for a verdict, walk the diagnostic before forming the verdict. When the diagnostic exposes a gap between the public chain and the responsibility chain, the gap is the chapter already written for you to read.

The rule is portable across the cases installed and the cases not. It applies whether the event is a court filing, a corporate apology, an inquiry charter, an AI incident report, a contract clause, a news framing of a recent institutional event, or an HR investigation in which we are the named party. The rule does not pre-determine the verdict. The rule produces the walk on which the verdict will rest.

This is our one use of the term that names the diagnostic. Responsibility laundering is the recurring institutional method by which power keeps control or benefit while moving blame, liability, moral cost, or public suspicion onto a weaker bearer, procedural shell, proxy, machine, market, committee, legal entity, or record. The diagnostic we now hold is the portable counter-method.

## We are the escape

We are the escape.

This is the exit move. For twelve chapters we have been the observer of intercepts. The thirteenth pass inverts the position. The intercepts were performed by people. The intercepts were named — Picquart, Serpico, Alan Bates, Sabraw J, Mr Justice Fraser, the Joint Investigation Team, Chilcot, Walsh, Alsup. Each began before the inquiry existed. Each began with a person who noticed something and refused to let the noticing close.

The diagnostic in the preceding six artefacts is what the noticing looks like in formal dress. We have been arguing throughout that the diagnostic is not the property of the analyst or the inquiry. The diagnostic is the formalisation of the noticing. Picquart's bordereau comparison was the diagnostic walked through document layer. Serpico's documentation of the pad was the diagnostic walked through institutional layer. Bates's identification of the dispersed Horizon prosecutions was the diagnostic walked through the system/object alibi. Each intercept, in its operative moment, was the diagnostic running inside one person who refused the public account. The diagnostic followed, in the form the earlier chapters retrieved it from the public record.

Our next intercept will not have a name yet. It will appear in the institutional event we meet next — the morning headline, the press release, the docket number, the corporate apology, the version-number readme, the algorithmic denial, the contract clause, the HR investigation, the inquiry charter draft, the AI disclosure rulemaking. We meet the event with the field guide. The field guide is the formalisation. We are the actor.

We owe an honest reckoning with the cost.

Most applications produce partial intercepts. Picquart was reassigned to Tunisia and later imprisoned. Serpico was shot in the face at 778 Driggs Avenue. Bates spent an unpaid decade running the Justice for Subpostmasters Alliance. Ellsberg faced indictment under the Espionage Act. Wilkerson's career did not advance after Powell's departure. Drumheller's role at CIA ended. The Knight-Ridder reporters did not enter the major-paper consensus. Stephanie Hofeller Lizon was estranged from her father before his death made the Hofeller files available. Career line prosecutors of the January 6 docket filed Office of Special Counsel and Merit Systems Protection Board complaints whose dispositions are pending. The intercepts that produced our worked examples cost the intercepting actors something we have not euphemised.

Most intercepts do not deliver remedy in the operative window. Chilcot took seven years and produced no prosecutions. The Hague convicted three MH17 perpetrators in absentia who have not been delivered. The ICC has issued arrest warrants on the Ukrainian-children case it cannot execute in non-recognising jurisdictions. The *Bartz* settlement of $1.5 billion is the cost of the conduct for one defendant on one corpus subset; it is not the price of restoring the laundered architecture to a non-laundering one. The pardons of December 2020 reversed the Nisour Square convictions. The Office of the Special Prosecutor for Anti-Corruption in New York was defunded in 1990, twenty years after Knapp had built it. The pattern Knapp documented at the NYPD recurred under Mollen within twenty years. The institutional architecture that produced each intercept is, in each case, more fragile than the laundering it intercepted.

Most cost-bearers cannot wait for the intercept to land. The sub-postmasters at Horizon — at least four of whose suicides are documented as connected to the prosecutions — did not live to be exonerated. Many Stolen Generations survivors did not live to the National Apology. Most children transferred under Russian Decree No. 330 and Decree No. 585 have not been returned. The Iraqi civilians of the Iraq Body Count's documented floor through 2011 — approximately 100,000 to 110,000 deaths — are not waiting for Chilcot's record. Voyne Ray Cox died in September 1986. Seema Misra was sentenced eight weeks pregnant in 2010 and exonerated eleven years later. The diagnostic does not undo the cost. The diagnostic produces the record on which future remedy may or may not rest.

To carry the diagnostic and apply it is to accept these costs as the floor of the work. We do not pretend they are not real. We argue that the alternative — accepting the institutional verb-subject as the cause, accepting "the model decided" or "the system flagged" or "the process was followed" or "the regulation required it" — is a higher floor of cost, paid by a more diffuse population, distributed over a longer interval, and recorded nowhere.

We are the escape because the escape is not a single act. The escape is a population of acts: the FOIA request filed at the seam, the contemporaneous record kept against the institution's retention policy, the refused signature on the unallocated function, the contract clause proposed for the second draft, the comment submitted on the rulemaking, the inquiry-charter amendment voted up at the council meeting, the news story that named the human chain the press release displaced. None of the acts is the priest's gesture in the ancient rite. The cumulative effect of the acts is the modern equivalent of the watching community. The community had stopped watching. The diagnostic is how the watching resumes.

The diagnostic is handed across.

When the publicly named cause of a harm differs from the named decision-maker who held control, the diagnostic should run. The marker is a procedure cited without a named person; a name without a named function; a record held by one party to the dispute; or a destruction announcement without an inventory.

#### Act on it.

Eight sentences. One per reader role.

> 1. As a citizen, you ask which named function-holder controlled the decision the procedure produced.
> 2. As a juror, you ask whether the record presented to you was held by a single party to the dispute.
> 3. As a journalist, you ask whether the destruction log exists.
> 4. As an employee, you write the decision and your signature on the day.
> 5. As a manager, you refuse to sign for an outcome whose Statement of Responsibilities you have not seen.
> 6. As a contractor, you insist on multiple custodians of the consequential record.
> 7. As a professional, you ask which sub-delegation document authorises the decision you are asked to certify.
> 8. As a voter, you ask whether the inquiry statute gives the inquiry chair compulsory-evidence power.

#### Avoid becoming it.

The goat is whoever signs for an outcome whose decisions, records, and beneficiaries they do not control.

The altar moves. The questions stay.

*No one did it* was never a fact. It was a seam — the place where a name had been lifted out of the sentence. We put names back.
