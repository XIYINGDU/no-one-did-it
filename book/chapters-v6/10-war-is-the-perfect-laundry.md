# Chapter 10 — War Is the Perfect Laundry

> All warfare is based on deception.
>
> — Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* I.18, trans. Lionel Giles (1910)

On 5 February 2003, in the Security Council chamber at the United Nations, Secretary of State Colin Powell held up a small glass vial. The vial contained simulated anthrax. He held it between thumb and forefinger, near eye level, long enough for the cameras to find it. He said: "Less than a teaspoon full of dry anthrax in an envelope shut down the United States Senate in the fall of 2001."[<sup>1</sup>](#c10-1).

The vial was a prop. It carried no live agent. It stood in for the case Powell had come to make: that Iraq possessed an active stockpile of biological and chemical weapons, was reconstituting its nuclear weapons programme, and had an operational relationship with al-Qaeda. Each of the three strands had its own evidentiary spine. The mobile bioweapons laboratories — the load-bearing image of the speech — came from a single Iraqi defector, code-named Curveball, who had been debriefed not by the CIA but by Germany's foreign intelligence service, the BND[<sup>2</sup>](#c10-2). The aluminum tubes, presented as centrifuge components for uranium enrichment, were contested inside the US intelligence community, with the State Department's Bureau of Intelligence and Research recording its dissent in text boxes set off from the main assessment and in Annex A[<sup>3</sup>](#c10-3). The Atta-Prague meeting, presented as evidence of operational ties between Iraq and al-Qaeda, was promoted to the speech through the Pentagon's Office of Special Plans rather than the standard intelligence-community vetting line[<sup>4</sup>](#c10-4).

Powell spoke for approximately seventy-five minutes. The presentation had been prepared over four days at CIA headquarters in Langley, with Powell and his chief of staff, Lawrence Wilkerson, working from a draft assembled by the Office of the Vice President's national-security staff and the National Security Council. Wilkerson has subsequently described that drafting in public statements: material was, he said, "pushed" into the speech from non-INR sources over INR objections[<sup>5</sup>](#c10-5).

The vial mattered because the vial was simulated. We return to that fact below. For now it stands as the visible centre of an evidentiary case that, eighteen months later, had collapsed.

The invasion began six weeks after the speech. By the end of 2004, the Iraq Survey Group — established by the CIA and led by Charles Duelfer — had concluded that Iraq's WMD programmes had been effectively dismantled by 1991 sanctions; no stockpiles existed; no reconstitution was underway[<sup>6</sup>](#c10-6). The image at the centre of Powell's speech had no physical referent. The vial had been simulated all along.

## The intelligence was wrong

The phrase arrived quickly. By the autumn of 2003 it was a Washington commonplace; by the autumn of 2005 it was the explicit framing of the administration that had launched the war.

On 14 December 2005, at the Woodrow Wilson International Center for Scholars, President George W. Bush addressed the question directly. "It is true that much of the intelligence turned out to be wrong," he said[<sup>7</sup>](#c10-7). The line was repeated, in variations, across years of subsequent statements. The British government's parallel framing followed the same shape: Prime Minister Tony Blair characterised the British case as having rested on intelligence assessments he had taken as established and which had proved less established than they had appeared.

The framing performs a specific operation. It names a category — "the intelligence" — and assigns to that category the property that caused the collapse: it was wrong. The category is institutional. The intelligence community is an entity composed of agencies, analysts, products, and processes. The framing treats this entity as a single actor with a property. The intelligence community produced an assessment; the assessment was wrong; the war proceeded on the basis of the wrong assessment; the responsibility lies with the producing entity.

This framing has the shape of a confession. It accepts that something went wrong. It assigns the going-wrong to a nameable place. It does not assign the going-wrong to the person reciting the confession.

Chapter 8 showed institutions blaming themselves to narrow the diagnosis: the institution names itself as the failed component, the inquiry is fenced to that component, the case closes inside the fence. "The intelligence was wrong" is the same move at strategic scale. The executive branch named the intelligence community as the failed component. The first commission convened to investigate the failure — the Commission on the Intelligence Capabilities of the United States Regarding Weapons of Mass Destruction, commonly called Robb-Silberman after its co-chairs — was chartered by Executive Order 13328 on 6 February 2004. The order explicitly excluded examination of how policy-makers had used the intelligence[<sup>8</sup>](#c10-8). The fence was drawn before the inquiry began. The charter, written by the actor under examination, set the commission to audit how the intelligence had been collected, analysed, and disseminated, and gave it no authority to ask whether anyone had read the result.

Three sub-claims sat inside the strategic case, and each was subsequently found by formal inquiry to have exceeded what the underlying intelligence supported. First: Iraq possessed chemical and biological stockpiles. The 1 October 2002 National Intelligence Estimate had asserted this with "high confidence"[<sup>9</sup>](#c10-9); the Duelfer Report falsified it[<sup>10</sup>](#c10-10). Second: Iraq was reconstituting its nuclear weapons programme. The aluminum tubes were the headline evidence; the Niger yellowcake documents the secondary. At the Security Council on 7 March 2003, IAEA Director General Mohamed ElBaradei declared the Niger documents forgeries: "based on a thorough analysis, the IAEA has concluded, with the concurrence of outside experts, that these documents… are in fact not authentic"[<sup>11</sup>](#c10-11). The invasion began twelve days later. Third: Iraq had an operational relationship with al-Qaeda; the Atta-Prague meeting was the documentary node. The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's Phase II report, released on 25 May 2007, concluded that public statements on Iraq-al-Qaeda ties had exceeded the intelligence record[<sup>12</sup>](#c10-12).

Each of the three sub-claims was thus contested inside the intelligence record at the time it was made, then formally found to have exceeded the record after the war. The framing "the intelligence was wrong" treats the contestation and the post-hoc finding as a single property of a single actor. The framing is not a falsification. The framing is a redescription. It moves the question — was the public case warranted by the underlying assessments — into a different question — was the underlying intelligence right. The two questions are not the same. The diagnostic we have been installing across previous chapters distinguishes them.

## Eight questions inside the war

The case has the standard shape: an apparent cause (the intelligence community), a public attribution (it got it wrong), and a residual question (then why did the war happen). The same eight questions from chapter 3 — blame, control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, cost, and what responsibility looks like if it followed control instead of visibility — answer the residual question here, applied to the war. Walking them through Iraq WMD locates the answers in places the "intelligence was wrong" framing does not point to.

Where the war case bends the instrument hardest is at control, benefit, and knowledge, because each names a war-specific architecture the peacetime cases did not contain; the rest run as they did in chapter 3, only as far as the war case carries them. The walk is full — all eight.

#### Who was publicly blamed.

The intelligence community as an institution. CIA Director George Tenet personally, via his reported remark to the President in December 2002 that the WMD case was "a slam dunk" — a remark sourced through Bob Woodward's *Plan of Attack* and contested in Tenet's own subsequent account[<sup>13</sup>](#c10-13). Curveball / Rafid Ahmed Alwan al-Janabi as a late-stage fabricator, after his identification by the Robb-Silberman Commission in 2005 and his public self-identification in 2011[<sup>14</sup>](#c10-14). Specific analytic offices within the CIA, DIA, and INR, named in the Robb-Silberman tradecraft critique[<sup>15</sup>](#c10-15). The blame distributed across an institutional category, a senior official's reported informal remark, a defector, and a set of line analysts.

#### Who had control.

Control over the public WMD case sat in three rooms: the White House Iraq Group (WHIG), a communications coordination body formed in August 2002 to shape the public case, whose existence was reported by *The New York Times* on 7 September 2002[<sup>16</sup>](#c10-16); the Office of the Vice President, where Vice President Richard Cheney (who died in 2024) and his chief of staff I. Lewis Libby ran the policy coordination; and 10 Downing Street, where Blair, his director of communications Alastair Campbell, and Joint Intelligence Committee chair John Scarlett worked on the parallel UK case. Control over the analytic product sat with Tenet and Deputy Director John McLaughlin at CIA, and with the National Intelligence Council, whose Chairman Robert L. Hutchings oversaw the NIE produced on a three-week timeline in September 2002[<sup>17</sup>](#c10-17). Control over the final UN presentation text sat with Powell (who died in 2021) and Wilkerson, working from a draft Wilkerson has stated had material pushed into it from OVP and NSC sources over INR objections[<sup>18</sup>](#c10-18).

#### Who benefited.

The framing produced UN Security Council Resolution 1441 on 8 November 2002[<sup>19</sup>](#c10-19); the Authorization for Use of Military Force Against Iraq, P.L. 107-243, on 16 October 2002[<sup>20</sup>](#c10-20); and the domestic political coalition for the war. Career outcomes are documentary. Tenet resigned on 3 June 2004 and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom on 14 December 2004[<sup>21</sup>](#c10-21). Condoleezza Rice was promoted to Secretary of State on 26 January 2005. Stephen Hadley succeeded her as National Security Adviser. Douglas Feith remained Under Secretary of Defense for Policy until his departure in August 2005. None of the senior officials named in the SSCI Phase II report or the Chilcot Inquiry faced criminal process on this record.

#### Who had documented knowledge, per inquiry findings.

SSCI Phase II concluded that public statements on Iraq-al-Qaeda ties and the Atta-Prague meeting exceeded the intelligence record[<sup>22</sup>](#c10-22). The Department of Defense Inspector General's report on the Office of Special Plans found that Feith's office had presented conclusions in IC-vetted form when they were not IC-vetted; the report found that the work "did not provide 'the most accurate analysis of intelligence to senior decisionmakers'" and that the conclusions were "inappropriate"[<sup>23</sup>](#c10-23). Former CIA European Division chief Tyler Drumheller has stated, in Senate testimony and in his 2006 book *On the Brink*, that a German BND liaison warned Tenet personally that Curveball should not be considered reliable for public statements; Tenet has disputed whether the warning reached him before the Powell speech[<sup>24</sup>](#c10-24). The Chilcot Inquiry found that the assessments and judgments underlying the September 2002 Dossier were presented with a "certainty that was not justified," that the Joint Intelligence Committee should have made clear to Mr Blair that the assessed intelligence had not established beyond doubt that Iraq had continued to produce chemical and biological weapons, and that the process by which the Dossier was prepared blurred the line between assessment and policy presentation[<sup>25</sup>](#c10-25).

#### Who could have prevented recurrence.

This question runs as it did in chapter 3: it asks where, in the documented record, the chain had a discount opportunity it did not take. The war case supplies a sequence of them. The Drumheller-route Curveball warning, if received as Drumheller has stated. INR's objections to the aluminum tubes and the Niger claim, set off from the Key Judgments — in text boxes and Annex A — rather than carried as headline dissents[<sup>26</sup>](#c10-26). ElBaradei's 7 March 2003 finding that the Niger documents were forgeries, on the public record before the invasion. Hans Blix's UNMOVIC reports through early 2003, finding cooperation increasing and no stockpiles located[<sup>27</sup>](#c10-27). Each was a prevention window. The invasion proceeded on 19 March 2003 through all of them.

#### Who controlled the record.

Initially the executive branch, in three forms: the classification regime over the underlying intelligence reporting (the October 2002 NIE was declassified in heavily redacted form only on 18 July 2003); the WHIG synchronisation of public statements; the structural advantage that strategic-justification inquiries operate after the war they would adjudicate, while record-hardening operates during the precondition-shaping window. The decisive record breaks came from outside the executive: SSCI Phase I in July 2004; the Robb-Silberman Commission in March 2005, even within its narrowed charter; SSCI Phase II's two components in September 2006 and May 2007; the DoD Inspector General's report on OSP in February 2007; and the Chilcot Inquiry in July 2016. Investigative journalism contributed before any of the formal inquiries reported: Warren Strobel and Jonathan Landay at Knight-Ridder filed dissenting pre-invasion coverage that did not enter the major-paper consensus narrative[<sup>28</sup>](#c10-28).

#### Who bore the cost.

Iraqi civilians, first.

The Iraq Body Count project, which records documented civilian deaths from violence based on cross-referenced media and official sources, recorded approximately 100,000 to 110,000 documented civilian deaths from 2003 to 2011[<sup>29</sup>](#c10-29). Survey-based estimates ran higher. The Roberts et al. household survey published in *The Lancet* on 11 October 2006 estimated approximately 654,000 excess deaths through June 2006[<sup>30</sup>](#c10-30). The Iraq Family Health Survey, published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 31 January 2008, estimated approximately 151,000 violent deaths from March 2003 to June 2006[<sup>31</sup>](#c10-31). We report the Iraq Body Count figure as documented floor and flag the methodology dispute on the survey-based estimates without averaging across methodologies that do not share priors.

Military deaths: per US Department of Defense casualty figures, approximately 4,491 US service members across Operation Iraqi Freedom and Operation New Dawn through 2011[<sup>32</sup>](#c10-32); per UK Ministry of Defence figures, 179 UK service members[<sup>33</sup>](#c10-33). Displacement: the UN High Commissioner for Refugees recorded approximately two million Iraqis displaced internally and approximately two million as refugees by 2007[<sup>34</sup>](#c10-34). Iraq's institutional capacity, infrastructure, and historical archive — including the looting of the Iraq Museum in April 2003 and the de-Baathification of the professional civil service — were costs that fell on the population over decades.

Curveball, the pure-scapegoat node, absorbed the late-stage attribution of the bioweapons claim. The chain that had multiple opportunities to discount his account did not absorb its share.

#### What responsibility would look like if it followed control.

The senior actors who controlled the WHIG communications architecture, the OVP and NSC drafting process for Powell's speech, the OSP alternative-intelligence channel, the compressed-timeline NIE production, and the No. 10 dossier drafting would face individual accountability. Criminal accountability where the statute and jurisdiction reach. Public-record accountability where they do not. Statutory inquiry where the legal route is blocked. None has, on this record, as of mid-2026.

The walk yields a structural finding the framing "the intelligence was wrong" obscures. The walk located control in the executive branch and 10 Downing Street; the knowledge in the inquiry record; the cost in the population. It located the failure in the gap between the product and the public statements. "The intelligence was wrong" names none of these places. It names the one actor the walk found least able to set the public certainty — and assigns it the property that caused the collapse.

Return to the vial. It was a prop; it carried no live agent; everyone in the chamber knew it was simulated. The certainty around it was the same kind of object — a prop standing in for a case the inquiry record later found had been presented with, in Chilcot's words, "a certainty that was not justified." The vial held nothing. The walk locates the gap between the staged certainty and the hedged product in the rooms it just named, not in the analysts the public framing blamed.

Chapter 9 showed the model becoming the named cause. Here the model is the National Intelligence Estimate, and the same reification operates: an institutional product is treated as if it had agency, while the policy choice to commission it on a three-week timeline and to present its hedged language with "high confidence" framing is treated as background noise. The NIE became the war's model. The hedges inside it — including the State Department's INR dissent on the aluminum tubes and the nuclear claim, set off in a boxed "Alternative View" with its own text boxes and an Annex A — lived in the margins while the certainty lived in the Key Judgments. The boxed dissent was not in the speech.

## Five layers, four callbacks

War launders responsibility through five layers, and only one of them is new here. Across the earlier chapters we installed four laundering patterns; war fires all four at once, plus a fifth — strategic justification — installed here through the Iraq WMD anchor. Layer 1 is the one just installed at the case level: the NIE as institutional product, the public certainty exceeding the product's hedges, the audit body fenced from policy use. Layers 2 through 5 are callbacks — patterns already installed earlier, re-firing in war. Re-walking them is not what we are here for. What we are here for is the demonstration that the four prior patterns and the strategic-justification pattern fire simultaneously in war, and that the diagnostic resolves each by climbing into the specific chain layer by layer. Each callback below pulls only the layer-observation new to the present argument.

### The chain-of-command layer — Abu Ghraib

Abu Ghraib entered the argument in chapter 2 as a partial scapegoat. The night-shift military police of the 372nd MP Company were genuinely culpable; eleven enlisted soldiers and one brigadier general were sanctioned. From the 372nd MP Company: Specialist Charles Graner, Private Lynndie England, Staff Sergeant Ivan Frederick II, Sergeant Javal Davis, Specialist Sabrina Harman, Specialist Megan Ambuhl, and Specialist Jeremy Sivits were each court-martialled, with dispositions ranging from pleas to contested convictions and sentences from administrative discharge to ten years' confinement. From the military-intelligence side, Specialist Armin Cruz of the 325th Military Intelligence Battalion was separately court-martialled on assault and conspiracy charges arising from interrogation conduct at the prison. Brigadier General Janis Karpinski, commander of the 800th MP Brigade, was demoted to colonel and relieved of command in May 2005[<sup>35</sup>](#c10-35). The laundering operated by stopping the chain at those convictions and that demotion. Above them sat the Office of Legal Counsel memoranda of 1 August 2002 (Bybee and Yoo) authorising coercive interrogation techniques; the 2 December 2002 Rumsfeld approval of those techniques for Guantanamo; the 16 April 2003 expansion; and Lieutenant General Ricardo Sanchez's 14 September 2003 authorisation that migrated Guantanamo-style techniques into Iraq[<sup>36</sup>](#c10-36).

War adds a chain-of-command secrecy layer to the partial-scapegoat pattern. The distinguishing mark is the boundary: the chain stops not because the evidence runs out but because it crosses a classification line above which the public record cannot reach during the operative window. The Taguba Report of March 2004, the Schlesinger Report of August 2004, the Fay-Jones Report of August 2004, and the Senate Armed Services Committee report on detainee treatment of 20 November 2008 each pierced one layer; none reached adjudicated criminal liability above the rank of staff sergeant[<sup>37</sup>](#c10-37). The Knapp Commission and Serpico counter-case installed in chapter 2 against the "bad apples" framing applies here at strategic scale: institutional resistance to chain-stopping has documented historical precedent. The chain-of-command secrecy layer is what makes the precedent harder to apply in wartime.

A second feature of the chain explains why it stops where it does, independent of classification. The legal architecture of the order-following obligation places the determination of an order's lawfulness on the individual at the bottom of the chain. Rome Statute Article 33 provides that superior orders are a defence to war crimes only where the order was not manifestly unlawful, the subordinate was under a legal obligation to obey, and did not know the order was unlawful; Article 33(2) declares that orders to commit genocide or crimes against humanity are manifestly unlawful as a matter of law. The US Uniform Code of Military Justice carries a parallel doctrine: lawful orders must be obeyed; manifestly illegal orders are not a defence, and obedience to a manifestly illegal order is itself an offence. The architecture cuts in one direction at the seam — it makes the enlisted soldier answerable for executing a manifestly unlawful order — without, by its own terms, carrying liability upward to whoever issued the doctrine the order rested on. That asymmetry is part of why the staff-sergeant-level dispositions could close the case while the authorisation memoranda above them stayed unadjudicated.

### The proxy layer — MH17

The 17 July 2014 destruction of Malaysia Airlines flight MH17 was read in chapter 4 as a partial scapegoat with a system / object alibi secondary across a state border. The Joint Investigation Team led by the Dutch Public Prosecution Service established that the Buk-TELAR missile launcher that fired the missile belonged to the Russian Federation's 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade based in Kursk, and traced the launcher's outbound and inbound paths through Russian territory[<sup>38</sup>](#c10-38). Three individuals — Igor Girkin, Sergey Dubinskiy, and Leonid Kharchenko — were convicted in absentia at The Hague District Court at Schiphol on 17 November 2022 for the murder of all 298 persons aboard[<sup>39</sup>](#c10-39). President Vladimir Putin has publicly denied Russian state involvement in the launcher's supply chain, including in remarks at a press conference on 21 July 2014[<sup>40</sup>](#c10-40). The JIT's 8 February 2023 update concluded there were "strong indications" that President Putin had personally approved the provision of the Buk system to the armed groups in eastern Ukraine[<sup>41</sup>](#c10-41). The European Court of Human Rights inter-state proceedings *Ukraine and Netherlands v Russia* remain pending on the merits as of mid-2026.

The proxy layer is the element that did not appear in chapter 4: a state hides behind a non-state actor it supplies. The "DPR" legal-political shell, the alternative-cause framings — a Ukrainian Su-25, a Buk from Ukrainian stocks — circulated through Russian state media, and the non-recognition of foreign criminal jurisdiction together produce a deniability architecture that operates independently of the underlying evidence. The Hague judgment resolved individual culpability at one layer. State-actor attribution at the proxy-supply-chain level remains procedurally pending, anchored as of mid-2026 to the JIT's "strong indications" formulation and the ECtHR's active docket. The architecture does not become inoperative because the evidence is good. The architecture stays operative until a body with jurisdiction completes its adjudication; the convicted individuals have not been surrendered.

### The contractor-shell layer — Blackwater at Nisour Square

Chapter 4 also carried the 16 September 2007 shooting at Nisour Square in Baghdad, read there as a partial scapegoat with a system / object alibi secondary. Four convoy guards from the private military company Blackwater — Nicholas Slatten, Paul Slough, Evan Liberty, Dustin Heard — fired weapons that killed seventeen Iraqi civilians, including nine-year-old Ali Kinani, who was sitting in the back seat of his father's car, according to a US Department of Justice indictment and State Department reports on the incident[<sup>42</sup>](#c10-42). In *United States v. Slatten* and *United States v. Slough et al.*, the four guards were convicted in the US District Court for the District of Columbia in 2014: Slatten of first-degree murder; Slough, Liberty, and Heard of voluntary manslaughter, attempted manslaughter, and weapons charges[<sup>43</sup>](#c10-43). They were pardoned by President Trump on 22 December 2020[<sup>44</sup>](#c10-44). The laundering operated above them at two levels: Coalition Provisional Authority Order 17, revised on 27 June 2004, granted contractors immunity from Iraqi criminal jurisdiction[<sup>45</sup>](#c10-45); the State Department's contractual relationship with Blackwater (later Xe Services, later Academi) insulated the corporate principal from operational accountability.

Here the added layer is a legal-status shell — a category that exempts a known actor from jurisdiction, where the proxy layer hid the actor entirely. The "contractor" category is not a criminal-immunity category in international law, but it operated as one in the operational moment. The 22 December 2020 pardons of the four convicted guards then reversed the individual-level accountability that had survived the shell. The shell launders the operation; the pardon launders the conviction; the chain of command above the contractor was never criminally examined. Erik Prince, Blackwater's founder, has spoken publicly about the company's role in subsequent interviews and writings; the public record does not show a criminal charge against him on the Nisour Square matter as of mid-2026[<sup>46</sup>](#c10-46).

### The civilian-invisibility layer — Ukrainian children

The Russian transfers of Ukrainian children from occupied territories came into the argument in chapter 2 as a cost-bearing-goat case where the laundering operated through language. "Evacuation," "humanitarian relocation," and "guardianship" reframed forcible transfer as care.

Russian Presidential Decree No. 330 of 30 May 2022 and Decree No. 585 of 11 July 2022 created an accelerated citizenship pathway for children from Ukrainian orphanages and care institutions in territories under Russian occupation[<sup>47</sup>](#c10-47). President Putin and Russia's Commissioner for Children's Rights, Maria Lvova-Belova, have publicly defended the transfers as humanitarian — for example, in statements broadcast on Russian state television during 2022 and 2023[<sup>48</sup>](#c10-48).

On 17 March 2023, the International Criminal Court's Pre-Trial Chamber II issued arrest warrants for President Putin and Commissioner Lvova-Belova, finding reasonable grounds to believe both bear individual criminal responsibility for the alleged war crime of unlawful deportation of children from Ukrainian territory to the Russian Federation[<sup>49</sup>](#c10-49). The Russian Federation does not recognise the jurisdiction of the International Criminal Court and has stated it will not transfer the named individuals; no trial has been held as of mid-2026.

The civilian-invisibility layer works through euphemism, which the diagnostic resolves only at the level of the underlying conduct, not the language. The cost-bearer category in wartime is reframed faster than blame is moved: the children are not accused of anything, but the linguistic apparatus presents the transfer as care while the transfer continues. The ICC's reasonable-grounds finding establishes the legal mechanism but does not by itself pierce the language. Piercing the language requires naming each euphemism against the underlying conduct. "Evacuation" is named against forcible transfer of a protected population. "Guardianship" is named against acquisition of citizenship pathway under occupying-power authority. "Humanitarian relocation" is named against the absence of mechanisms for return. The Stolen Generations counter-case installed in chapter 2 against cost-bearing-goat euphemism applies here at the same diagnostic layer; chapter 2 did the work.

### Five layers, fired at once

The callbacks together establish a load-bearing generalisation. War does not run its laundering patterns one at a time; it fires the whole stack simultaneously. Layer 1 is strategic-justification reification, installed here through the anchor. Layers 2 through 5 are the patterns installed across the preceding chapters and re-fired in war: chain-of-command secrecy (Abu Ghraib), proxy deniability (MH17), contractor legal-status shell (Blackwater), and civilian-invisibility euphemism (Ukrainian children). Five layers fire at once. The diagnostic resolves each by climbing into the specific chain question by question.

## Classification during the window

The strategic-justification record was not produced in a vacuum. It was produced inside a record-control architecture, operating during the operative window of the precondition-shaping decisions, that consistently advantaged the public case and disadvantaged the underlying contestation.

Three elements of that architecture are documentary. First, executive-branch classification of the underlying intelligence reporting. The October 2002 NIE was declassified in heavily redacted form only on 18 July 2003 — four months after the invasion and three months after Powell's UN speech. The public was asked to evaluate certainty without access to the hedges that qualified it. Second, the WHIG communications coordination synchronised public statements across the White House, the Pentagon, the State Department, and 10 Downing Street, producing a unified message whose internal contestation was inaccessible to outside auditors during the operative window[<sup>50</sup>](#c10-50). Third, the structural advantage: strategic-justification inquiries operate after the war they would adjudicate. The record-hardening operates during the precondition-shaping window; the inquiries operate later, when the war is a historical fact and the policy choices it depended on have already taken effect.

The same record-hardening pattern operates in each callback layer, with different mechanisms. The Abu Ghraib chain hardened its record through the classification of the OLC and Pentagon authorisation memos. The Bybee memo of 1 August 2002 was declassified only after Office of Legal Counsel head Jack Goldsmith and his successor Daniel Levin withdrew it on 30 December 2004[<sup>51</sup>](#c10-51). Many Pentagon authorisation memos were declassified only via the Senate Armed Services Committee inquiry that reported in November 2008. MH17's record was hardened by a Russian state-media counter-record, broadcast through RT and Sputnik and other outlets, operating independently of the JIT and inaccessible to ECtHR proceedings as positively probative of state non-involvement. Blackwater's record was hardened by CPA Order 17 as record-shielding within occupation law; the contractor accountability documentation that would have followed Iraqi criminal jurisdiction never existed because the jurisdiction was excluded. The Ukrainian-children record was hardened by the Russian decrees that framed the transfers as legal under Russian domestic law and by the Russian non-recognition of ICC jurisdiction.

Each layer's record-hardening differs in form. Each layer's record-hardening operates during the operative window. Each layer's record-hardening is challenged only retrospectively, by mechanisms whose charters and jurisdictions reach into the chain at different depths.

In chapter 7 we installed the record-control diagnostic and saw it operate at artifact, institutional, and state scale. War operates the same mechanism at strategic scale: the record is hardened by classification during the operative window and softened by post-hoc inquiry whose charter typically excludes the policy use of the record.

## The steelman of shared priors

The resistance to the "intelligence was wrong" framing did not, in the operative window, come from the named goats. It came from the same outside-executive actors the walk already met — the Knight-Ridder reporters Strobel and Landay[<sup>52</sup>](#c10-52), Powell's chief of staff Lawrence Wilkerson[<sup>53</sup>](#c10-53), the CIA's Tyler Drumheller[<sup>54</sup>](#c10-54), and, belatedly, Curveball himself[<sup>55</sup>](#c10-55) — whose dissents were available, marginalised, or unread. The steelman has to reckon with one document that is sharper than any of them. The Downing Street Memo, dated 23 July 2002 and published by *The Sunday Times* on 1 May 2005, recorded MI6 chief Sir Richard Dearlove's observation that "the intelligence and facts were being fixed around the policy"[<sup>56</sup>](#c10-56) — written eight months before the invasion, by the head of the service whose product was being fixed. Each of these resistances, the Memo included, reached the public only after the operative window had closed.

The cost of resistance was visible. Wilkerson's career did not advance after Powell's departure. Drumheller's role at CIA ended. The Knight-Ridder reporters did not join the major-paper consensus, and were structurally marginalised in the pre-war coverage period. The Plame disclosure, the Niger forgery sequence, and the subsequent Wilson opinion piece sit in this slot as a specific instance of resistance met with retaliation — the disclosure of the identity of an undercover CIA officer following her husband's public dissent from the Niger yellowcake claim. The pattern is consistent across the resistance set: documented, technically available, marginalised in the operative window.

Now the strongest counterargument. It is that the assessments reflected genuine uncertainty rather than a manufactured case, and the policy choice to act on that uncertainty is contestable on the merits but not laundering.

Stated at its strongest: many intelligence services believed Iraq retained some WMD capability as of late 2002. The German BND continued to debrief Curveball; the French DGSE assessed continuing Iraqi interest in WMD though without the certainty of the US position; the Russian SVR's assessments are partially documented and were not assessments of zero capability. Iraq's documented history of WMD use — the chemical weapons attacks on Iranian forces in the 1980s and on Kurdish civilians at Halabja in 1988 — gave foreign services a reasonable prior that programmes could persist. Iraq's post-1991 obstruction of UNSCOM and UNMOVIC's predecessor inspections gave them a reasonable prior that obstruction would conceal continuing activity. After his capture, Saddam Hussein told FBI agent George Piro, in interviews conducted in 2004 and declassified in July 2009, that he had cultivated WMD ambiguity to deter Iran[<sup>57</sup>](#c10-57). The counterargument concludes: the prior was widely shared; the inference from the prior to active stockpiles was reasonable under uncertainty; the policy choice to act under that uncertainty is contestable on the merits but is not laundering, because nothing was hidden that the policy-makers had documented as hidden.

The counterargument is strongest on the prior. The foreign-intelligence consensus that Iraqi WMD activity persisted is documented and was not unanimous noise. Saddam's deterrence-ambiguity strategy, as Piro recorded it, is real and explains part of the chain.

The counterargument is weakest on the gap between the prior and the public statements. Our claim is not that the underlying assessments were certainly fabricated. Our claim is that the gap between the underlying assessments and the public statements was systematically widened by policy-makers and then attributed back to the intelligence community after collapse. SSCI Phase II and Chilcot are the load-bearing documents because they specifically measure that gap. Phase II concluded that public statements on Iraq-al-Qaeda and the Atta-Prague meeting exceeded the intelligence record. Chilcot concluded that the JIC assessments on which the UK public case rested had been presented with "a degree of certainty that was not justified"[<sup>58</sup>](#c10-58). The widespread foreign-intelligence consensus argument is partially correct on the prior; it does not reach the public-statements-versus-intelligence-record gap that the inquiries establish.

This is the laundering charge. It attaches to the gap, not to the prior. It attaches to the system / object alibi — "the IC got it wrong" — that absorbed the policy choice — "we presented certainty the IC's product did not support." It attaches to the chartering of an IC-only audit body — Robb-Silberman — that was structurally prevented from examining the gap.

A second counterargument follows. War is genuinely different, this argument runs, from peacetime: secrecy is legitimate; the chain of command is the operational architecture; civilian casualty is inherent to armed conflict under the laws of war themselves; sovereign immunity and non-recognition of jurisdiction limit the reach of inquiry; the absence of post-event remedy infrastructure means the diagnostic runs and produces nothing actionable. We have built the diagnostic across peacetime cases; the argument is that the cases do not transfer.

The argument is strongest in naming what it names. Each of the structural conditions it lists is real. Secrecy is legitimate in defined wartime functions. The chain of command is the operational architecture, not an obstacle to it. Civilian casualty is inherent to armed conflict; international humanitarian law does not prohibit war, it regulates it. Sovereign immunity and non-recognition limit jurisdiction. The post-event remedy infrastructure is partial and uneven.

The argument is weakest where it concludes that the diagnostic produces nothing actionable. The Iraq Survey Group's Duelfer Report is an actionable finding: it established that the stockpiles did not exist. SSCI Phase II is an actionable finding: it established the public-statements gap. The DoD Inspector General's report on the Office of Special Plans is an actionable finding: it established the IC-vetted misrepresentation. The Chilcot Report is an actionable finding: it established that the legal basis for war was "far from satisfactory"[<sup>59</sup>](#c10-59). The Hague District Court at Schiphol's MH17 judgment is an actionable finding: it convicted three individuals in absentia. The actionability of these findings does not produce remedy. They are operative findings of our diagnostic. The diagnostic does not deliver remedy in war; the diagnostic delivers the record on which future remedy can be built. We turn next to that record.

## Thirteen years to Chilcot

The "intelligence was wrong" alibi did not weaken in a single moment. It weakened across thirteen years, through a sequence of documentary instruments, and the weakening's most important feature is not the conclusion of any single instrument but the architecture that produced them.

The Iraq Survey Group reported on 30 September 2004, with addenda in March 2005. The forensic floor: no stockpiles; no active programmes; the strategic justification's central empirical claim was false[<sup>60</sup>](#c10-60). The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence reported Phase I on 7 July 2004, concluding that the NIE's key judgments were "either overstated, or were not supported by, the underlying intelligence reporting"[<sup>61</sup>](#c10-61). The Robb-Silberman Commission reported on 31 March 2005; within its charter, it identified Curveball as a fabricator and documented the chain-of-handling failures on the bioweapons claim[<sup>62</sup>](#c10-62). SSCI Phase II reported in two components: "Postwar Findings About Iraq's WMD Programs and Links to Terrorism" on 8 September 2006, and "Report on Whether Public Statements Regarding Iraq by U.S. Government Officials Were Substantiated by Intelligence Information" on 25 May 2007[<sup>63</sup>](#c10-63). The DoD Inspector General's report on the Office of Special Plans was completed on 9 February 2007[<sup>64</sup>](#c10-64). Curveball self-identified in *The Guardian* on 15 February 2011[<sup>65</sup>](#c10-65). The Chilcot Inquiry, announced on 15 June 2009, reported on 6 July 2016 with twelve volumes and approximately 2.6 million words; its core findings were that judgments about Iraq's WMD capabilities "were presented with a certainty that was not justified," that the September Dossier presented the JIC assessments with "a degree of certainty that was not justified," and that the legal basis for war was "far from satisfactory"[<sup>66</sup>](#c10-66).

Thirteen years from invasion to Chilcot. The alibi, even as it weakened, operated for thirteen years. The weakening did not produce prosecutions. It did not unwind the war. It did not deliver remedy to the cost-bearers. We do not soften this:

> War's complexity is the design that makes responsibility hard to assign; the diagnostic survives the design because the design is what the diagnostic was built to name.

The complexity is not an obstacle to the diagnostic. The complexity is the laundering surface the diagnostic was constructed to identify. Each layer's structural condition — strategic-justification reification, chain-of-command secrecy, proxy deniability, contractor legal-status shells, civilian-invisibility euphemism — is, in the diagnostic's vocabulary, the specific architecture of a specific laundering operation. Naming the architecture is naming the operation. Climbing the chain with the eight questions resolves the operation into its constituent control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, and cost. The resolution does not produce remedy. The resolution produces the record.

The record is the precondition of remedy. Without the record, the remedy is impossible; with the record, the remedy may or may not arrive. We are precise about which is which.

## Refuse the domain's claim to exception

Our rule follows directly from the layer-stack and the alibi-weakening sequence:

> Where strategic justification stacks chain-of-command secrecy, proxy attribution, contractor shells, civilian-invisibility euphemism, and institutional-product reification, the diagnostic still resolves the case by climbing into each layer with the eight questions. Treat any claim that responsibility in war is irrecoverable as the laundering mechanism itself, and refuse the domain's claim to exception.

The rule is portable: it holds whatever the war, whatever the configuration the layers fire in, and whatever the strategic-justification product — an NIE, an expert assessment, a model output, an intelligence summary, a public-statement schedule, a press background briefing. The five-layer stack is the take-home form; the eight questions are the per-layer protocol.

The rule is a refusal, not an accusation. It refuses the claim, made on every relevant occasion in every relevant domain, that this domain's exceptional conditions make the diagnostic inapplicable. It does not pre-assign blame. It does not pre-determine the case's classification. It runs the diagnostic, layer by layer, and lets the chain speak.

## What made the inquiry work

Interceptions do exist. They are partial, uneven, layered onto the same five-layer stack the laundering operates across, and they reach different depths in different layers. Our load-bearing interception is the UK Iraq Inquiry under Sir John Chilcot.

Chilcot was announced on 15 June 2009 and reported on 6 July 2016 — seven years from announcement to report, thirteen years from invasion. It operated under the UK Inquiries Act 2005 as a statutory public inquiry. Its load-bearing institutional features are four, and the four together explain why this inquiry reached findings the US inquiries did not.

First, statutory subpoena power. Chilcot had access to UK Cabinet papers, JIC assessments, the Manning-Rice memos, and the Bush-Blair correspondence — including the 28 July 2002 minute in which Blair wrote, "I will be with you, whatever," eight months before the invasion[<sup>67</sup>](#c10-67). Cabinet papers are presumptively protected; the Inquiry's statutory access pierced that protection.

Second, charter breadth. The Inquiry was empowered to examine the policy use of intelligence, not only the intelligence itself. This distinguished Chilcot from Robb-Silberman, whose founding Executive Order 13328 of 6 February 2004 explicitly excluded examination of how policy-makers used the intelligence[<sup>68</sup>](#c10-68). Robb-Silberman's charter fenced the alibi; Chilcot's charter did not.

Third, political distance from the original decisions. Chilcot was a privy counsellor; the inquiry was chaired by him and conducted alongside four other panel members; the chair was appointed by Prime Minister Gordon Brown six years after the invasion; the inquiry continued through the May 2010 change of UK government. Distance produced an independence an immediate-aftermath inquiry could not have produced.

Fourth, time horizon. Seven years from announcement to report. The horizon meant the documents were available, the witnesses could not plead immediate-event memory limitations, and the inquiry could trace the chain across the full pre-war, war, and post-war windows.

These four features produced the findings already set out above — the unjustified certainty, the dossier, the "far from satisfactory" legal basis — and one more: the UK decision to commit to military action was effectively taken by Blair ahead of the formal Cabinet process (Executive Summary, ¶¶ 374–432).

The Hague District Court at Schiphol's MH17 judgment of 17 November 2022 demonstrates a different institutional response to a different layer of war-laundering: criminal adjudication of individuals inside a proxy chain. It convicted three individuals in absentia; it has not delivered them. The European Court of Human Rights's *Ukraine and Netherlands v Russia* proceedings demonstrate inter-state adjudication of state responsibility; the case is pending on the merits as of mid-2026. Neither reaches the strategic-justification layer that Chilcot reached. Neither has Chilcot's charter breadth. Together with Chilcot they establish that interception mechanisms exist at multiple war-laundering layers and operate under different procedural forms.

We are honest about the cost of the escape.

The strategic-justification alibi operated, even as it weakened, for thirteen years before formal documentary closure. SSCI Phase II's findings, the DoD IG OSP report's findings, and Chilcot's findings are inquiry conclusions, not adjudicated liability. We cannot characterise senior officials as criminally responsible for the strategic deception absent specific adjudications that do not exist as of mid-2026. The invasion is a historical event; no inquiry has reversed it. Iraqi civilians killed in the invasion and occupation, UK and coalition military families, displaced Iraqis: Chilcot did not deliver compensation, did not reverse displacement, did not prosecute responsibility-holders. The inquiry's product was the record, not the remedy.

A hostile reader will note an asymmetry we must face directly. The four interceptions named here — Chilcot, Schiphol, the ECtHR, the ICC — all operate inside or against open political systems and against states whose archives, courts, parliaments, and inquiry statutes can be turned on their own conduct. No Chilcot-equivalent has ever issued from the Russian Federation regarding Chechnya, Syria, or the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. No equivalent has issued from the People's Republic of China regarding Xinjiang or Tibet. No equivalent has issued from the Islamic Republic of Iran regarding its proxies' conduct in Iraq, Syria, Yemen, or Lebanon. The asymmetry is real and we do not paper it over: the interception architectures named here are conditional on institutional preconditions — independent judiciaries, statutory inquiry charters, archival access, parliamentary subpoena power — that closed authoritarian systems are designed to deny. The diagnostic still resolves those cases at the level of the eight questions; what is unavailable is the domestic interception step. Our argument is that the same architecture-preconditions that make Chilcot possible in the UK are the architecture-preconditions whose erosion later chapters name in the US. The asymmetry is not a defence of the case we analyse. The asymmetry is the reason the case we analyse matters: where the preconditions exist, their use is contested; where they do not exist, the laundering is total.

The structural choice to scope strategic-justification inquiries to intelligence-product rather than policy-use has not been reformed in US practice; the Robb-Silberman charter precedent remains in US executive-order practice. And across the other four layers the interception coverage is partial, uneven, and reaches different ranks — the in-absentia operators at Schiphol, the enlisted soldiers at Abu Ghraib, the contractors at Nisour Square whose convictions were then pardoned, the unexecutable ICC warrants — every one of them stopping short of the controlling decision a layer up.

Chilcot exists because the Inquiries Act 2005 gave a privy counsellor subpoena power over Cabinet papers and a charter broad enough to ask how policy-makers used the intelligence. The inquiry took seven years to produce. It produced the record. It produced no prosecutions, no unwinding, and no remedy. The architecture that made the inquiry necessary remains in place — both in the UK, where the Inquiries Act covers the next instance, and in the US, where no equivalent charter has been written.

The Knapp Commission and Serpico counter-case installed in chapter 2 against the partial-scapegoat "bad apples" framing applies here at strategic scale: institutional resistance can be designed, documented, and sustained. The Stolen Generations counter installed in chapter 2 against cost-bearing-goat euphemism applies here in the Ukrainian-children layer: linguistic apparatus can be named, decomposed, and matched against the underlying conduct. The diagnostic does not, by itself, produce remedy in war. The diagnostic produces the record on which future architectures of remedy can rest.

The five-layer stack shows up in coverage and policy debate; it does not show up in headlines labelled "laundering layer." Recognising it begins with the layers themselves.

The strategic-justification layer is operating when officials cite intelligence or expert assessments with high certainty in public, where the underlying products are unavailable or contested, and where the institutional product is treated as agentic — "the intelligence says," "the assessments show" — while the policy choice to commission and present that product is in the background. The chain-of-command layer is operating when a wartime incident is publicly blamed on enlisted personnel or a unit commander while doctrine memos, theatre commander authorisations, and interrogation-technique approvals remain classified during the operative window. The proxy layer is operating when operations are conducted by entities with deniable legal status — "armed groups," "people's republics," "local forces," "irregulars" — while sovereign-state command of supply, training, and recovery is publicly denied. The contractor-shell layer is operating when private military, private surveillance, private interrogation, or private logistics operate under contractual arrangements that exclude criminal jurisdiction and are described as administrative convenience rather than as accountability architecture. The civilian-invisibility layer is operating when cost-bearers — civilians, refugees, transferred children, displaced populations — are described in care-language ("evacuated," "rescued," "relocated") or aggregated into casualty figures whose methodology is contested without being adjudicated.

Recognition is a count, not a verdict. In a war or statecraft case we are being asked to evaluate, we can ask which of these five layers is in operation. Where three or more appear together, the case carries the structural shape we have been describing — and the eight questions are the per-layer tool we already have.

Three observable markers diagnose the strategic-justification layer specifically. First, the institution making the public case — the executive branch or cabinet — is presenting an institutional product it commissioned. Second, the body that audits the product is barred by charter or practice from examining the policy use of the product. Third, the public certainty exceeds the hedges in the underlying product, and the hedges are inaccessible to the public during the operative window. When two or more of these markers are present, the diagnostic is active. We do not need to know what the underlying product says. We need to know who is allowed to ask whether the public statement matches the product.

We are most likely to be positioned as goat at the layer our own role intersects. The enlisted soldier meets the manifest-illegality limit set out above; the analyst's hedges are stripped from the public statement; the contractor sits inside a legal-status shell that voids jurisdiction in the operational moment; the civilian's harm is renamed into care-language — "evacuation," "guardianship," "humanitarian relocation" — over the underlying forcible transfer. Each seam is the same shape: the cost lands where the role sits, while the controlling decision stays a layer up.

The diagnostic was built to work where it is hardest to use. The hardest domain is the one where every laundering layer we have installed fires together, where the operative window is closed by classification, where the audit charters are fenced by executive order, where jurisdiction is selective, where the cost-bearers are aggregated into figures whose methodology is contested, and where the inquiry, when it arrives, takes thirteen years and produces the record but not the remedy. It survives even there. We leave holding the five-layer stack and the eight questions — and the seam, for each of us, is the layer our own role intersects.

The next part installs the rules.
