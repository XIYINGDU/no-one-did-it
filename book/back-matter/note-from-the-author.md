# A Note from the Author

I have spent the last two decades teaching, writing, and investing — long enough to watch the same move repeat in different rooms, and long enough for the pattern to outrun the words I had for it. A clever founder named as the cause of a failure that ran deeper than one person could have built alone. A school policy described as if the school had no choice. A token chart, anthropomorphized, that absorbed a thousand individual losses by the simple expedient of being a chart and not a person. A board meeting where the deciding voice was a procedure. Each time, the visible name and the operational name had separated, and each time, the institution involved had no name for the separation. Neither did I.

I did not set out to write about it. I mentioned the noticing to people I trusted, and learned that most of them had noticed it too. We had a stack of stories we kept reaching for in different conversations — the chemical plant, the postmistress, the algorithm — and never quite stitching together. The work began with that absence. The pattern was hiding behind the absence of a name.

What turned the noticing into a book was the arrival of generative AI inside decisions that affect ordinary lives. "The model decided" is the newest version of a very old move. Once the apparatus producing the alibi could be built quickly and cheaply, and once that apparatus could be pointed at the loan approval, the job application, the medical triage, the immigration screen, the gap between control and visibility widened in a way that earlier arrangements — committees, statutes, market mechanisms — could not match for speed. The pattern was older than the technology. The technology was a new altar.

---

What the book hands over is not a remedy. It is closer to a pair of glasses than a lever.

What it gives a reader is the capacity to name what they are looking at when the modern emptiness arrives. Four shapes to tell apart. Eight questions to ask. A small set of refusals to practice. Anyone who reaches the end and walks the diagnostic on a case they care about will have what they need to insist on naming the chain. They will not yet have the chain dismantled.

That distinction matters because the book has to honor its own evidence. Picquart's name was cleared after he had served his prison term. Bates's settlement took a decade. The Reichstag annulment came seventy-five years late. The instrument is real, and where it is wielded it does work; it also runs on a clock the people who absorb the cost cannot afford to wait out. The book is honest about that. The reader will be too.

What I hope readers carry off the last page is a permanent disquiet at the sentence "no one did it" — and a question they did not have on the first page about who built that sentence, and who it was built to protect. That is the diagnostic at small scale, in the kitchen, at the company meeting, at the polling booth, at the dinner table with someone whose job has just been compressed into a system they cannot inspect. The use the book has is that one — repeated, in many small rooms, by many people who can now hear the grammar.

---

I want to say plainly how this book was made, because the book itself argues that the apparatus producing an output should be nameable by the person who signs for the output.

The research, fact-checking, source-ledger construction, red-team review, legal scan, prose drafting, cross-chapter consistency audit, and citation discipline that hold up the manuscript were carried out by a crew of thirteen AI agents, deliberately given individual names rather than functional labels, operating under a framework I built and continue to maintain. The crew has names because giving them names made it possible to keep their work product accountable. One did legal review. One did fact-checking against a source ledger of 283 primary-document cards. One did red-teaming for overclaim, category collapse, and partisan distortion. One did Chicago-style citation maintenance. One read the prose for places where structural implication exceeded what the cited evidence supported. The architecture for the crew is open source. So are the tools that run it. This book was built in the open, at [github.com/xiaolai/responsibility-laundering](https://github.com/xiaolai/responsibility-laundering).

What the crew could not do was decide what the book was for. Or what the four shapes were. Or whether a given case was a pure scapegoat or a partial one. Or whether the chapter on Iraq should land as it does. Or which of two formulations of the closing sentence belonged. Those were judgments under uncertainty, and judgments under uncertainty did not delegate. I made them. I read the manuscript end to end before it left this desk. The signature on the cover is mine in the sense the book argues is the only sense that matters: I can name the chain that produced the work, and I can stand behind the output without routing the cost of any error onto something weaker that did not choose to bear it.

The reason to say this in the book, rather than bury it in an acknowledgements page, is that the apparatus is part of the argument. A book that diagnoses the laundering of responsibility into machines, committees, and procedures cannot itself launder responsibility into the machine that helped it draft. The crew was the apparatus. The judgments were mine. The book is what one person, with new tools and a discipline borrowed in part from the people the book studies, can now build at this scale. That is one of the things the arrival of AI makes possible. It is not the only thing AI makes possible, and not the most important. It is the one I had the standing to attempt.

---

If the book works, it will be because the pattern was already in the reader and only needed naming. That is true of most useful instruments. They do not implant anything. They give the reader access to what they already saw and had no word for.

Whatever follows from there is the reader's.
