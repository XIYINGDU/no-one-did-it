# A Note on Cases

Two questions arrive before the first chapter does. Anyone reading thirteen chapters about responsibility laundering will notice that some of the cases involve United States administrations of the present decade, and that other cases involve administrations of the present decade in other states. The first question: is this a partisan book? The second: is this a Western book?

The answer to both is no, and the structure of the cases is the evidence.

The recurring move documented here — the substitution of one visible face for a chain of decisions made by people whose names did not appear in the coverage — is administration-agnostic and jurisdiction-agnostic. Chapter 2 walks Abu Ghraib's partial-scapegoat pattern through the chain-of-command record assembled by the Senate Armed Services Committee, the Taguba report, and the Fay-Jones report; the architects named in those records served under the George W. Bush administration. The Office of Legal Counsel memoranda redefining torture under domestic and international law were signed in 2002 by Jay Bybee, drafted by John Yoo, then withdrawn by Jack Goldsmith in 2004. The destruction of the CIA interrogation videotapes was ordered in November 2005, under the same administration, before the next. Chapter 12 reads the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's 2014 study of the program — itself drafted under congressional Democratic majority, on records from a program initiated under Republican executive — through record-discipline rather than party. The Pentagon Papers chapter belongs to a different presidency again. Watergate to another. The chain of preventability is the unit of analysis; the party affiliation of whoever happened to hold the office is not.

The same agnosticism holds for state. Chapter 4 reads the Russian Federation's official accounts of Crimea 2014 and MH17 alongside the Joint Investigation Team's reconstruction; chapter 8 reads the United Kingdom Cabinet Office through the Iraq Inquiry; chapter 7 reads the British Post Office through the Williams Inquiry. Chapter 10 reads United States artificial-intelligence companies under Judge Alsup's June 2025 summary-judgment ruling. The Therac-25 deaths happened at six United States and Canadian medical centres; the Boeing 737 MAX crashes happened in Indonesia and Ethiopia; the Bhopal disaster killed thousands in India; the Cour de Cassation's eventual quashing of Alfred Dreyfus's conviction happened in Paris in 1906. The cases were chosen because the chain-of-responsibility record exists in sufficient public form to be read forensically. Where the record exists, the diagnostic applies; where it does not, no chapter rests on it.

Three of the live-content chapters do return repeatedly to Donald Trump — the same person, in two presidencies separated by a single term. That recurrence reflects the underlying record. The pardons of the Blackwater four in chapter 4, the family-separation architecture and the census-citizenship pretext in chapter 6, the invocation of the Alien Enemies Act in 2025 and the deferred-records litigation around DOGE in chapter 9, the executive-design example in chapter 11 — each rests on a specific documented act with a specific court filing, signed order, sworn testimony, or inquiry record. The aggregation across chapters is not editorial: it is what the public record currently contains. Where future records foreground a different figure under different circumstances, the same diagnostic will run on that record. The questions stay. The named cases change.

Anyone picking this up looking for partisan ammunition will be disappointed; anyone looking for a single villain will be disappointed too. The taxonomy in chapter 2 makes both expectations difficult. Pure scapegoats, partial scapegoats, system-and-object alibis, and cost-bearing goats are four distinct moves, and most of the cases fail to fit a single category cleanly. The laundering pattern is administration-agnostic and ancient. The technology of laundering changes — from priest to bureaucrat to algorithm — while the structure does not. The only durable defence is a portable diagnostic we can run on whatever case lands in our inbox next.

Two short references sit at the back of the book — *Cases at a Glance* (one paragraph per case, with dates, named actors, outcome, and the chapter where each appears) and *Acronyms and Institutions* (the legal, administrative, and technical shorthand the chapters use). The chapter prose introduces each case as it arrives; the references are there for the reader who meets Bhopal in chapter 5 or Horizon in chapter 7 and wants a fast refresher without breaking the chapter's flow.
