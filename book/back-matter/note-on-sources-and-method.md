# A Note on Sources and Method

This is a short, candid account of how the cases were chosen, how the sources were graded, and what the chapters do and do not claim. *A Note on Cases* in the front matter addressed *which* cases the book treats and why; this note addresses *how* the treatment was done.

## How the cases were chosen

Three filters operated, in order.

The first filter is documentary. A case is in the book only when the chain of preventability — control, benefit, knowledge, record-control, cost-bearing — is readable from the public documentary record: court judgments, official inquiries, inspector general and legislative reports, regulatory findings, primary documents, and reputable investigative journalism. Where the documentary chain stops at speculation, no chapter is built on the case. The diagnostic the book argues — eight questions about who controlled, who benefited, who knew, who could have prevented — only works on cases where those questions can be answered against the record.

The second filter is taxonomical. The four-category taxonomy installed in chapter 2 — pure scapegoat, partial scapegoat, system or object alibi, cost-bearing goat — admits cases that fit one of the four shapes or that fit two of the four as a declared hybrid. Hybrid declarations are explicit in the prose; the book does not flatten ambiguity. Cases that fail to fit any of the four shapes are not included — not because such cases do not exist, but because the diagnostic the book installs does not apply to them.

The third filter is what a chapter can teach. Many cases that pass the first two filters are not the load-bearing case for any chapter the book wanted to write. The cases the chapters anchor on are the ones the diagnostic teaches against most clearly; the cases named only in passing — the bibliography is full of these — are the ones the diagnostic touches but did not need to walk in full.

## How the sources were graded

The project's evidence-grade discipline assigns one of four grades to every load-bearing factual claim before it earns space in a chapter.

**A** is reserved for high-confidence public-record sources: court judgments, official inquiries (the Iraq Inquiry under Sir John Chilcot; the Williams Inquiry under the Inquiries Act 2005; the U.S. Senate Armed Services Committee Detainee Inquiry of 2008), inspector general and GAO reports, statutes, executive orders, regulator findings, primary documents, and audited technical reports. Most of the book's load-bearing claims rest on A-grade sources.

**B** is credible secondary or investigative: major investigative journalism (the Pulitzer-winning *Seattle Times* series on the Boeing 737 MAX; the *Computer Weekly* and Nick Wallis reporting on Horizon; *Bellingcat*'s open-source work on MH17), reputable NGO investigation, scholarly articles, expert forensic analysis, documentary records with partial access. B-grade claims are appropriate for chapter-level claims that the chapter says are B-grade in the surrounding text.

**C** is live, contested, incomplete, or allegation-stage. Ongoing litigation. Single-source reports. Active conflict-zone events. Cases with conflicting forensic claims. The chapters that rest on live content — the 2025 administrative-law arc in chapter 8; the AI-stack chapter 9; the war-stack chapter 10's Ukrainian-children, Iraq-WMD, and MH17 references — carry the procedural status of each named case as it stands at the manuscript's most recent re-pass, and are scheduled for re-verification at galley-lock-minus-thirty-days.

**D** is excluded. Viral claims, unsourced anecdotes, partisan summaries, rumour, emotionally satisfying but unverified stories do not appear in the book.

A chapter-level claim cannot rest only on C-grade evidence unless the chapter is explicitly about uncertainty or contested record control.

## Procedural-stage discipline

The book operates inside a procedural-stage discipline. Every claim about a living person or a corporation is rendered in language that matches the documented procedural status: *charged with*, *alleged*, *reported*, *admitted*, *settled*, *found liable*, *convicted*. The book does not use a guilt verb without a guilt finding, and it does not use a knowledge verb (*knew*, *was aware that*) without a documented knowledge source. Where the procedural status is contested or ambiguous, the contestation is named in the same sentence ("charged in the United States and in Germany, and has denied knowing").

Quotations are byte-exact to the source. Alterations are marked: ellipsis for shortened text, square brackets for inserted clarifications, *[sic]* for original errors preserved, "(author's translation)" for translated material. Anonymous journalism sources may stand only where the source meets the journalism source's own disclosure standard and the chapter says so.

## How live-content cases are handled

Several chapters rest in part on cases that are still moving in the public record: *Bartz v. Anthropic* (under submission post the May 14 2026 fairness hearing); *CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service* (preservation orders in force); *American Oversight v. DOGE*; *Noem v. Abrego Garcia*; *Trump v. J.G.G.* and the Alien Enemies Act docket; the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber II warrants on the Russian transfer of Ukrainian children; the Williams Inquiry's continuing volumes after Volume 1.

For each live case the chapter (i) names the operative procedural stage as of the manuscript's date, (ii) frames each load-bearing claim in language that survives a subsequent change in that stage, and (iii) carries a scheduled re-verification clock at galley-lock-minus-thirty-days. Where a later edition encounters a fact pattern this edition could not have known, the chapter's diagnostic re-runs on the new record — the rule, not the case, is what the book installs.

## The diagnostic, in compact form

The book argues that responsibility laundering is the recurring institutional method by which power keeps control or benefit while moving blame onto a weaker bearer — a person, a procedural shell, a proxy, a machine, a market, a committee, a legal form, or a record. The diagnostic the book installs is eight questions about any laundering candidate (blame, control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record control, cost, and what responsibility would look like if it followed control) and four shapes the laundering takes (pure scapegoat, partial scapegoat, system or object alibi, cost-bearing goat).

The diagnostic does not assign guilt. The diagnostic names the gap between visible blame and held responsibility, and lets the reader test whether that gap is what the public account of an institutional event is actually doing.

## Limits

The book does not claim that every institutional failure is laundering. Many are not. The book claims that *the recurring move* it documents has a recognisable shape and that the recognisable shape has portable countermeasures. Where the move is not present, the diagnostic returns nothing, which is the diagnostic working correctly.

The book also does not claim that the cases as it treats them are the complete account of those cases. Each chapter's bibliography names the further reading that any reader who wants the deeper account should consult.
