# Preface

A plane is in the sea, and the first explanation is the crew. A village postmistress is in prison for a shortfall that existed only inside accounting software she was told no one could alter without her knowing — and was not allowed to inspect. A child taken at a border cannot be matched back to a parent, because the record that would have matched them was never built. The harm is real. The cost is real. Someone is bearing it right now. And when we go looking for who is responsible — who decided, who could have stopped it — we find a strange, modern emptiness. The pilots erred. The system flagged it. Procedures were followed. Mistakes were made. The model decided. No one did it.

We have all heard this grammar. Most of us have stood on the receiving end of it — told that it is policy, that the system requires it, that the regulations leave no choice — while knowing, with perfect certainty, that somewhere a person made a decision and was then written out of the sentence. That sentence is what follows here.

The argument is simple, and once seen it is hard to un-see. What looks like an accident, or complexity, or an unfortunate system failure is very often a move — old, often deliberate, and increasingly refined. Power keeps the control and keeps the benefit, and routes the blame, the liability, and the cost onto something weaker: a junior employee, a proxy, a contractor, a machine, a market, a committee, a procedure, a record. The name for the move is responsibility laundering.

The rite is older than any of the institutions that perform it now. Thousands of years ago a goat was driven into the wilderness carrying the community's sins, and the priest, the elders, and the crowd all watched it go, knowing exactly what they were doing. We never stopped performing the rite. We stopped naming it. The altar moved — from the temple to the org chart, from the org chart to the legal entity, from the legal entity to the algorithm — and at each move the substitution went a little further underground, until the part where someone announces "this one will carry it for us" had been deleted entirely.

What stands where the altar stood is a bureaucracy, and bureaucracy here is not a complaint about red tape. It is the name for the apparatus that separates the person who decides from the person who pays. None of us lives outside one. The apparatus is built to make a single question hard to answer: if responsibility followed control instead of visibility, where would it land? Hold that question. Most of what is described in these pages is an attempt to answer it on case after case where the institutions involved worked hard to keep it unanswerable.

What is offered here is not a lecture. It is an instrument — eight questions and four recognizable shapes — walked through real cases until it becomes ours to use. Who was blamed. Who had control. Who benefited. Who knew. Who could have prevented it. Who held the record. Who bore the cost. And what responsibility would look like if it followed control. The four shapes recur: the innocent substitute, the small actor blamed so the chain above goes unexamined, the machine or company or committee that becomes the named cause, and the person who absorbs the harm without ever being the one who held the controls.

The hardest case is the one closest to home: we read about the postmistress and the crew from a safe distance, but it is the same machinery we all live inside, and it keeps no seat reserved. One day the only name on the document could be ours — the form we initialed, drafted by a hand we never met, on grounds we were never shown. If it fails, the box with our initials is the first thing anyone finds, and the hand that drafted it is already gone. The instrument is built to be turned on that case too.

What follows moves in one direction: from seeing the move, to taking it apart on the hardest cases power has built, to the design rules that would catch it, to a field guide compact enough to carry.

The move belongs to no party and no flag. In the United States it has run under administrations of both parties for decades — but it is not an American story. It killed thousands at a chemical plant in India and let the blame stop at a local subsidiary; it shot a passenger airliner out of the sky and routed the blame to a proxy across the border; it described the removal of children from occupied territory as rescue; and it has been pulled back apart, after the fact, in the dockets of international courts and inquiries. It is not a property of the left or the right, and not of any one country. It is a property of power operating under procedure — and procedure is everywhere power is.

There is a reason to set all of this down now. "The model decided" is the newest alibi the apparatus has built. It is arriving, this decade, in the rooms that decide the loan, the job, the diagnosis, the benefit — rooms where a system can be pointed to as the cause precisely because no single person inside it can be made to answer for the output. The oldest move power knows has acquired its most modern instrument, and the two are converging fast.

So return, at the end, to where this began. A plane in the sea. A postmistress in a cell. A child who cannot be matched to a parent. *No one did it* is not a fact about the world. It is a thing that was built — sometimes by a decision, always by an arrangement. By the last page we will not be able to hear *no one did it* again without asking who built the sentence — and who it was built to protect.
