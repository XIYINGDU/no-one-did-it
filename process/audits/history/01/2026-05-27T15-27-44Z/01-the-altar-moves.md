# The Altar Moves

## Accusation scene

Shortly after nine o'clock on the night of 27 February 1933, the Reichstag in Berlin was on fire. The glow was visible from the Brandenburg Gate. Police entered the building through a side door; smoke was already coming out under the cornices. In one of the lower chambers they found a young man, bare-chested, his shirt and jacket abandoned in a corridor where he had used them to start the flames. He was carrying matches and a packet of firelighters. He did not run.

His name was Marinus van der Lubbe. He was twenty-four years old, Dutch, a former bricklayer, a council communist whose first language was not German, who could not see well, and who had walked across half of Europe to be in Berlin that month. At arrest he gave his name and confessed at once. He had set the fire himself, he said, alone, as a signal to the German working class to rise against the new chancellor, who had been in office for less than four weeks. He repeated the confession through the night.

By dawn, four thousand other people in Prussia were under arrest. None of them had been inside the Reichstag.

## Official story

The first version of the story did not wait for the fire to be out. Within hours, Hermann Göring, who was both Reich Minister without Portfolio and Reich Commissioner for the Prussian Interior, announced that the fire was the opening signal of a Communist insurrection. Van der Lubbe, the official statement went, was an agent of the Communist Party of Germany (the KPD), coordinating with the Communist International in Moscow. The KPD had been planning the uprising for weeks. The fire was the match.

The Prussian political police acted on the announcement before they finished investigating the building. Arrest lists already drawn up for emergency use were issued through the night of 27 / 28 February. By the morning of the 28th, roughly four thousand KPD officials, deputies, journalists, and members had been taken into custody in Prussia alone — a figure reconstructed by Richard J. Evans in *The Coming of the Third Reich* (2003) from the surviving Reich Security Main Office files held at the Bundesarchiv as record group R 58 [CITE: evans-2003-coming-of-third-reich-ch1]. Newspapers in friendly hands carried the conspiracy framing the same morning.

That morning, 28 February 1933, President Paul von Hindenburg signed the *Verordnung des Reichspräsidenten zum Schutz von Volk und Staat* — the Reichstag Fire Decree — published in *Reichsgesetzblatt* I 1933, page 83 [CITE: rgbl-i-1933-p-83-reichstagsbrandverordnung]. The decree, two pages long, suspended seven articles of the Weimar Constitution: personal liberty, freedom of expression, freedom of the press, freedom of association and assembly, the privacy of postal and telephonic communication, the inviolability of the home, the inviolability of property. It authorised the Reich government to override the constituent states. It provided the legal scaffolding for preventive detention of political opponents. It contained no sunset. It was renewed routinely and remained in operative legal force for the next twelve years.

Three weeks later, on 23 March 1933, the Reichstag — meeting at the Kroll Opera House because its own building was uninhabitable — passed the *Gesetz zur Behebung der Not von Volk und Reich*, the Enabling Act, conferring legislative authority on the Reich Cabinet for four years and permitting cabinet decrees that deviated from the Constitution itself. The Act was published the following day in *Reichsgesetzblatt* I 1933, page 141 [CITE: rgbl-i-1933-p-141-ermaechtigungsgesetz]. The vote was carried by 444 to 94. The 94 dissenters were Social Democrats. The 81 deputies of the KPD, who had been elected on 5 March, were absent; almost all of them had been arrested under the decree signed three weeks earlier, under the legal authority created by the fire one man had confessed to setting.

This was the story as the regime presented it through the spring and summer of 1933. A fire. A foreign communist. A conspiracy. A decree. A law. A new order. Each step had a name. The name at the centre of the story, the name on which the rest of the structure was hung, was van der Lubbe's.

## Hidden architecture

There is a word in English for what the Nazi government did with van der Lubbe in the first week of March 1933, and the word is older than the German state.

In 1530, in Antwerp, an exiled English priest named William Tyndale was translating the Pentateuch from Hebrew into English for the first time. When he came to a passage in Leviticus describing an annual rite of purgation — two goats brought before the priest, one slaughtered as a sin offering, the other sent alive into the wilderness carrying the iniquities of the community — he needed a word for the second goat. The Hebrew text gave the destination of that second goat as *Azazel*, a word the rabbinic tradition had already been arguing over for a thousand years. Tyndale read *Azazel* as a compound: *ez ozel*, the goat that goes away, the escape-goat. In his English Pentateuch of 1530 he wrote *scapegoate*. Eighty years later the King James translators kept the word; the transmission is documented in David Daniell's 1992 Yale critical edition of Tyndale's Old Testament [CITE: daniell-1992-tyndale-old-testament-pentateuch].

Much modern Hebrew Bible scholarship treats Tyndale's compound etymology as philologically strained. Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible commentary reads *Azazel* as the name of a demonic being inhabiting the wilderness, not a phrase describing the goat itself; the demon-name reading is widely held but not universal among contemporary commentators [CITE: milgrom-1991-anchor-bible-leviticus-azazel]. The word is, by current scholarly judgement, a misreading. But the misreading went into English at the historical moment English was acquiring its modern shape, and it remained there. By the early nineteenth century the noun *scapegoat* had migrated out of the Bible into general use to name any person made to carry blame that belongs to others; the *Oxford English Dictionary* dates the first figurative use to 1824, in a passage describing country-boys who "bear their fate as scape-goats, (for all sins whatsoever are laid as matters of course to their door,..), with amazing resignation" [CITE: oed-scapegoat-figurative-1824].

This is how the English word *scapegoat* arrived. It carries, in its morphology, the Bronze Age purgation ritual that produced it. It is a ritual term that became a political one without anyone deciding to translate it again.

The practice the word names is the same practice, and it has not stopped. What has changed is the altar.

The ancient rite was a substitution that named itself. The priest declared it. The goat was the goat. The community watched. The modern case does not declare it; it does not need to. The substitution has gone underground into the morning's headline, into the press release, into the docket number. The same machine, the same product — only the part where the substitution is named has been deleted.

## Older echo

Leviticus 16 is a ritual text. It does not argue; it instructs. On the tenth day of the seventh month, the Day of Atonement, Aaron the priest is to bring two goats to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting and to cast lots over them. One lot reads "for the LORD" — that goat is offered as a sin offering. The other lot reads "for Azazel" — that goat is "set alive before the LORD, to make atonement over it, to send it away into the wilderness to Azazel" (v. 10, JPS translation) [CITE: jps-1985-tanakh-leviticus-16].

Then come the two pivotal verses:

> Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat and confess over it all the iniquities and transgressions of the Israelites, whatever their sins, putting them on the head of the goat; and it shall be sent off to the wilderness through a designated man. Thus the goat shall carry on it all their iniquities to an inaccessible region; and the goat shall be set free in the wilderness [CITE: jps-1985-tanakh-leviticus-16].

The architecture is exact. The priest is named. The hands are placed. The iniquities are confessed aloud and transferred to the goat by physical contact. The destination is named — *Azazel*, the demon or the place, the text will not say which. A specific man, designated, leads the goat out. The land is "cut off". The goat does not return. The community is purged for the year. Next year it is performed again. The ritual is annual; the cycle does not close.

This is openly substitutionary. The community knows. The priest knows. The goat is not pretending to be anything other than what it is. Iniquities are not the goat's iniquities. They are the community's. The goat is the bearer.

Roughly a thousand miles to the west, on the sixth day of the month Thargelion, the Athenians performed a related rite. The earliest surviving description is from Hipponax of Ephesus, who wrote in the sixth century BCE; the surviving fragments are collected in M. L. West's Oxford Classical Text [CITE: hipponax-west-fragments-pharmakos]. A marginal person — destitute, deformed, sometimes criminal, often a foreigner — was selected to serve as the city's *pharmakos*. Hipponax says he was fed at public expense in the days before the rite. On the day, he was led in procession through the city, beaten with branches of squill and figs, and driven out beyond the walls. In some sources he was killed; in others he was only expelled; the scholarship is divided on which formed the standard practice, as Jan Bremmer set out in *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* in 1983 and as Dennis Hughes mapped at book length in *Human Sacrifice in Ancient Greece* in 1991 [CITE: bremmer-1983-scapegoat-rituals-greece; hughes-1991-human-sacrifice-ancient-greece]. The function is undisputed: the *pharmakos* was understood to carry the city's pollution out with him. He absorbed it physically. He was the *pharmakos*; the city was the city; nobody confused which was which.

In Babylon a similar grammar was performed at the *Akitu*, the New Year festival, in a rite the Akkadian scribes called *kuppuru* — to wipe clean. A *mašmaššu* priest cleansed the temple by passing a goat or sheep over the surfaces, the animal taking on the impurities of the place; the carcass was then disposed of in the Euphrates. The *Anchor Bible Dictionary*'s entry on Atonement sets out the *kuppuru* ritual and its etymology; Walter Burkert's *Structure and History in Greek Mythology and Ritual* (1979) reads the Babylonian rite alongside the Greek and Levantine parallels [CITE: anchor-bible-dictionary-atonement-kuppuru; burkert-1979-structure-history-greek-mythology]. The Akkadian *kuppuru* and the Hebrew *kipper* — atone — share a root.

In Rome the same impulse named a month. The *Februa* were the lustration rites by which the city periodically purged itself; the Luperci ran the boundaries; the month of February took its name from the verb *februare*, to purify, as Ovid sets out in *Fasti* II.31 and following and as Plutarch describes in his *Life of Romulus*, chapter 21 [CITE: ovid-fasti-2-februa-luperci].

The grammar is regional. Anatolia, Syria-Palestine, Mesopotamia, the Aegean, Italy — the substitution rite is a Bronze Age and Iron Age technology of communal hygiene, available in several languages at once. It survives in Leviticus 16 because Leviticus 16 was canonised; the others survive in fragments because their cultures were canonised by others. But the rite itself, as a way for a community to discharge accumulated pollution onto a designated bearer, was something the ancient world took as obvious.

In every version, the substitution is named. The community holds the rite knowingly. The priest, the elders, the *mašmaššu*, the Luperci, the citizen body of Athens — these are not deceiving themselves. They have a problem (sin, plague, miasma, civic crisis), they have a method (transfer the problem to a bearer), and they have an outcome (the bearer leaves; the community continues). The ritual's openness is not a flaw. It is the technology.

The point of the long detour to the ancient world is not that Berlin in 1933 was a city performing the Thargelia. Berlin did not know it was performing anything. The point is that the practice predates the politics by three thousand years, and that the modern case lost only one feature of it. The modern case lost the naming.

## Record hardens

After the fire, in the months that followed, the alibi went into the record.

The Prussian political police — Section IA, the predecessor of the Gestapo, under Rudolf Diels — controlled the investigative file. The Reich Propaganda Ministry, established 13 March 1933 under Joseph Goebbels, controlled the press framing. The *Reichsgericht*, the Reich Supreme Court at Leipzig, controlled the legal record. Each of these institutions did its work. None of them were independent in any sense the Weimar Constitution would have recognised six months earlier.

In the weeks after the arrest the Prussian prosecutors widened the charge from arson to *Hochverrat*, high treason, and joined four more defendants to van der Lubbe's case: Ernst Torgler, the KPD parliamentary leader, who had turned himself in to clear his name; and three Bulgarians who had been in Berlin on Comintern business — Georgi Dimitrov, Blagoi Popov, and Vasil Tanev. None of them had been in the Reichstag on the night of the fire. They were the human shape of the alleged conspiracy.

On 29 March 1933, six weeks after the fire, the regime passed a retroactive amendment to the Reich Penal Code. The *Gesetz über Verhängung und Vollzug der Todesstrafe* — published in *Reichsgesetzblatt* I 1933, Nr. 28, page 151 [CITE: rgbl-i-1933-nr-28-p-151-lex-van-der-lubbe] — raised the penalty for arson against public buildings from penitentiary confinement to death and made the higher penalty applicable to acts committed before the law was passed. The law is known in the historical literature as the *Lex van der Lubbe*. It existed so that one man, already in custody, could be killed for an act that, on the night he committed it, did not carry the death penalty.

The trial opened in Leipzig on 21 September 1933 and ran until 23 December [CITE: reichsgericht-judgment-23-dec-1933-saechsisches-staatsarchiv]. It was reported across Europe. Dimitrov, defending himself from the dock in German he had taught himself in jail, cross-examined Göring and reduced the prosecution's conspiracy case to public embarrassment. Van der Lubbe sat through most of it with his head down. The court acquitted Dimitrov, Popov, Tanev, and Torgler for want of evidence. It convicted van der Lubbe alone of *Hochverrat* and arson against a public building. He was sentenced to death.

The acquittal of the four did the alibi a service the prosecution had not intended. In the foreign press the trial began to look fair. Four communists had walked. Only the man with the matches had been condemned. The international story shifted in the regime's favour: this was not a show trial; this was a court doing its work; the man who had set the fire had been convicted, the men against whom there was no case had been let go. The regime did not need to maintain the conspiracy in detail any longer. By the time of the Leipzig verdict, the Enabling Act had been operative for nine months. The decree on which it stood had been operative for ten. The justification could fade. The legal architecture would not.

On 10 January 1934, three days before his twenty-fifth birthday, van der Lubbe was guillotined in the prison courtyard at Leipzig. He had spent the trial nearly silent. He had spent the night of the fire shouting his confession.

## Goat resists

The case did not stay closed.

After 1945, with the Reich gone and its files moved into the *Bundesarchiv* and into the captured-records collection at the United States National Archives, German historians began to reconstruct what had happened in the building on the night of 27 February. The reconstruction continues. It is not, by 2026, a settled question.

The first major postwar challenge to the received story came from an unlikely place. Fritz Tobias, an official in the Lower Saxony state office for the protection of the constitution, published *Der Reichstagsbrand: Legende und Wirklichkeit* in 1962 [CITE: tobias-1962-reichstagsbrand-legende-wirklichkeit]. Tobias argued, against the prevailing Cold War-era assumption on both sides of the Iron Curtain that the Nazis had set the fire themselves, that the simplest reading of the surviving evidence was the one the regime itself had abandoned by 1934: van der Lubbe had acted alone. The thesis was unwelcome in both East Germany, where Nazi authorship of the fire was a doctrinal pillar, and in much of the West, where the convenience of Nazi authorship had hardened into common sense. Hans Mommsen, in a 1964 *Vierteljahrshefte für Zeitgeschichte* article later expanded for an English-language volume in 1972, defended Tobias's reading on different evidentiary grounds [CITE: mommsen-1964-vfz-reichstagsbrand].

Other historians disagreed. Walther Hofer led an international scholarly committee — the *Internationaler Komitee zur wissenschaftlichen Erforschung der Ursachen und Folgen des Zweiten Weltkrieges* — which from the early 1970s onwards published multivolume documentation pressing the Nazi co-conspiracy thesis [CITE: hofer-1972-reichstagsbrand-dokumentation]. In 2001 Alexander Bahar and Wilfried Kugel returned to the contesting side with *Der Reichstagsbrand: Geschichte einer Provokation*, arguing for Nazi involvement on the basis of newly available archival material [CITE: bahar-kugel-2001-reichstagsbrand-provokation].

In 2008 the journalist and historian Sven Felix Kellerhoff published *Der Reichstagsbrand: Die Karriere eines Kriminalfalls*, a synthesis drawing on Berlin Court archive material that had become available in the years immediately preceding. Kellerhoff concluded for the alone-perpetrator thesis [CITE: kellerhoff-2008-reichstagsbrand-karriere]. The Hofer committee and the Bahar–Kugel volume remain the standing counter-positions. Each side has read the available archives and reached a different conclusion; the question is open. [EVIDENCE NEEDED: Delon to verify the Tobias / Mommsen / Hofer / Kellerhoff chronology against the original bibliographies].

We do not take a side here. Whether the match was struck by one man or by several is a question of historical evidence on which qualified scholars have read the same archives and reached different conclusions; the question is open and may stay open. What is not open, and what every position in the historiographical debate agrees on, is what the regime did with the fire in the first hours and days after it occurred. The Prussian arrest orders were issued before any investigation. The conspiracy framing was announced before the fire was out. The decree was signed within hours. The four thousand arrests were made before any defendant had been charged. The Reichstag elections of 5 March were held while the KPD vote was under preventive detention. Whoever set the fire, the response was prepared. Whoever set the fire, the response did not follow from it; the response was waiting.

That is the point we rest on, and that point does not depend on resolving the historiography. The substitution would have worked whether the act was authentic or staged. The substitution is what makes the response convertible into law.

## Alibi weakens

The Reich fell in May 1945. The decree had been operative for twelve years.

The records survived. Not all of them — the regime had time to burn its files before the Red Army reached the *Reichssicherheitshauptamt* — but most of the architecture of legal administration was captured intact. The *Bundesarchiv* in Koblenz and later Berlin received the Reich Ministry of Justice files (R 3001) and the surviving Reich Security Main Office files (R 58). The Berlin Landesarchiv received the Berlin court records. The *Sächsisches Staatsarchiv* Leipzig held the *Reichsgericht* trial record itself. The United States National Archives, in College Park, held the captured German records returned in the 1950s as Record Group 242. The papers were not in one place; they did not need to be. They were durable.

In 1981 Johannes van der Lubbe, brother of the executed man, petitioned the Berlin *Landgericht* to set aside his brother's death sentence. The court did so, narrowly: the sentence had rested on a retroactive criminal law, and a retroactive criminal law was, in the postwar Federal Republic, incompatible with the elementary principles of criminal justice. The conviction itself was not, at that stage, fully annulled.

Seventeen years later, in 1998, the Bundestag passed the *Gesetz zur Aufhebung nationalsozialistischer Unrechtsurteile in der Strafrechtspflege* — the NS-AufhG, the Law on the Annulment of National Socialist Unjust Judgments — published in *Bundesgesetzblatt* I 1998 at page 2501 [CITE: bgbl-i-1998-p-2501-ns-aufhg]. The statute provided for the automatic annulment of National Socialist criminal judgments contrary to elementary principles of justice. The Bundestag amended it in 2002 and 2009 to broaden its scope. It was not specific to van der Lubbe; it was specific to the category of injustice the *Reichsgericht* had perfected. The category included him.

## Anti-laundering rule

We can go back now to the opening scene. The fire above the chamber, the bare-chested young man, the matches, the four thousand arrests by dawn. Read it again, and something the first reading did not show comes forward.

The man was visible because someone needed him to be visible. The decree was almost invisible because nobody needed to notice it for a generation. The story attached itself to the face at speed; the law attached itself to the country slowly. Both attachments were the same operation, performed at different volumes.

The altar moves. The substitution does not.

This is our rule so far, and it is not yet a method. It is a posture. When blame attaches to a single visible face faster than investigation could have produced it — within hours, before anyone has examined the building, before the medical examiner has filed, before the docket is complete — watch what is being signed in the same week. The substitution is older than any specific case, and the photograph on the front page is not the only document with a signature on it.

## The escape

In a determination dated 6 December 2007, publicly announced in a press release of 10 January 2008 (Aktenzeichen 2 AR 187/07), the Federal Prosecutor General of Germany — the *Generalbundesanwalt*, Monika Harms in office at the time — found that van der Lubbe's 1933 conviction was annulled by operation of the *Gesetz zur Aufhebung nationalsozialistischer Unrechtsurteile in der Strafrechtspflege* of 25 August 1998 (the NS-AufhG).

The Bundesanwaltschaft's own formulation was direct: *"Die Bundesanwaltschaft hat am 6. Dezember 2007 festgestellt, dass das Urteil gegen den im 'Reichstagsbrandprozess' verurteilten Marinus van der Lubbe aufgehoben ist"* — the Federal Public Prosecutor's Office determined on 6 December 2007 that the verdict against the man convicted in the Reichstag fire trial was annulled (author's translation).

The annulment rested on the fact that van der Lubbe's death sentence was grounded in two specifically National Socialist injustice provisions: the *Verordnung des Reichspräsidenten zum Schutz von Volk und Staat* of 28 February 1933, and the *Gesetz über Verhängung und Vollzug der Todesstrafe* of 29 March 1933, the so-called *Lex van der Lubbe* [CITE: generalbundesanwalt-van-der-lubbe-annulment-2008]. The conviction had rested on a retroactive criminal statute. That was the legal ground for the annulment. The annulment was automatic; the statute had been doing this work since 1998. Van der Lubbe's was one judgment among many.

This is what a partial interception of the laundering pattern looks like at its best.

The mechanism has a name. It is statutory record durability across regime change. The Federal Republic of Germany, three constitutional generations downstream of the Third Reich, legislated a category of annullable injustice and then applied the category to a specific record that had been signed by a court of the regime it had succeeded. The German state of 2008 reached back into the legal output of the German state of 1933 and unmade one of its judgments. The mechanism took three regimes — the Reich, the Federal Republic of the founding period, the reunified Federal Republic — and a continuous archival custody across all of them. It took a parliament willing to legislate the category. It took prosecutors willing to apply it. The archives survived. The lawyers were available. The legislative will was found.

It is also the kind of interception that can only be described honestly with the cost named in the same paragraph.

Seventy-five years late. Van der Lubbe was executed on 10 January 1934. The annulment came in January 2008. Three generations of his family lived and died between the two dates. He himself was beyond the reach of the document by seventy-four years.

Formal, not evidentiary. The 2008 annulment did not reopen the historical question of who lit the fire, nor did it pronounce on the substitution by which his conviction was used. It annulled the conviction on the narrow ground that it had rested on a retroactive statute incompatible with elementary justice. The deeper laundering — the use of his individual confession to authorise a constitutional emergency against four thousand other people and then against a continent — is not what the 1998 NS-AufhG addresses. The statute fixes verdicts. It cannot fix the regime built on the verdicts.

Not restorative. The four thousand KPD officials, deputies, journalists, and members arrested in Prussia on the night of 27 / 28 February 1933 were not named in the annulment, because the annulment was of van der Lubbe's judgment. Those four thousand were the first arrests under the decree. They were followed by hundreds of thousands more, and then by millions, across the twelve years the decree remained in operative legal force. The decree itself was not annulled because the decree was not a criminal judgment; it was an executive instrument issued under Article 48(2) of the Weimar Constitution. The Federal Republic could and did refuse to recognise its validity from its own founding, but the events the decree authorised had already happened. No annulment retrieves them. The legal architecture of the Reich was dismantled by defeat and occupation in 1945; the human cost it had purchased was not refundable.

The execution stands as an event. The decree's twelve operative years stand as events. Only the criminal judgment of 23 December 1933 is unmade.

This is genuine. It is also late, partial, and unrecoverable in everything it leaves untouched. Both have to be true at once, in the same sentence, or the meaning of the escape is lost.

## What this might mean for us

Step back from Berlin in 1933 and from Leipzig in 2008, and notice the position we now stand in.

We recognised the pattern. Probably before we were halfway through. The fire, the foreign man at the scene, the speed of the framing, the law signed in the same week — we have seen this rhythm before, not in a textbook but in a news cycle within the past several years. We will see it again, in a different language and a different country, within several years more. The recognition is not something we installed here. It is something we named. Which example each of us reaches for is itself part of what comes next to discipline.

What follows is about three further things, in order. The first is the recognition itself — how to tell when we are watching it happen rather than only feeling that we are. The second is the kinds of substitution that exist, because there are several and they are not interchangeable. The third is the questions to ask of any particular case to find where blame is actually doing its work. None of those are here yet. We have done one thing so far, which is to make the rhythm audible.

Three signs we can already feel.

The first is speed. When the official story arrives faster than any investigation could have produced it — hours rather than days, days rather than weeks — the story has not been derived from the event. It was waiting. The event activates it.

The second is scale mismatch. When the named cause is small relative to the consequence the cause authorises, the gap between the cause and the consequence is doing work. Something else is filling the gap. The shapes the gap takes are not identical across cases — a constitutional emergency is not a corporate settlement, and the chapters that follow will keep the differences visible — but the structural sign is the same one.

The third is absence. When the people whose names appear in the coverage of the event are not the people whose names appear on the documents signed in the days after, the visible names and the operational names have separated. The cameras are pointed at the substitute. The signatures are happening elsewhere.

These are signs, not a method. The method is in the next chapter and the one after. Recognition without method is the chapter's deliberate stopping point. You can finish this page, close the book, and have learned something true; you will not yet have learned how to use it.

One last thing.

Read it carefully, because it is the only sentence in this chapter that asks something of you directly.

If your face is the public face of a decision, and you cannot name in writing who authorised the decision above you, you are at the altar.

This is not a metaphor for some other kind of person. The named employee in the press release. The named clinician on the chart. The named local commander in the after-action report. The named junior signatory on the contract. The ancient *pharmakos* was sometimes paid for the role and was sometimes coerced into it; the modern position is the same role, with neither the ritual's openness nor the *pharmakos*'s stipend. Knowing this does not yet free you from it. But not knowing it makes the substitution easier on the people performing the rite.

The altar moved.

You already know where it is.
