# When Power Calls Itself the Goat

Evidence grade: A

## Accusation scene

On January 20, 2025, hours after the inauguration, the President signed Proclamation 10887, published in the Federal Register on January 29, 2025. The proclamation granted "a full, complete and unconditional pardon to all other individuals convicted of offenses related to events that occurred at or near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021," and commuted to time served the sentences of fourteen named individuals, including the convicted seditious-conspiracy defendants from the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys [CITE: proclamation-10887-j6-pardons-2025-01-20]. The text of the proclamation describes the act as ending "a grave national injustice that has been perpetrated upon the American people over the last four years."

In the weeks that followed, the Department of Justice moved to dismiss the remaining January 6 cases. Reporting in Reuters, the Washington Post, and the New York Times documented that the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia removed the Capitol Breach case-tracking dashboard from its public-facing pages [CITE: j6-prosecution-dismissal-coverage-2025] and that career line prosecutors who had worked the January 6 docket were terminated or reassigned [CITE: doj-j6-prosecutor-osc-mspb-complaints-pending — pending-stephen-lock]. The Capitol Police Labor Committee issued a statement on January 21, 2025 [CITE: capitol-police-labor-committee-statement-2025-01-21].

On March 14, 2025, the President signed Proclamation 10903, published in the Federal Register on March 20, 2025, invoking the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 against the Venezuelan organization Tren de Aragua and authorizing the summary removal of Venezuelan nationals identified as members [CITE: proclamation-10903-alien-enemies-act-2025-03-14; alien-enemies-act-50-usc-21-24]. The statute had been invoked three times before in American history: the War of 1812, World War I, and World War II.

On March 15, 2025, the United States government conducted removal flights to El Salvador. According to reconstructions in the Associated Press, ProPublica, and CBS News, approximately 137 Venezuelan nationals were transferred to the Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo, known as CECOT, a maximum-security facility operated under President Nayib Bukele's "estado de excepción" detention program [CITE: cecot-march-15-2025-removal-flights-coverage]. On one of those flights, the government later acknowledged in court filings in Abrego Garcia v. Noem in the District of Maryland, was Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia, a Maryland resident with a 2019 immigration-judge order forbidding his removal to El Salvador [CITE: abrego-garcia-v-noem-d-md-complaint-2025].

Each of these three acts was publicly defended as the correction of a prior wrong.

## Official story

The official frame is consistent across the three acts and forms a single argument.

The January 6 pardons, in the language of the proclamation itself, "end a grave national injustice." Public statements from administration officials in early 2025, as indexed at whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/, characterized the prosecutions as politically motivated and described convicted January 6 defendants using language including the term "hostages" [CITE: proclamation-10887-j6-pardons-2025-01-20; admin-statements-j6-hostages-framing-2025].

The records litigation against the Department of Government Efficiency was, in the administration's filings and public communications, an obstruction of an authorized reform program. Executive Order 14158, signed January 20, 2025 and published in the Federal Register on January 29, 2025, established the U.S. DOGE Service to "modernize federal technology and software to maximize governmental efficiency and productivity" [CITE: executive-order-14158-doge-2025-01-20]. When Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington filed CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service, No. 1:25-cv-00511, in the District Court for the District of Columbia, the administration's position was that the new service was not an "agency" subject to the Freedom of Information Act, that records of personnel embedded inside other agencies belonged to those agencies, and that encrypted-messaging use was not official business requiring preservation [CITE: crew-v-doge-service-ddc-complaint-2025-02-20].

The Alien Enemies Act invocation, in the language of Proclamation 10903, responded to an "invasion." Subsequent administration communications indexed at whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/ characterized the removed individuals using language including "terrorists" and "the worst of the worst," and senior administration officials publicly criticized federal judges who enforced due-process limits, in some instances using language including "activist judges" [CITE: proclamation-10903-alien-enemies-act-2025-03-14; admin-statements-aea-worst-of-the-worst-activist-judges-2025].

The three frames stack into one move. In each case, an accountability mechanism — prosecutions, records statutes, due-process review — is named as the prior abuse, and dismantling the mechanism is named as the correction. The actor with control of the executive branch is positioned as the goat-bearing reformer; the institutions that exist to check that actor are positioned as the wrongdoers.

This is not a misreading of the public communications. It is what the public communications, on the record, claim.

## Hidden architecture

The case to walk through is the Alien Enemies Act invocation, because it produced the cleanest record-hardening pair. The eight-question diagnostic applies as written.

**Who was publicly blamed?** Abrego Garcia, characterized by the administration in post-removal statements as a member of the MS-13 gang, with the characterization disputed in filings in Abrego Garcia v. Noem [CITE: admin-statements-aea-worst-of-the-worst-activist-judges-2025; abrego-garcia-v-noem-d-md-complaint-2025]. The 2019 immigration-judge order on file did not find him to be a gang member; the withholding-of-removal grant was based on a particularized threat against him by gangs in his home town of Los Nogales, San Salvador, as reflected in subsequent court filings [CITE: abrego-garcia-2019-withholding-of-removal-order]. The approximately 137 other Venezuelan nationals removed on March 15, 2025, were each alleged by the government to be members of Tren de Aragua; the allegations have not been individuated to adjudicated finding as of May 2026, per the J.G.G. v. Trump docket and parallel proceedings [CITE: j-g-g-v-trump-ddc-complaint-2025-03-15].

**Who had control?** The executive branch. Proclamation 10903 was the operative act. The Department of Homeland Security executed the removals. Immigration and Customs Enforcement constructed the manifests, filed into the J.G.G. v. Trump and Abrego Garcia v. Noem dockets [CITE: proclamation-10903-alien-enemies-act-2025-03-14; j-g-g-v-trump-ddc-complaint-2025-03-15; abrego-garcia-v-noem-d-md-complaint-2025].

**Who benefited?** Three layers. The administration's enforcement-volume narrative; the broader precedent of an eighteenth-century wartime statute being available for peacetime removal; the bilateral arrangement under which Salvadoran authorities received the transferred persons, a partial-disclosure record as of May 2026 with state.gov as institutional anchor and no formal instrument text yet released [CITE: bilateral-arrangement-cecot-partial-record-2025].

**Who had documented knowledge?** The 2019 withholding-of-removal order was in the federal immigration-record system. The government's own subsequent court filings in Abrego Garcia v. Noem characterized the removal of Abrego Garcia as an "administrative error" [CITE: abrego-garcia-v-noem-d-md-complaint-2025]. The chapter does not infer mental states; it cites the documented record.

**Who could have prevented?** The same officials who executed the removal. The withholding order existed in the system to be checked. Statute and constitutional due process required individuated process before designation as "alien enemy" — a point later codified in the Supreme Court's per curiam order in Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931 (Apr. 7, 2025) [CITE: trump-v-jgg-24a931-per-curiam-2025-04-07].

**Who controlled the record?** Until federal judges intervened, the executive branch. After March 15, control passed in part to the courts: the District Court for the District of Columbia, the District of Maryland, the Western and Southern Districts of Texas, the Fourth Circuit, and the Supreme Court each entered orders on the public docket. The 2019 withholding order was the durable counter-record the executive branch could not vacate.

**Who bore the cost?** Abrego Garcia, removed contrary to a binding immigration order and detained at CECOT. His wife, Jennifer Vasquez Sura, and three United States-citizen children, remaining in Maryland. The approximately 137 other men on the March 15 flights, removed and detained without individuated process. The institutional record of withholding-of-removal adjudication itself, demonstrated to be operationally overridable.

**What would responsibility look like if it followed control?** It climbs. From the field officers who executed the manifests to the Department of Homeland Security operational chain to the Secretary of Homeland Security as signatory of the operational regime to the proclamation's signatory. The chain does not stop at "administrative error."

The diagnostic does not depend on whether the framing of the removed persons is true or false. The diagnostic asks who controls, who benefits, who had documented access to the record, who could have prevented, who bears the cost. The questions yield the same chain whether the answer to the framing question is yes, no, or contested.

## Older echo

This pattern is older than the second-term administration. The two A-grade historical echoes that follow are the instances with the deepest documentary record across past US administrations; the pattern is structural across executive branches, regardless of party. The same diagnostic, applied to any administration's framing of accountability mechanisms as the prior abuse, returns the same answer.

**Watergate.** On August 5, 1974, the Department of Justice's Office of Legal Counsel issued a memorandum titled "Presidential or Legislative Pardon of the President," concluding that "under the fundamental rule that no one may be a judge in his own case, the President cannot pardon himself" [CITE: olc-presidential-self-pardon-memo-1974-08-05 — pending-stephen-lock]. The memorandum is on the OLC public record. The episode it addressed was an internal discussion of whether the sitting President could pardon himself before resigning. Earlier, on October 20, 1973, in the act later called the Saturday Night Massacre, the President had ordered the firing of Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox; the public framing accompanying the firing characterized Cox's investigation as itself the abuse. The structural move — the prosecution recast as the persecution — is in the public record fifty years before the present administration.

**Iran-Contra.** On December 24, 1992, the outgoing President signed Proclamation 6518, pardoning former Secretary of Defense Caspar Weinberger and five others connected to the Iran-Contra investigation [CITE: proclamation-6518-bush-iran-contra-pardons-1992-12-24]. Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh, in his Final Report of the Independent Counsel for Iran/Contra Matters, dated August 4, 1993, wrote that the pardons "completed the cover-up" of the Iran-Contra affair [CITE: walsh-final-report-iran-contra-1993-08-04 — pending-stephen-lock]. The contemporaneous record from earlier in the investigation, documented in the Tower Commission Report of February 1987 and the Senate Iran-Contra hearings of 1987, included destruction of records by named subordinates, including Lt. Col. Oliver North's shredding of relevant documents in November 1986 [CITE: tower-commission-report-1987-02]. The accountability mechanism — an Independent Counsel — was, in the political register of the period, characterized as itself a partisan persecution.

The two echoes are different administrations, different parties, different decades, different statutes. They share one structural shape: the accountability mechanism is named as the prior laundering, and dismantling the mechanism is named as the correction. The shape does not belong to one party. The diagnostic predates the second term of the current administration and has historically applied across the aisle.

That history is the reason the diagnostic survives. The diagnostic asks about control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, cost. The questions yield the same answers whether the proclamation is signed by a Democrat or a Republican. The eight-question walk did not change between 1974 and 1992 and 2025. The case files changed.

## Record hardens

What hardened in 2025 was not opinion. It was record entries.

The proclamations themselves entered the Federal Register with timestamps that cannot be revised. Proclamation 10887 at 90 Fed. Reg. 8331, January 29, 2025. Executive Order 14158 at 90 Fed. The dockets accumulated.

CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service, No. 1:25-cv-00511, was filed February 20, 2025 in the District Court for the District of Columbia, before Judge Christopher R. Cooper [CITE: crew-v-doge-service-ddc-complaint-2025-02-20].

J.G.G. v. Trump, No. 1:25-cv-00766, was filed March 15, 2025 in the same court, before Chief Judge James E. Boasberg [CITE: j-g-g-v-trump-ddc-complaint-2025-03-15].

Abrego Garcia v. Noem, No. 8:25-cv-00951, was filed 2025 in the District Court for the District of Maryland, before Judge Paula Xinis [CITE: abrego-garcia-v-noem-d-md-complaint-2025].

American Oversight v. DOGE, Musk, Davis, Gleason, and Rubio was filed 2025 in the District Court for the District of Columbia, alleging use of Signal and Google Docs to circumvent the Federal Records Act [CITE: american-oversight-v-doge-musk-fra-signal-2025 — pending-stephen-lock].

The court orders accumulated.

In March 2025, Judge Cooper entered an order in CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service directing preservation of DOGE records and finding plaintiffs likely to succeed on the agency-status question. Subsequent orders in the same case elaborated the agency analysis, addressing whether the U.S. DOGE Service "wields substantial authority independently of the President" [CITE: docket entries and stage-specific orders pending Stephen verification].

On April 7, 2025, the Supreme Court issued a per curiam order in Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931. The order vacated the District Court's temporary restraining order on venue grounds and required that detainees challenging removal under the Alien Enemies Act proceed by habeas corpus in the place of detention [CITE: 24A931 per curiam].

The order was not unanimous. Justice Sotomayor filed a dissent, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, and joined in part by Justice Barrett. The dissent argued among other things that the venue holding rewarded the government's conduct in the original removals and disregarded the proper procedural posture; it is part of the same docket entry [CITE: 24A931, Sotomayor, J., dissenting].

On April 10, 2025, the Supreme Court issued a per curiam order in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949. The order directed the government to "facilitate" Abrego Garcia's release from custody in El Salvador and "to ensure that his case is handled as it would have been had he not been improperly sent to El Salvador" [CITE: 24A949 per curiam]. Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, wrote a separate statement [CITE: same order].

On April 16, 2025, Chief Judge Boasberg issued a finding of probable cause for criminal contempt in connection with the March 15 TRO [CITE: J.G.G. docket entry, Apr. 16, 2025].

On April 17, 2025, the Fourth Circuit declined to stay Judge Xinis's order [CITE: Abrego Garcia v. Noem, 4th Cir. order, Apr. 17, 2025].

The personnel record accumulated. Career line prosecutors who had worked the January 6 docket filed complaints with the Office of Special Counsel and the Merit Systems Protection Board alleging that their terminations were retaliatory personnel actions prohibited by 5 U.S.C. § 2302(b)(8); the matters are pending as of May 2026 [CITE: complaints pending; Stephen to lock prosecutor names and complaint numbers per Delon's verification].

On the right, the entries the same period made into the Federal Register, the federal court dockets, and the United States Reports. Arrows from right to left mark which entry contradicts which framing.

The same institutions the administration's communications named as obstructions issued the entries.

## Goat resists

A serious chapter must take the strongest defenses of the official frame at full strength. There are three.

**The pardon power is plenary.** The Constitution states, at Article II, Section 2, Clause 1, that the President "shall have Power to grant Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment." The clause is textually unconditional within the federal-offense domain. The Framers debated and rejected limits on the pardon power at the Constitutional Convention [CITE: Records of the Federal Convention, 1787].

The Supreme Court has sustained the breadth of the power across three principal cases: Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. 333 (1866); Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79 (1915); and Schick v. Reed, 419 U.S. 256 (1974). Mass clemency has historical precedent. President Carter's 1977 Proclamation 4483 pardoned approximately 200,000 Vietnam-era draft offenders [CITE: Proclamation 4483, Jan. 21, 1977]. President Andrew Johnson's 1868 Christmas pardon proclamation extended amnesty for Civil War offenses [CITE: Proclamation of Dec. 25, 1868]. Scholars who study clemency — Margaret Love, Mark Osler, others — have argued that the pardon power's breadth is not a bug but a constitutional feature, designed precisely so that the political branch can correct what it views as injustice without judicial leave [CITE: Love and Osler scholarship; secondary B].

Calling lawful clemency "laundering" risks collapsing legality and propriety. That collapse would itself be an analytic error.

The book does not collapse them. The diagnostic the book applies is not a legal test. It is a forensic walk: who controlled, who benefited, who had documented knowledge, who could have prevented, who controlled the record, who bore the cost. The pardon power being plenary does not exempt its exercise from the diagnostic. It locates the chain at the signatory. The proclamation's constitutional authority is conceded entirely. The chapter's claim is narrower: when a constitutional act is publicly defended as the correction of a prior abuse by the institutions that act would also dissolve, the diagnostic does not stop at the signature line.

**The Presidential Records Act and the Federal Records Act have historically been weakly enforced.** The administration's position on the U.S. DOGE Service's agency status has serious legal support. In Kissinger v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, 445 U.S. 136 (1980), the Supreme Court held that the Executive Office of the President includes components not subject to FOIA as agencies. In Armstrong v. Executive Office of the President, 1 F.3d 1274 (D.C. Cir. 1993), and in the long Judicial Watch v. NARA litigation, courts have historically declined to compel records preservation against the executive in real time. From this angle, the DOGE litigation may be reasserting a long-dormant judicial role rather than naming a fresh abuse.

The chapter's response is narrower than the steelman's territory. The chapter does not claim the agency-status question is settled against the administration. As of May 2026, the question is contested on appeal. The chapter claims something specific: calling enforcement of the Federal Records Act itself the overreach inverts the diagnostic. The traditional weakness of records-statute enforcement is exactly what makes that inversion effective. A long-dormant law cannot be "obstruction" of a new agenda; if anything, the law's dormancy is what allowed the practices the litigation is now contesting.

**The Alien Enemies Act is a statute on the books.** Enacted in 1798, never repealed, used in 1812, in World War I, and in World War II. Courts have historically applied substantial deference to executive judgments about foreign threats. Korematsu v. United States, 323 U.S. 214 (1944), is the often-cited precedent for wartime deference, though the Supreme Court repudiated Korematsu in the relevant respect in Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). From this angle, the March 14, 2025 invocation may be legally available even if its peacetime application is novel.

The chapter does not argue that the statute is dead. The chapter argues that the diagnostic still climbs. The Supreme Court itself, in Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931 (Apr. 7, 2025), held that detainees are entitled to habeas review before removal under the act. The Supreme Court itself, in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949 (Apr. 10, 2025), unanimously per curiam ordered the government to facilitate the return of a person removed in violation of an immigration-judge order. The deference the steelman invokes is the deference the Supreme Court has narrowed within the same period the proclamation was issued. The statute being live does not exempt its invocation from the eight-question walk.

The three steelmen survive. The chapter does not contest them. The diagnostic survives them because the diagnostic does not occupy that territory. It asks a different set of questions, and the answers climb regardless of how the constitutional questions ultimately resolve.

## Alibi weakens

The same institutions the official frame had named as obstructions issued the entries that contradicted the frame.

In Trump v. J.G.G., 24A931, decided April 7, 2025, the Supreme Court per curiam vacated the District of Columbia TRO on venue grounds [CITE: 24A931 per curiam]. The same order required habeas corpus in the place of detention before any further Alien Enemies Act removal. The Court did not address the merits of the invocation. It did reset the procedural minimum: due process, in habeas form, before removal.

Three days later, on April 10, 2025, the Supreme Court issued the per curiam order in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, 24A949 [CITE: 24A949 per curiam]. The Court directed that the government "facilitate" Abrego Garcia's release from CECOT custody. The Court directed that his case be handled "as it would have been had he not been improperly sent to El Salvador." The order was unanimous on the central holding. Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, added a separate statement.

In the District Court for the District of Columbia, Judge Cooper had entered orders in CREW v. U.S. DOGE Service in March 2025 directing preservation of DOGE records, and subsequent orders in the case addressed the FOIA-applicability and agency-status questions [CITE: docket entries and stage-specific orders pending Stephen verification].

In the District Court for the District of Maryland, Judge Xinis ordered the government to "facilitate and effectuate" Abrego Garcia's return [CITE: Abrego Garcia v. Noem, D. Md.]. The Fourth Circuit declined to stay the order on April 17, 2025 [CITE: 4th Cir. order, Apr. 17, 2025]. On April 16, 2025, Chief Judge Boasberg entered the probable-cause-for-contempt finding [CITE: J.G.G. docket]. In subsequent proceedings in the Western and Southern Districts of Texas, district-court judges — including judges appointed by the current President — entered orders restricting Alien Enemies Act use on due-process grounds [CITE: W.D. Tex. and S.D. Tex. rulings, May 2025 onward, carried in card wd-sd-tex-aea-due-process-rulings-2025 with courtlistener.com as institutional anchor; per-case captions and Trump-appointee identification pending PACER lock].

At the Department of Justice, the prosecutors who had been removed produced records on their way out: complaints filed with the Office of Special Counsel, complaints filed with the Merit Systems Protection Board, public statements where permitted, declarations entered into civil dockets where required [CITE: pending OSC/MSPB complaints; Stephen to lock]. Whatever else the personnel actions accomplished, they generated a documentary trail that was not under the control of the institution that issued them.

The pattern is what the chapter said it would be. The mechanisms named as the prior abuse — the courts, the records statutes, the personnel-protection regime — produced the contemporaneous record by which the framing can be tested. The mechanism is not the laundering. The mechanism is the surface against which the framing fails to hold.

## Anti-laundering rule

The chapter's rule is one sentence. Apply it as a protocol, not as a slogan.

**The accountability mechanism is not the laundering. Calling the mechanism the laundering IS the laundering.**

This is the only sentence in the chapter in which the term "responsibility laundering" is used by name. The term has been earned at this point in the book. What this chapter adds is the inversion case: when the actor with control publicly reframes the mechanisms that exist to surface that control as themselves the prior abuse, the diagnostic does not collapse. It transfers.

Run the eight-question walk on the act of reframing. Ask:

- Who controls the reframing?
- Who benefits from the reframing?
- Who had documented access to the record the mechanism would have surfaced?
- Who could have prevented the harm the mechanism was designed to prevent?
- Who bears the cost of the mechanism's removal?

The questions about who controls the proclamation, who benefits from the proclamation, who had documented knowledge before the proclamation, and who bears the cost of the proclamation are the same questions that climbed before. The frame inverted; the chain did not. The diagnostic survives the inversion because the questions ask about the underlying chain, not about which side of a public argument is speaking.

## The escape

The counter-case for this chapter is in the case itself. The escape, in real time, is the record of the same period.

Within twenty-six days of the Alien Enemies Act proclamation, the Supreme Court of the United States entered two per curiam orders. Trump v. J.G.G. issued on April 7. Noem v. Abrego Garcia issued on April 10. Together the two orders narrowed the act's operational scope and directed the government to facilitate the return of a person removed in violation of an immigration order [CITE: 24A931; 24A949].

Within twenty days of the executive order establishing the U.S. DOGE Service, an Article III judge entered a preliminary injunction ruling the service likely subject to FOIA [CITE: Cooper, Mar. 10, 2025]. Within roughly a month of the March 15 removal flights, a chief judge of the District Court for the District of Columbia entered a finding of probable cause for criminal contempt [CITE: J.G.G. docket, Apr. 16, 2025].

The mechanism that worked: Article III judges and per curiam unanimity, issuing time-stamped orders against the proclamation chain, fast.

The escape is partial. As of May 2026, Abrego Garcia is the subject of continuing procedural posture: he was returned to U.S. custody in June 2025, then re-detained; the procedural status is in continuing motion as of the chapter's evidence cutoff [CITE: Stephen to lock current docket position]. The approximately 137 other men removed on the March 15 flights are not, on the public record as of the same date, restored to the position they would have occupied absent the proclamation. The personnel actions at the Department of Justice are pending in OSC and MSPB venues; restoration is not yet ordered. The records-preservation litigation is on appeal in multiple proceedings.

The cost of the intercept is institutional confrontation that continues across an entire branch of government. Article III judges, the Supreme Court included, have issued orders against the executive branch's framing of its own acts; the executive branch, in public communications, has characterized those orders as themselves overreach. This is what an intercept of a chapter-9 pattern looks like in real time: the mechanism documented in the record, the cost documented in the same record, the result partial.

The counter-case is the case. The chapter is not making a prediction. It is reporting what the docket says.

## What this means for you

The chapter's reader-level test is not partisan. The test is structural, and it is administration-agnostic.

The first test concerns the claim that a given prosecution was the real abuse. The questions the diagnostic raises are these: what specific record did the prosecution surface that the speaker would prefer buried; who controlled that record before the prosecution; whose conduct did the prosecution make public; is that conduct, separately from the prosecution, documented elsewhere. The diagnostic is not whether the prosecution was conducted well or badly. The diagnostic is whether the speaker is using "abuse" to describe the prosecution or to describe what the prosecution revealed.

The second test concerns the claim that a given records statute is obstruction. The questions are these: what specific record the statute would have preserved; who controls that record now; whether the speaker is in the chain that would be visible if the record survived. The diagnostic is not whether the statute is well-drafted. The diagnostic is whether the speaker's argument with the statute is an argument about good government or an argument about the speaker's visibility.

The third test concerns the claim that a given judicial order is judicial overreach. The questions are these: what specific executive act the order would have constrained; whose conduct the order names; whether the speaker is in the chain the order would reach. The diagnostic is not whether judges are infallible. The diagnostic is whether the speaker's complaint with the order is about judicial method or about being named.

These three tests work the same regardless of which side of an aisle the speaker is standing on. They worked in 1974, when the prosecution was characterized as the abuse and the abuse was characterized as the prosecution. They worked in 1992, when an Independent Counsel was characterized as a partisan apparatus and the pardons were characterized as the correction. They work in 2025, applied to the second-term proclamations and the orders that contradicted them. They will work in 2029 and 2033 and beyond, under any administration, because they do not ask which side is speaking. They ask about control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control, and cost.

If a reader applies the test in good faith to one administration but refuses to apply it to another, the test has been put down. The diagnostic does not protect the reader from that choice. The diagnostic only describes what climbing the chain looks like when the chain is climbed.

The chapter's instruction is therefore the same as the rule above. When the language of accountability and the language of abuse trade places in a public argument, do not adjudicate the trade by tone. Run the walk. The chain still climbs.
