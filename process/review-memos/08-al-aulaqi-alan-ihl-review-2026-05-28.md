## Question 1 — Is the §1119(b) "public authority justification" a recognized doctrine or a bespoke construction?

**Verdict: BOTH, and the precise split matters. The chapter must not call it either one flatly.**

The public-authority justification *as a general principle of criminal law* is genuinely recognized: it is the long-standing doctrine that a government official acting within lawful authority does not commit the crime an identical private act would constitute (the police officer who shoots within the lawful contours of the job; the soldier who kills a lawful combatant). "Lawful conduct of war" is a recognized variant of it. On this, the steelman is correct and the chapter must concede it.

What is **contested — and was contested by serious, non-partisan experts at the time of release** — is the OLC memo's *application* of that general doctrine to *this statute and this actor*:

- §1119(b) contains **no express public-authority exception**. The memo *reads one in* by construing the statute to reach only "unlawful" killings and then placing the operation in the "lawful conduct of war" box. That interpretive move (statute is silent → exception implied → operation fits the implied exception) is a construction, not a recital of settled law. Critics argued the public-authority justification was historically narrow ("fire trucks breaking the speed limit") and that expanding it this way risks justifying broad executive action by interpretation.
- The sharpest documented expert critique is **actor-specific**: even if the AUMF supplies the *military* a public-authority basis to override §1119, the memo's extension of the same justification to the **CIA** was widely questioned (Kevin Jon Heller, Opinio Juris: "we still don't know where the CIA's public authority comes from"). The strike that killed al-Aulaqi is generally understood to have been a CIA/JSOC operation; the CIA is not obviously a body to which "lawful conduct of war" public authority attaches in the same way the armed forces are.

**Strength language Wayne SHOULD use:**
- "The public-authority justification is a recognized principle of criminal law — a government official acting within lawful authority does not commit the crime an identical private act would. The memo's contested move was to read that justification *into* §1119, which contains no express exception, and to extend it to the operation contemplated here." [CITE: barron-olc-al-aulaqi-targeting-memo-2010-07-16]
- If the chapter wants one sentence of contestation (recommended, and rule-07 compliant): "Legal scholars at the time questioned whether the justification stretched that far — particularly as applied to a CIA operation rather than the armed forces."

**Formulations to AVOID:**
- "The public-authority justification is a recognized doctrine" *full stop, with no qualifier* — this is the steelman's phrasing and it over-credits by eliding the application gap. UNDER-claim error (cedes too much).
- "The OLC simply invented a defense" / "a made-up exception" — OVER-claim error. The *general* doctrine is real; only its application is contested. This phrasing would itself fail the book's own evidence discipline and hand the steelman a kill-shot.
- Any verb imputing to Barron an awareness that the construction was wrong (rule 05). The honest claim is *contested construction*, not *bad-faith construction*.

## Question 2 — AUMF-as-armed-conflict: mainstream, contested, or split?

**Verdict: SPLIT — and the split has two distinct layers the chapter must not collapse.**

This is the single place where the steelman as drafted is strongest *and* where it most needs bounding. Two separate propositions hide inside "the AUMF placed the US in a NIAC with AQAP reaching Yemen":

**Layer 2a — Is there a NIAC with al-Qaeda / associated forces at all?** *Largely mainstream by 2010–2011, but not unanimous.* The Obama administration, much of the US legal academy, and a substantial body of IHL scholarship accepted that the US was in a non-international armed conflict with al-Qaeda and "associated forces." Note a frame caution: the AUMF is a *domestic* statute; it cannot by itself *create* an *IHL* armed conflict (a NIAC exists as a matter of fact under IHL — organization + intensity thresholds — not because Congress says so). The accurate characterization is that the AUMF supplied *domestic* authority, and the administration asserted that the *factual* IHL conditions for a NIAC were met. The chapter should not write as though a statute conjured an armed conflict into legal existence.

**Layer 2b — Does that conflict "reach Yemen" / extend to a target far from the Afghan "hot battlefield"?** *Genuinely contested — this is the live fault line, both-with-a-split.* There is a substantial, named scholarly debate on the **geography of NIAC**:
- One camp (e.g., Michael Schmitt, "Charting the Legal Geography of NIAC") holds the conflict travels with the parties — a member of an organized armed group remains targetable wherever found, not only inside a defined zone.
- Another camp (e.g., Jennifer Daskal, "The Geography of the Battlefield"; Kenneth Anderson framing the "legal geography of war" question itself) holds that targeting *outside* the hot battlefield demands additional constraints (closer to a law-enforcement/self-defense imminence-and-feasibility test), precisely because unbounded geography risks a borderless war.
- Even Jack Goldsmith — former OLC head, no dove — has said the AUMF was "losing its efficacy" and that the US was engaged in warfare "the nation hasn't openly debated."

**Strength language Wayne SHOULD use:**
- "The administration's legal position rested on the premise that the 2001 AUMF authorized force against al-Qaeda and associated forces including AQAP, and that the United States was in a non-international armed conflict reaching Yemen. The first half of that premise was widely (not universally) accepted; the second — that the conflict extended to a target far from any conventional battlefield — was, and remains, genuinely contested among IHL scholars." [CITE: barron-olc-al-aulaqi-targeting-memo-2010-07-16]
- If compressing to one clause: "...a contested premise that the armed conflict extended to Yemen, far from any conventional battlefield."

**Formulations to AVOID:**
- "The AUMF placed the US in an armed conflict with AQAP in Yemen" stated as settled fact — OVER-claim. It launders a contested geographic-scope position into a given. (Ironically, exactly the move the book diagnoses.)
- "The AUMF created a non-international armed conflict" — frame error; a statute supplies domestic authority, it does not constitute an IHL armed conflict.
- "Most legal scholars rejected the Yemen framing" — OVER-claim in the other direction; it was a real split, not a rout. Do not under-claim the steelman here either.

## Question 3 — The steelman's IHL core: "citizenship confers no immunity from lawful targeting in armed conflict" — correct, and at what strength? Where are the limits?

**Verdict: The IHL proposition is CORRECTLY STATED and must be carried at FULL strength as far as it goes — but it is narrower than the steelman's surrounding sentence implies, and its three real limits are exactly where the case lives.**

**The core proposition is sound IHL.** Under IHL, targetability turns on a person's *status or conduct* (membership in an organized armed group with a continuous combat function, or direct participation in hostilities), **not on nationality**. A person who is a lawful target does not acquire immunity by holding the targeting state's passport. This is correct, it is not seriously contested *as a pure IHL proposition*, and the chapter must concede it cleanly. Carrying it weakly would be an under-claim and would make the chapter's eventual point look like it depends on a citizenship-immunity claim it does not need.

**But the proposition does NOT, by itself, establish that al-Aulaqi was lawfully targetable.** It only establishes that *if* he was a lawful target, his US citizenship was no bar *under IHL*. Three limits carry the entire real dispute — and the chapter should name them as the limits of the steelman, not as accusations:

1. **The status/conduct predicate was never adversarially tested.** The whole IHL proposition is conditional on al-Aulaqi being a lawful target (continuous combat function / DPH). That predicate rests on the government's untested factual characterization ("operational AQAP leader"). Per Stephen's gap #1, this must be attributed to the government, never asserted. The steelman's "on the government's evidence, a legitimate military target" is the honest phrasing — note the "on the government's evidence."

2. **Imminence and feasibility-of-capture are contested, fact-dependent, and largely a *domestic*/jus-ad-bellum-flavored overlay, not pure IHL.** The memo's *Mathews* due-process analysis and its "continued and imminent threat / capture infeasible" conditions are constraints the *administration itself imposed* (and the leaked white paper notoriously elasticized "imminent"). Under pure IHL targeting law, a lawful target may be struck without an imminence showing; the imminence/feasibility test is a *self-imposed and constitutionally-driven* limit, the contours of which experts disputed. The chapter should not present imminence as a settled IHL requirement, nor as plainly satisfied.

3. **Geographic scope (the Question-2b limit) re-enters here.** The citizenship-no-immunity rule presupposes the person is within the conflict's reach. If one doubts the conflict extended to Yemen (the live split), the clean IHL targeting frame weakens and the law-enforcement/imminence frame strengthens — which is why critics argued the lawfulness "depends entirely on facts the memo, written ~14 months beforehand, could not analyse" (imminence, no-feasible-alternative, proportionality).

**Strength language Wayne SHOULD use (carry strong, bound precisely):**
- Full-strength concession: "Under the law of armed conflict, a lawful target's nationality confers no immunity; a US passport does not shield a person who is otherwise a lawful target in an armed conflict. On that point the government's defenders are right."
- Immediately bounded (rule 07 — name the limit, do not imply guilt): "What that principle does *not* settle is whether al-Aulaqi was a lawful target in the first place — a characterization that rested on the government's own evidence and was never tested in court — nor whether the conflict reached Yemen, nor whether the imminence and capture-infeasibility conditions the administration set for itself were met. Those questions never reached adversarial review."

**Formulations to AVOID:**
- Stating or implying that citizenship *should* have conferred immunity — that is bad IHL and not the book's claim.
- Letting "legitimate military target" stand unattributed — collapses the untested predicate into a finding (rule 05/07; Stephen gap #1).
- Presenting "imminent threat / capture infeasible" as either a settled IHL rule or as a satisfied fact. It is a self-imposed, contested standard.
- Treating the IHL targeting frame and the domestic *Mathews* due-process frame as one thing. They are two distinct legal tracks the memo bridges; the bridge is part of what makes it a single closed instrument.

## Question 4 — Is the book's framing defensible: the laundering diagnostic turns on control/benefit/knowledge/preventability and on the opinion detaching a discretionary lethal decision from external/adversarial review — NOT on the strike's legality?

**Verdict: YES — fully defensible, and it is the ONLY defensible framing given the doctrinal contestation above. This is the correction that protects the whole chapter.**

Everything in Questions 1–3 shows that the strike's *legality* is genuinely contested and was never adjudicated. That is precisely why the diagnostic must not rest on it. A laundering claim that depended on "the strike was illegal" would be a C-grade claim wearing A-grade clothes, and would collapse under the steelman. The framing the card and Stephen's memo already adopt — control/benefit/knowledge/preventability + the opinion-as-instrument detaching a discretionary lethal decision from external review — is **independent of the legality question** and survives regardless of how the legality debate resolves. That independence is the chapter's strongest ground.

Two precision notes so the framing holds at full doctrinal strength:

- **The "detachment from review" claim is doubly documented and should be carried at A-strength** — but as *structure*, not motive. Two independent facts establish it without any mental-state attribution: (a) the memo was classified and withheld for years, released only on court order (2d Cir. 2014); and (b) both lawsuits were dismissed on threshold grounds — standing/political-question pre-strike (*Al-Aulaqi v. Obama*), special-factors post-strike (*Al-Aulaqi v. Panetta*) — so no court ever tested the government's factual case adversarially. "No court reached the merits" is an A-grade structural fact, not an inference. Carry it hard.
- **Keep the IHL/domestic distinction visible in the diagnostic.** The detachment-from-review point is about *adversarial/external* review (courts, public). It is NOT a claim that the decision had *no* internal legal vetting — it plainly did (that is what the memo *is*). The honest and sharper claim is that the review was *internal to, and by, the branch that would act and benefit*, and was structurally insulated from *external* test. That is the laundering structure, and it is true whether or not the internal vetting was done in good faith and whether or not the strike was lawful.

**Strength language Wayne SHOULD use:**
- "Whether the strike was lawful was never decided — the memo's reasoning is contested, and no court reached the merits before or after. The diagnostic here does not depend on the answer. It turns on a different question: who controlled the decision, who benefited from the authority it established, and whether the instrument that authorized a citizen's killing was ever exposed to review outside the branch that wielded it." [CITE: barron-olc-al-aulaqi-targeting-memo-2010-07-16; al-aulaqi-v-obama-ddc-pre-strike-dismissal-2010; al-aulaqi-v-panetta-ddc-post-strike-dismissal-2014]

**Formulations to AVOID:**
- Any phrasing that makes the diagnostic ride on illegality ("an unlawful killing dressed up as law").
- Any phrasing that denies internal legal vetting occurred ("no legal review at all") — false and hands the steelman a point. The claim is *external/adversarial* insulation, not absence of review.

## Bottom line for the steelman's strength (per over-rule 4)

The steelman in the Loki packet §6 is doctrinally the **strongest in the chapter's cross-administration set — stronger than the Bybee/Yoo counter** — and Stephen is right that it must be carried more strongly than Bybee/Yoo. Confirmed. The reason it is stronger: torture faces an *absolute, exceptionless* prohibition, so the Bybee/Yoo memos had to *redefine the prohibited act* to escape it; targeted killing in armed conflict is *lawful in principle*, so the al-Aulaqi memo is applying a real body of law, not redefining around an absolute bar. That asymmetry is real and the chapter must concede it openly.

But "stronger" is bounded by Questions 1–3: the steelman is strong on the *general* principles (public-authority exists; lawful-conduct-of-war exists; citizenship is no IHL immunity) and weak/contested on every *application* that would establish THIS strike as lawful (public authority read into §1119 and extended to the CIA; the conflict reaching Yemen; imminence/feasibility; the untested target predicate). The chapter carries the steelman at full strength on the principles, then shows that the principles do not reach the case without traversing contested ground that was never externally tested — and that the diagnostic does not need that ground resolved either way.
