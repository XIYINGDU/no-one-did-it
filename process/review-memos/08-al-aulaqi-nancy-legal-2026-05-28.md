Every wording decision below flows from one fact pattern, now web-verified:

## 2. David J. Barron — the sitting-judge problem

**Status (verified):** Barron authored the 41-page OLC memorandum dated July 16, 2010 while serving as Acting Assistant Attorney General heading the Office of Legal Counsel. The Senate confirmed him to the First Circuit in 2014; he now serves as **Chief Judge** of that court. He is a living federal judge.

### Why his current office raises, not lowers, the bar

A common intuition is that public officials are "fair game." Two corrections:

1. **The conduct predates the office.** Writing the memo was Barron's prior executive-branch work, not a judicial act. Under US law his current judicial status does not make him *more* protected, but it makes the *reputational stakes* — and therefore the litigation incentive — higher, and it makes any imputation of bad faith resonate as an attack on the integrity of a sitting jurist. That is a per-se-adjacent reputational category (professional integrity of a lawyer/judge) and the kind of claim a plaintiff's lawyer notices.
2. **US actual-malice shield is real but is not a drafting license.** As a public figure on a public matter, Barron would have to prove actual malice (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard). That protects *accurate reporting and honest opinion*. It does **not** protect a false factual assertion of bad faith, dishonesty, or criminality. The book should never rely on the actual-malice shield as a reason to write loosely; it should write so that the shield never has to be tested.

### What is documented fact about Barron (GREEN territory)

- That he authored / principally drafted the July 16, 2010 OLC memorandum. (Court-released; his authorship is on the public record and acknowledged in the litigation and reporting.)
- What the memo **concluded** — stated as the memo's own conclusions, attributed to the memo: that the 2001 AUMF supplied authority; that 18 U.S.C. § 1119's "public-authority justification" applied; that a *Mathews v. Eldridge* due-process balance posed no bar where capture was infeasible and the threat "continued and imminent." These are the document's holdings; quoting or closely paraphrasing them, attributed to the memo, is reporting a public document.
- His title and role at the time (Acting AAG, head of OLC).
- His subsequent confirmation to the First Circuit (relevant, neutral, public fact).

### What is RISKY about Barron (do not assert)

- That he **knew** the conclusions were wrong, acted in **bad faith**, "rubber-stamped," wrote to **order**, or tailored the law to a predetermined result. (Mental-state/integrity imputation; rule 05; no source establishes it; against a sitting judge this is the highest-risk sentence in the candidate.)
- That the memo was a "**sham**," "**fig leaf**," "**cover**," or that Barron "**authorized a killing**" / "**signed a death warrant**" / "**played judge and jury**" (the last is a real published opinion-piece headline — it may be *reported as someone's characterization with attribution*, but the book must not adopt it as its own narration).
- That the killing was **unlawful** / **illegal** / **murder** / an "**assassination**" stated as fact. (No court so held; "assassination" and "murder" are legal-conclusion words the record does not support.)
- Any verb that makes Barron the actor of the killing rather than the author of an opinion. He wrote the instrument; the strike decision and execution sat with the President, NSC principals, and the operational chain.

## 3. Safe framing for "a US citizen killed without trial"

The fact is unassailable and powerful **stated plainly and narrowly**: Anwar al-Aulaqi was a US citizen; he was killed by a US drone strike in Yemen on September 30, 2011; he was never charged with a crime, never tried, and no court adjudicated the lawfulness of his killing. Every clause there is documented and carries no imputation requiring proof of motive.

The imputation to avoid is the *unstated legal conclusion* that "without trial" smuggles in — i.e., that the killing was therefore unlawful. The book's own diagnostic does not need that conclusion and must not assert it. The honest and safe move is the one Loki already identified: the book's claim is **not** "Obama murdered a citizen"; it is "a discretionary executive decision to kill a citizen was converted, by an internal legal opinion, into a closed legal conclusion that no court ever tested." That framing is true regardless of whether the strike was ultimately lawful, and it carries no defamatory imputation against any living person — it describes a *structure*, attributing the opinion to its author and the decision to the executive, both accurately.

**Abdulrahman al-Aulaqi** (16-year-old US citizen, killed in a separate October 2011 strike, never alleged to be a combatant) and **Samir Khan**: handle as **C-grade, hedged**. State only what is documented (citizen; minor; killed in a later strike; no public combatant allegation; no adversarially-tested official accounting). Do **not** quote the "wrong place at the wrong time" remark as an admission unless Stephen locks the speaker, venue, and date and it is framed as that official's statement, not as the book's finding of cause. Recommended: confine the chapter to the memo + the first strike, with at most one hedged sentence on the second strike (see open question 3 in Loki's packet).

## 4. Naming Barron vs. "the OLC" / "the memo's authors" — RECOMMENDATION

**Recommendation: NAME Barron, narrowly and factually, as the memo's author — do not anonymize to "the OLC."**

Reasoning (this is a judgment call; I give the mechanism so Wayne and xiaolai can verify it):

1. **Anonymizing does not reduce legal risk; it can increase it.** "The memo's authors" or "the OLC" is *less* precise, and imprecision is where implication-burden (rule 07) breeds. Naming the documented author of a court-released public document, and confining the claim to "authored the memorandum that concluded X," is the *lowest-risk* formulation available, because it is a bare, verifiable, non-evaluative fact. The risk in this case is never "we named him"; it is "we imputed something to him." You can impute bad faith to "the OLC" just as defamatorily as to Barron — and vaguer phrasing makes the hedge harder to police.
2. **The book's whole diagnostic is "find the invoker / name the author of the instrument."** Hiding behind "the OLC" would make the book commit the exact system/object-alibi move it is diagnosing — letting the institution be the named cause so no human author appears. Naming Barron (as author, not as wrongdoer) is the book practicing what it preaches. This is also why the parallel must hold with **Bybee and Yoo**, whom the book already names as authors of the torture memos (per the portfolio sweep, ch-02 names them as document authors and that is cleared). Treating the Republican-administration authors as named and the Democratic-administration author as anonymous would itself be a partisan-asymmetry surface Laura would (rightly) flag.
3. **The line that makes naming safe is the verb.** "Barron **authored** the memorandum that concluded X" is safe. "Barron **authorized the killing**" is not. The clearance below is built entirely around keeping Barron the subject of *authored / drafted / concluded* and never the subject of *killed / authorized / decided to kill*.

**Condition on naming:** every sentence that names Barron must (a) use an authorship/conclusion verb only, (b) attribute the substantive claims to the memo (not to his mental state), and (c) appear in the same passage as the procedural fact that he is now a sitting federal judge **only if** that fact is presented neutrally (it is relevant context, not an insinuation that a wrongdoer ascended the bench). If Wayne cannot hold all three, drop to "the OLC memorandum" for that specific sentence — but the default is to name.

## 5. Fair-report and opinion protections — and their limits

Two protections are genuinely available; neither is a blank check.

**(a) Fair-report privilege.** Reporting the contents of an official, public proceeding or document — here, the court-released OLC memo and the three court opinions — is protected if the report is **fair and accurate**. This is strong cover for: quoting the memo's conclusions; stating the dismissals and their grounds; stating that the merits were never reached. *Limit:* the privilege protects the *report*, not editorial gloss layered on top. "The memo concluded the killing was lawful conduct of war" (accurate report of the document) is privileged. "The memo was a transparent attempt to legalize an assassination" (editorial conclusion) is not — that is the book's voice asserting a defamatory characterization, and the privilege does not reach it.

**(b) Opinion / fair-comment.** Honest opinion on a matter of public concern, disclosing the facts it rests on, is protected — opinion is not provably false. *Limit (critical):* an opinion that **implies undisclosed defamatory facts** is NOT protected. "In my view this is responsibility laundering" is protected **only if** the book has laid out the documented facts (control, benefit, knowledge, preventability, record-control) and the reader can see the opinion is a characterization of those disclosed facts. The same sentence is unprotected if it implies the book knows of bad faith it has not shown. **Operational rule:** the word "laundering" applied to this case is defensible as a *category characterization built on disclosed facts* (run taxonomy first, per the defamation-wording skill's recipe 5) — never as a free-standing accusation. It is also subject to the chapter's once-per-chapter cap on the term (rule 04); ch-8 already spends that cap in beat 8, so the al-Aulaqi passage should make the structural point *without* re-using the word.

**(c) Substantial truth.** The ultimate defense. It is available for the documented facts (authorship, conclusions, dispositions, citizenship, death). It is **not** available for the contested legal conclusion (lawful/unlawful) because there is no adjudicated truth to be substantially true to. Hence: stay on the documented facts; do not assert the contested conclusion.
