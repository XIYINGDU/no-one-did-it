## Candidate 1 — ch-1: Laws of Manu (Manusmṛti) 8.317, Bühler

**VERIFIED.**

### Exact text as it should appear in the book

> The killer of a learned Brāhmaṇa throws his guilt on him who eats his food, an adulterous wife on her (negligent) husband, a (sinning) pupil or sacrificer on (their negligent) teacher (or priest), a thief on the king (who pardons him).

(Bühler's printed parenthetical insertions are part of his translation and must be retained verbatim. Diacritic on "Brāhmaṇa": Bühler's SBE text uses the diacritical form; web renderings flatten it to "Brahmana." Match the original SBE typography — "Brāhmaṇa" — or, if a plain-ASCII house style applies, render "Brahmana" and note the diacritic-flattening as a rule-06 typographic normalization. Wayne's design call.)

### Cleanest epigraph span (advice)

The full verse is the strongest pick — it states the guilt-transfer mechanism four ways and lands on "a thief on the king (who pardons him)," which is the manuscript's exact theme. If a shorter cut is wanted for display, the cleanest single-clause lift is:

> ...a thief [throws his guilt] on the king (who pardons him).

— but this requires a bracketed insertion of the elided verb and loses the parallelism. Recommend the full verse; it cuts cleanly at the period, no ellipsis needed. Do NOT pair it with v.316 ("the king...takes upon himself the guilt of the thief") inside one epigraph unless re-verified as a two-verse span.

### Locked citation

The Laws of Manu 8.317, trans. Georg Bühler, *Sacred Books of the East*, vol. 25 (1886).

### Verification basis & nuance
- Byte-exact against the Bühler SBE text, corroborated across three independent renderings of that same translation (arthistoryproject SBE reprint, wisdomlib, hinduismfacts) — all identical word-for-word including the parentheticals.
- Locus confirmed: Chapter VIII (Bühler's "Eighth Lecture"), verse 317; sits between v.316 (king takes the thief's guilt if he does not punish) and v.318 (the punished go to heaven). The 8.317 numbering is Bühler's own verse numbering — correct.
- Public domain: Bühler (d. 1898); SBE vol. 25 published 1886. No permissions required.

## Candidate 2 — ch-2: Confucius, Analects 13.3, Legge

**VERIFIED.**

### Exact text as it should appear in the book

> If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success.

### Locked citation

Confucius, *Analects* 13.3, trans. James Legge.

### Verification basis & nuance
- Byte-exact against Legge, confirmed via Wikisource's *The Chinese Classics* Vol. 1 (the authoritative Legge primary text) and a second independent rendering — identical.
- Locus confirmed: Book XIII (Tsze-lû / Zi Lu), chapter 3. The "rectification of names" passage. Legge sets it as a numbered sub-section within 13.3; the two-sentence span above is a clean cut at the period.
- The fuller passage continues with the cascade ("affairs cannot be carried on to success... proprieties and music will not flourish... punishments will not be properly awarded..."). The two-sentence cut is the cleanest epigraph; it stops at a period, no ellipsis needed. Do not extend without re-verifying the continuation byte-exact.
- Public domain: Legge (d. 1897); *The Chinese Classics* vol. 1 published 1861/1893. No permissions required.

## Candidate 3 — ch-6: Aesop, "The Wolf and the Lamb," Townsend

**VERIFIED.**

### Exact text as it should appear in the book

> The tyrant will always find a pretext for his tyranny.

### Locked citation

Aesop, "The Wolf and the Lamb," trans. George Fyler Townsend.

### Verification basis & nuance
- Byte-exact against the Townsend primary text (Internet Classics Archive hosts the Townsend translation), corroborated by a second Townsend-edition search confirming "pretext... tyranny."
- This IS the stated moral of the fable in Townsend's edition — the closing sentence after the Wolf eats the Lamb. Confirmed as the fable's moral, not an editorial gloss.
- **Caution flagged:** other public-domain Aesop translations render the moral differently — americanliterature.com's text reads "The tyrant can always find an excuse for his tyranny" (excuse, not pretext; can, not will). That is a DIFFERENT translation, not Townsend. The candidate specifies Townsend, and "will always find a pretext for his tyranny" is correct for Townsend. Do not let a copyeditor "correct" it to the other wording — the attribution and wording must stay matched.
- Public domain: Townsend (d. 1900); translation published 1867. No permissions required.

## Candidate 4 — ch-8: Thucydides, Melian Dialogue 5.89, Crawley

**VERIFIED.**

### Exact text as it should appear in the book

> the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.

(The popular paraphrase. Crawley's exact wording is byte-identical to it for this clause — confirmed below. If a fuller, more honest span is wanted, the cleanest larger lift is:)

> ...right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.

— recommended, because the standalone short clause is the over-quoted fragment; the fuller clause carries Crawley's actual "right...is only in question between equals in power" framing, which is more defensible and more on-theme. Leading ellipsis required if cut mid-sentence (the clause opens with "since you know as well as we do that right...").

### Locked citation

Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War* 5.89, trans. Richard Crawley.

### Verification basis & nuance
- Byte-exact against the Crawley primary text (FAS / man.fas.org Crawley edition): "...since you know as well as we do that right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The popular short form is, here, byte-exact to Crawley — no paraphrase drift. (Jowett and Hobbes render it differently; the candidate specifies Crawley and Crawley matches.)
- Locus: 5.89 is the canonical chapter number for this Athenian speech (Perseus indexes it at book 5, chapter 89; confirmed via the Perseus chapter-89 URL structure). The FAS edition sub-divides the dialogue as "Fifth Book, Chapter XVII," which is the same passage under a different sub-heading scheme — the standard scholarly cite is 5.89.
- Public domain: Crawley (d. 1924); translation published 1874. No permissions required.

## Candidate 5 — ch-10: Sun Tzu, The Art of War I.18, Giles (1910)

**VERIFIED.**

### Exact text as it should appear in the book

> All warfare is based on deception.

### Locked citation

Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* I.18, trans. Lionel Giles (1910).

### Verification basis & nuance
- Byte-exact against the Giles primary text (Project Gutenberg ebook #132, the Giles 1910 translation) — verse 18 of Chapter I ("Laying Plans") reads exactly "All warfare is based on deception." Corroborated by a second Giles-edition rendering.
- Locus confirmed: Chapter I, verse 18. Giles numbers the verses within each chapter; I.18 is correct. (Giles's verse 18 is immediately followed by his bracketed Henderson/Wellington scholarly note — exclude the note; the epigraph is the sentence only.)
- Public domain: Giles (d. 1958) — translation published 1910; the 1910 text is public domain in the US (pre-1929). No permissions required for the 1910 translation text.
