## Chapter 1 — The Altar Moves

**Exact diagnostic:** the ancient substitution rite that *named itself* — a community openly transfers its accumulated iniquity/pollution onto a designated bearer who carries it away. The modern case keeps the whole machine and deletes only the naming. Constraint: NON-BIBLICAL, ideally NON-WESTERN; and avoid the Greek/Roman/Babylonian rites already worked in the chapter body.

### Candidate 1A (RECOMMENDED)
> "The slayer of a learned Brahmana throws (the guilt of) his crime on him who eats his food, an adulterous wife on her (negligent) husband, a (sinning) pupil or sacrificer on (their negligent) teacher (or priest), a thief on the king (who pardons him)."
- **Author / culture / dates:** *The Laws of Manu* (Manusmriti / Mānava-Dharmaśāstra), Indian dharmaśāstra tradition; text compiled c. 2nd c. BCE–2nd c. CE.
- **Source:** Manusmriti 8.317, Georg Bühler translation (*Sacred Books of the East* vol. 25, 1886; public domain; surfaced against sacred-texts.com hin/manu/manu08.htm this pass).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (Bühler is the standard public-domain English; wording located this pass — Stephen to byte-lock 8.317 exactly, including Bühler's bracketed glosses).
- **Why apt:** This is the transfer-of-guilt mechanism *stated as doctrine* — iniquity does not vanish; it is thrown from the doer onto a designated bearer (the food-eater, the negligent superior, the pardoning king). It is the scapegoat's structural logic — guilt as a transferable load — in a tradition entirely distinct from the chapter's Levitical/Greek/Babylonian/Roman anchors, and it never names "scapegoat," which suits a chapter about the rite that lost only its name.

### Candidate 1B (RECOMMENDED ALT — shorter, sharper on the sovereign-bearer)
> "Whether he be punished or pardoned, the thief is freed from the (guilt of) theft; but the king, if he punishes not, takes upon himself the guilt of the thief."
- **Author / culture / dates:** *The Laws of Manu* 8.316, same tradition/edition as 1A.
- **Source:** Manusmriti 8.316, Bühler (1886; public domain).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** Compresses the transfer to a single clause — guilt is a thing that moves to a bearer. Slightly more about the sovereign absorbing guilt than about the discharge-onto-a-substitute; 1A is the cleaner scapegoat-mirror. (1B is also the recommended ch-3 pick below — do not use the same line in two chapters; if 1B is chosen for ch-1, ch-3 takes its own alt.)

**Recommended pick: 1A (Manu 8.317).** Non-biblical, non-Western (Indian dharmaśāstra), public-domain, and exactly on the rite's load-logic — guilt thrown onto a designated bearer — without touching any tradition the chapter already quotes.

## Chapter 2 — The Four Goats

**Exact diagnostic:** installing the four categories (pure scapegoat / partial scapegoat / system-or-object alibi / cost-bearing goat) and making *correct classification* the first move of every later diagnostic. The chapter's whole work is naming kinds rightly so the accounting can run.

### Candidate 2A (RECOMMENDED)
> "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success. ... Therefore a superior man considers it necessary that the names he uses may be spoken appropriately."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Confucius, *Analects* (Lunyu) 13.3, Chinese Confucian tradition; c. 5th c. BCE (compiled later).
- **Source:** *Analects* 13.3, James Legge translation (1861/1893; public domain; standard). (The downstream clause "punishments will not be properly awarded" is in the same passage and is the sharpest line for this book — Stephen to confirm whether to lift the compact opening or carry through to the punishments clause.)
- **Confidence:** HIGH (Legge is the canonical public-domain English; 13.3 is among the most-cited Confucian passages — Stephen to byte-lock the exact Legge wording and decide the cut point).
- **Why apt:** The *zhengming* (rectification of names) doctrine is precisely this chapter's claim: get the name wrong and the accounting fails — "punishments will not fit the crimes." The four goats are an exercise in rectifying the names for who carries blame; misname the goat and responsibility lands in the wrong place. Directly illuminates the chapter's classification move.

### Candidate 2B (ALT — if a tighter line is wanted)
> "When names are not correct ... punishments will not be properly awarded; and when punishments are not properly awarded, the people do not know how to move hand or foot."
- **Author / culture / dates / source:** Confucius, *Analects* 13.3, Legge (same passage as 2A).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** Lands the punishment consequence explicitly — the cleanest possible bridge to a book about blame mis-assigned. Pick 2A for the fuller frame, 2B for the punishment-edged compression.

**Recommended pick: 2A (Confucius, Analects 13.3).** Chinese Confucian, public-domain, and exactly on the chapter's name-the-category-correctly diagnostic. Use 2B for the sharper punishment clause.

## Chapter 3 — Who Could Have Stopped It?

**Exact diagnostic:** preventability — the chance not taken; the point at which the chain still held the option to decide differently; responsibility attaching to whoever could have prevented and did not (the eight-question diagnostic's fifth question made into the chapter's spine).

### Candidate 3A (RECOMMENDED)
> "Whether he be punished or pardoned, the thief is freed from the (guilt of) theft; but the king, if he punishes not, takes upon himself the guilt of the thief."
- **Author / culture / dates:** *The Laws of Manu* 8.316, Indian dharmaśāstra; Bühler translation (1886; public domain).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** This is preventability as doctrine: the actor who *could have acted and did not* takes the guilt onto himself. The chapter's claim — that the preventability question separates the unlucky from the responsible, and that responsibility climbs to whoever held the option — is the exact logic of "the king, if he punishes not, takes upon himself the guilt." (Note: shares the Manu source with ch-1; if 8.316 is used here, ch-1 takes 1A/8.317, which is the recommended ch-1 pick anyway, so no collision. But to vary the *tradition-text* across the set, see 3B.)

### Candidate 3B (RECOMMENDED ALT — varies the text away from a second Manu citation)
> "This possession of a sceptre [daṇḍa] ... when neglected, gives rise to the condition of the law of fishes (mātsyanyāya); for in the absence of a wielder of the sceptre, the strong will swallow the weak."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Kauṭilya, *Arthaśāstra* Book I, ch. 4 (the *daṇḍanīti* / "law of the fishes" passage), Indian arthaśāstra tradition; text c. 2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE.
- **Source:** *Arthashastra* I.4, R. Shamasastry translation (1915; public domain; surfaced against the Shamasastry text and Wikisource Arthashastra/Book I this pass). The descriptive paraphrase above is a reconstruction — **do NOT use as a quote; Stephen must lift the exact Shamasastry sentence(s) verbatim.**
- **Confidence:** MED — source and locus (Shamasastry, Book I ch. 4, the *matsyanyaya* passage) are solid; the exact epigraph sentence must be byte-lifted from the Shamasastry text, not from the paraphrase here.
- **Why apt:** The "law of the fishes" names what happens in the *absence* of the actor who could have restrained harm — the preventability gap rendered as a maxim. Keeps the Indian tradition in the set while citing a different text/genre (arthaśāstra, not dharmaśāstra), avoiding a second Manu line.

**Recommended pick: 3A (Manu 8.316)** for HIGH-confidence and exact preventability logic; it does not collide with ch-1 because ch-1's recommended pick is 8.317. **If the set wants text-variation from Manu, switch to 3B (Kauṭilya matsyanyaya), MED — Stephen lifts the verbatim Shamasastry sentence.**

## Chapter 4 — The Proxy and the Sponsor

**Exact diagnostic:** the principal who acts through a deniable intermediary — the sponsor who supplies the means and controls the territory of the act while engineering the visible actor's identification *out*, so the chain of attribution terminates at the proxy before it reaches the sponsor.

### Candidate 4A (RECOMMENDED)
> "Hide your tracks, conceal your sources, so that your subordinates cannot trace the springs of your action."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Han Feizi (Han Fei), Chinese Legalist tradition; c. 280–233 BCE.
- **Source:** *Han Feizi*, ch. 5 ("The Way of the Ruler" / *Zhu Dao*), Burton Watson translation (*Han Fei Tzu: Basic Writings*, Columbia Univ. Press, 1964). Surfaced against the Watson text this pass.
- **Confidence:** HIGH for wording-as-located, **but FLAG: NOT public domain** — Watson 1964 is in copyright; an epigraph use is short and likely fair use, but Nancy must clear permission/fair-use before `status: ready`, or Stephen substitutes a public-domain translation (e.g., W. K. Liao, 1939, also in/near copyright depending on jurisdiction — verify) of the same *Zhu Dao* passage.
- **Why apt:** This is the sponsor's instruction stated outright — erase the trace from the act back to yourself so that what is done through your agents cannot be traced to its source. It is the mechanism of ch-4 (the unmarked uniform, the deniable proxy, the supplied-but-disavowed means) in one imperative sentence, from a tradition built around rule through concealed agency.

### Candidate 4B (ALT — Persian breadth; public-domain)
> [A Sa'di line on the king who lets his officers oppress while keeping his own hands clean — exact line to be lifted.]
- **Author / culture / dates:** Sa'di of Shiraz, *Gulistan* ("Rose Garden"), Book I ("The Manners of Kings"), Persian tradition; 1258 CE.
- **Source:** *Gulistan*, Edward Rehatsek translation (1888; public domain; full text on archive.org). **Do NOT use without Stephen lifting the exact verbatim apt line; located the right book (I) and theme this pass, not the byte-exact sentence.**
- **Confidence:** MED — translation is public-domain and the "manners of kings" book is the right locus; the specific deniability line is not byte-confirmed this pass.
- **Why apt:** Adds a Persian source to the set and lands the proxy-and-sponsor theme (the ruler who benefits from oppression done by his agents) from a literary-ethical rather than legalist register. Use if the crew prefers a public-domain source over the copyrighted Watson, or wants Persian breadth.

**Recommended pick: 4A (Han Feizi, *Zhu Dao*, Watson)** — sharpest single statement of the sponsor erasing the trace — *conditional on Nancy clearing the Watson copyright* or Stephen substituting a public-domain rendering of the same passage. **Fall back to 4B (Sa'di *Gulistan*, Rehatsek, public-domain)** if the rights/permission path is unwanted; Stephen lifts the verbatim line.

## Chapter 5 — The Guilty Goat

**Exact diagnostic:** the partial/guilty scapegoat — a named actor whose culpability at the execution or local-decision layer is *real*, used as the boundary at which blame is stopped so it cannot climb to the design/cost-cutting/regulatory layers above. The rule: when the named actor is genuinely guilty, do not stop — run the questions on the floor above.

### Candidate 5A (RECOMMENDED)
> "The slayer of a learned Brahmana throws (the guilt of) his crime on him who eats his food ... a (sinning) pupil or sacrificer on (their negligent) teacher (or priest), a thief on the king (who pardons him)."
- **Author / culture / dates:** *The Laws of Manu* 8.317, Bühler translation (1886; public domain).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** The verse names exactly the move the chapter installs: real guilt *at one level* does not terminate the accounting — it climbs to the negligent superior (the teacher who failed to restrain the pupil, the king who pardoned the thief). That is the partial-scapegoat rule in doctrine: the chain does not stop at the guilty hand; it reaches the negligent floor above. (Shares the Manu source with ch-1's recommended 1A. If both ch-1 and ch-5 land on 8.317, that repeats the *same verse* across two chapters — not allowed. Resolve by: ch-1 takes 8.317 [substitution/transfer], ch-5 takes 5B below [climbing the chain]; OR ch-1 takes 8.317 and ch-5 takes Manu 8.318/8.335 on the king himself being punishable — see note.)

### Candidate 5B (RECOMMENDED — resolves the Manu collision; Persian breadth)
> [A Sa'di *Gulistan* / *Bustan* line on the lowly bearer punished while the powerful escape — exact line to be lifted.]
- **Author / culture / dates:** Sa'di of Shiraz, *Gulistan* Book I ("Manners of Kings") or *Bustan* Book I (on justice/government), Persian tradition; 1257–58 CE.
- **Source:** Rehatsek *Gulistan* (1888) or Clarke/Wickens *Bustan* — public-domain *Gulistan*-Rehatsek preferred. **Stephen to lift the exact verbatim apt line; located the right books/theme this pass, not the byte-exact sentence.**
- **Confidence:** MED — public-domain translation and correct locus identified; exact line not byte-confirmed this pass.
- **Why apt:** Adds Persian to the set and lands the guilty-goat asymmetry (the small bearer punished while the chain above is spared) without repeating the ch-1 Manu verse. Recommended as the ch-5 pick precisely to avoid the 8.317 collision and to widen the cultural range.

### Candidate 5C (HIGH-confidence safe fallback — if the Sa'di line cannot be byte-confirmed)
> "Manu has declared that ... [the king/judge who decides unjustly bears a share of the guilt]" — Manu 8.18: "One quarter of (the guilt of) an unjust (decision) falls on him who commits (the crime), one quarter on the (false) witness, one quarter on all the judges, one quarter on the king."
- **Author / culture / dates / source:** Manusmriti 8.18, Bühler (1886; public domain).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** Distributes guilt up the chain by quarters — the doer is only one quarter; the witness, the judges, and the king carry the rest. That is the partial-scapegoat arithmetic exactly: stopping at the doer accounts for one-quarter and launders the other three. It is a *different verse* from ch-1's 8.317, so the two chapters can both cite Manu without repeating a line. Use 5C if 5B (Sa'di) fails Stephen's byte-check.

**Recommended pick: 5B (Sa'di *Gulistan*/*Bustan*, Rehatsek/public-domain)** for cultural breadth and to avoid the ch-1 Manu collision — MED, Stephen lifts the verbatim line. **Safe HIGH-confidence fallback: 5C (Manu 8.18, the quarter-shares-of-guilt verse)**, which is a distinct verse from ch-1 and lands the partial-scapegoat arithmetic cleanly.
