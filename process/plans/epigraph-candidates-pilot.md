## Chapter 1 — The Altar Moves

**Exact diagnostic:** the ancient substitution rite that *named itself* — community discharges its accumulated blame/pollution onto a designated bearer, openly. The modern case keeps the whole machine and deletes only the naming. Seek a scholar/primary text on sacrifice, the scapegoat, or substitution-for-the-community.

Constraint noted: the chapter already quotes Leviticus 16 at length as its own anchor, so the epigraph should NOT be Leviticus (would double the chapter's load-bearing text). Best to bring a *different* tradition's substitution rite to the door.

### Candidate 1A (RECOMMENDED)
> "It is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Caiaphas, as reported in the Gospel of John (Hellenistic-Jewish / early Christian text; narrated event c. 30 CE, Gospel composed late 1st c. CE).
- **Source:** John 11:50, King James Version (1611; public domain).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (byte-exact KJV; one of the most-printed sentences in English).
- **Why apt:** This is substitution stated *openly and as policy* — one bearer killed so the community is spared — exactly the "rite that names itself" the chapter argues the modern case has learned to hide. It is the substitution logic in a single sentence, from a tradition distinct from the chapter's Levitical anchor.
- **Flag:** scripture-adjacent, and the chapter's surrounding material is already biblical. Offered as the aptest single line; if the crew wants to avoid a second biblical source, use 1B.

### Candidate 1B (RECOMMENDED ALT — non-biblical)
> The Roman *devotio*: a general "designating the enemies as vicarious substitutes for the Romans while consecrating their own life" for the safety of the state.
- **Author / culture / dates:** the rite as recorded by Livy (Roman; Titus Livius, 59 BCE–17 CE), *Ab Urbe Condita* Book 8 (devotio of Publius Decius Mus, 340 BCE).
- **Source:** Livy, *History of Rome*, Book 8, ch. 9–10 (devotio formula); standard public-domain English: Rev. Canon Roberts translation (1905, Everyman) or Loeb (Foster).
- **Confidence:** MED — the *ritual and its location in Livy 8.9–10 are solid; the exact English epigraph line must be lifted verbatim from a named translation by Stephen.* Do NOT use the paraphrase above as the epigraph; it is a description, not a quote.
- **Why apt:** A culturally distinct (Roman, non-Hebrew, non-Greek) substitution rite that *names itself* — the community openly offers one life for the whole — broadening the set away from the chapter's Levitical/Greek anchors.

### Candidate 1C
> "Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel ... putting them upon the head of the goat ... and the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Hebrew Bible (Priestly source), Leviticus 16:21–22.
- **Source:** KJV (public domain) or JPS 1985 (already cited in-chapter).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** It IS the named rite. **Flag: do not use** — duplicates the chapter's own load-bearing anchor text. Listed only to document that the obvious choice was considered and set aside per the no-duplication judgment.

**Recommended pick: 1A (Caiaphas, John 11:50)** — sharpest one-sentence statement of open substitution-for-the-community, in narrative voice, from a tradition adjacent-but-distinct from the chapter's Levitical core. **If a second biblical source is unwanted, switch to 1B (Livy/devotio)** for cultural breadth, with Stephen lifting the verbatim line from a named translation.

## Chapter 9 — The Model Did It

**Exact diagnostic:** blame displaced onto the tool / instrument / system via grammar — "the model decided," the thing as the verb's subject — so the human decision behind the instrument disappears. Seek a scholar on tools, instruments, technique, or displaced agency.

### Candidate 9A (RECOMMENDED)
> "the servant is himself an instrument, which takes precedence of all other instruments."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Aristotle (Greek; 384–322 BCE), *Politics* Book I, ch. 4.
- **Source:** *Politics*, 1253b–1254a, Benjamin Jowett translation (1885; public domain; Wikisource verified).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (wording confirmed against Jowett text this pass).
- **Why apt:** Aristotle's instrument/agency analysis is the canonical primary text on what it means for a thing to "act." His image — the tool that would work "obeying or anticipating the will of others, like the statues of Daedalus" — is the 4th-century-BCE prefiguration of "the model decided": the instrument that appears to act on its own and so absorbs the agency of the human who wields it. Directly illuminates the chapter's grammar-of-displaced-agency move.

### Candidate 9B (RECOMMENDED ALT — sharper image)
> "if ... the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Aristotle, *Politics* Book I, ch. 4 (same passage as 9A).
- **Source:** *Politics* 1253b, Jowett translation (public domain; Wikisource verified).
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Why apt:** The self-acting instrument that needs "no hand to guide" is a precise ancient mirror of the autonomous-model alibi. More vivid than 9A; slightly longer. Pick 9A for compression, 9B for image.

### Candidate 9C
> "It would be impossible to train today's leading AI models without using copyrighted materials."
- **Author / culture / dates:** OpenAI (modern corporate; 2024).
- **Source:** OpenAI written evidence to the UK House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee, 8 Jan 2024 (already cited in-chapter).
- **Confidence:** HIGH but **flag: do not use as epigraph.** It is a subject of the chapter, not a scholarly frame on it; using it as epigraph would editorialize. Documented only to show the modern primary source was considered and set aside (it belongs in the body, where it already sits).

**Recommended pick: 9A (Aristotle, *Politics* I.4, "the servant is himself an instrument")** — the foundational scholarly text on instrument-as-agent, culturally Greek, public-domain, and exactly on the chapter's displaced-agency grammar. Use 9B if the crew wants the more vivid self-weaving-shuttle image.

## Chapter 12 — Keep the Record

**Exact diagnostic:** the record as battlefield — survival vs. suppression/destruction; memory, archives, testimony, forgetting; and the corollary that a surviving record is not automatically a true one. Seek a scholar on memory, the record, history, or forgetting.

### Candidate 12A (RECOMMENDED)
> "this invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Plato (Greek; c. 428–348 BCE), the Egyptian king Thamus on writing, in the Theuth myth.
- **Source:** *Phaedrus* 275a, Harold N. Fowler translation (Loeb; Perseus Digital Library; public domain; wording confirmed this pass).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (confirmed against Perseus/Fowler).
- **Why apt:** The foundational Western meditation on the record and memory — and pointedly double-edged: the written record is both the externalization that *preserves* and the crutch that *displaces* living memory. The chapter's whole argument turns on the record as the contested medium of accountability; Thamus names the record's ambivalence at its origin.

### Candidate 12B (RECOMMENDED ALT — cultural breadth, distinct angle)
> "It would be better to be without the Book of History than to give entire credit to it. In the 'Completion of the War,' I select two or three passages only, which I believe."
- **Author / culture / dates:** Mencius / Mengzi (Chinese Confucian; c. 372–289 BCE).
- **Source:** *Mencius* (*Mengzi*), Book VII Part B (Jin Xin II), section 3, James Legge translation (1895; public domain; wording confirmed against ctext.org this pass).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (confirmed against ctext.org/Legge).
- **Why apt:** Hits the chapter's *sharpest and least obvious* move — that a surviving record is not automatically a true record; the record must be read critically, not credited wholesale. Brings the non-Western (Chinese) tradition into the set and pairs naturally with the chapter's "ten record fights" asymmetry table, which is itself an exercise in not crediting any single record wholesale.

### Candidate 12C
> "they make a desert and call it peace." (*solitudinem faciunt pacem appellant*)
- **Author / culture / dates:** Tacitus (Roman; c. 56–120 CE), placed in the mouth of the British chieftain Calgacus.
- **Source:** Tacitus, *Agricola* ch. 30; standard public-domain English (Church & Brodribb, 1877).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (one of the best-known lines in Latin historiography; Stephen to confirm chosen translator's exact English).
- **Why apt:** About the *euphemism that buries the act* — naming destruction "peace." Tangential to the record-survival core; better fit for a chapter on naming/euphemism than on records. Offered as a lower-priority breadth option; recommend 12A or 12B over it.

**Recommended pick: 12B (Mencius, *Mengzi* 7B.3)** — it lands the chapter's most distinctive claim (a surviving record is not a true record; read critically), brings cultural breadth, and is public-domain and byte-confirmed. **Use 12A (Plato/Thamus) if the crew prefers the canonical memory-vs-record frame**; both are HIGH-confidence and either would serve.

## Set-level calibration notes (for the all-13 rollout)

- **Cultural range achieved across recommended picks:** ch-1 early-Christian/Jewish (with a Roman alt), ch-9 Greek (Aristotle), ch-12 Chinese (Mencius, with a Greek alt). Across the recommended set we already span Hebrew/Christian, Roman, Greek, and Chinese traditions. The full 13-chapter set still needs deliberate Arabic/Islamic (Ibn Khaldun is the obvious anchor — but his aptest lines are about *injustice ruining civilization* and *the vanquished imitating the victor*, neither of which fit these three chapters' diagnostics; reserve him for a chapter on decline or imitation), Indian (Kautilya's *Arthashastra* on *danda*/punishment fits a chapter on enforcement, not these three), and African / Latin American traditions, which were not apt to these three diagnostics and should be sought where they genuinely fit rather than forced.
- **Register:** all recommended picks are short, declarative, and survive being read aloud (rule-12 V10) — good calibration target for the rest.
- **Discipline lesson:** the aptest line is sometimes one the chapter already quotes (Leviticus for ch-1; the OpenAI line for ch-9). Those are deliberately excluded as epigraphs to avoid doubling load-bearing text — a rule worth carrying into the full rollout.
